I once saved about $180 by routing through Frankfurt instead of flying direct, then spent that exact saving plus a little extra on a last-minute hotel because my 55-minute connection evaporated the moment my first flight pushed back from the gate. The bargain was real. So was the missed flight.

That trade is the whole game with cheap multi-stop tickets. The headline price is lower because the airline is handing you a tighter, more fragile itinerary, and quietly betting you don't know the difference between a connection that works and one that only works on paper. Most of the time you win that bet; the few times you don't, you lose. These are the six mistakes I see budget travelers make again and again, and the simple buffer that defuses each one.

1. Treating the minimum connection time as a safe connection time

Every airport publishes a minimum connection time, and booking sites will happily sell you an itinerary that meets it by a single minute. The mistake is reading that number as "comfortable" when it really means "the legal floor, on a perfect day, with nothing going wrong."

Why it costs you: the minimum assumes your inbound lands on time, your gate is close, and there's no passport queue. Strip away any one and you're sprinting. Miss the flight and you're rebooked on the next available seat, which on a budget carrier might be the next day.

My buffer rule

For a domestic-to-domestic connection I want 60 minutes minimum. For anything with an international terminal change, customs, or two different airlines, I won't book under 2 hours. It feels like dead time until the one trip it saves.

2. Forgetting that a self-transfer means nobody is holding the next plane

Here's the distinction that catches people out. On a single ticket, if your first flight is late, the airline has to get you onto the next one. On a self-transfer - two separate tickets stitched together to save money - each flight is a stranger to the other. The second airline doesn't know you're coming and owes you nothing if you don't show.

Why it costs you: a delayed leg on a through-ticket is the airline's problem. The same delay on a self-transfer is entirely yours, including a brand-new second ticket at the airport wall price. The saving from splitting the tickets vanishes instantly.

The fix is to know which kind you've bought before you pay. If the booking shows two separate confirmation numbers, treat the layover as a hard deadline and pad it generously. Some aggregators bundle a "missed connection" guarantee on self-transfers; read what it actually refunds first. The warning flags in these signs you're about to overpay for a flight apply just as well to spotting a connection that's quietly risky.

3. Ignoring whether your bag has to be re-checked

People obsess over their own route through the terminal and forget the suitcase following a separate path. On many itineraries your bag is checked straight through. On others - almost always self-transfers - you have to collect it, clear customs with it, and drop it again.

Why it costs you: re-checking a bag can eat 40 minutes you didn't budget. You exit to arrivals, wait at the carousel, queue at a desk that may not be open yet, then go back through security. A connection that looked generous gets swallowed whole.

Ask before you fly

If you can't confirm your bag is tagged to the final destination, assume it isn't. Pack carry-on only when the layover is tight, or add an extra hour for the bag dance.

4. Booking the last connection of the day with no plan B

The cheapest fares cluster around awkward times, and the last departure of the evening is often cheapest of all. It's also the most dangerous slot to connect through, because there's no next flight to roll onto if you miss it.

Why it costs you: miss a mid-afternoon connection and there might be three more options before midnight. Miss the 9:40pm and your next chance is tomorrow morning, which means an unplanned night, an airport hotel, and possibly a knock-on miss of whatever you'd booked at the other end.

The fix is to look at the full day's schedule, not just your itinerary. If your connecting flight is the last of the day, either widen the layover dramatically or pick an earlier inbound that still leaves later options behind you.

5. Cutting it fine at an airport you've never set foot in

A tight connection at home is one thing - you know which way to walk and how long the line runs. The same connection somewhere unfamiliar, with signage in a language you don't read, is a different animal.

Why it costs you: unfamiliar mega-hubs can mean a train between terminals, a second security screening, and a long walk to a far gate. Budget 15 minutes for the parts you can't see in advance and a 50-minute connection is already gone.

The fix takes five minutes the night before: pull up the airport map and find the realistic terminal-to-terminal time. If the layover is long, flip the problem on its head and treat the wait as a feature - there's real value in making a city tourist pass pay for itself on a deliberate stopover instead of pacing a departure lounge.

6. Assuming the airline owes you a hotel when the delay is on you

This is the misunderstanding that turns a bad day into a costly one. Travelers assume that if a connection breaks, someone hands them a meal voucher and a room. Whether that happens depends entirely on the cause and the ticket type.

Why it costs you: miss a single-ticket connection because of the airline's delay and you're usually rebooked, sometimes housed. But if the delay was weather, or the flights were on separate tickets, you may be on the hook for everything - new fare, hotel, meals. That's hundreds of dollars against a fare you saved tens on.

The fix is to know your protection before you leave. A through-ticket on one airline carries duty-of-care obligations a self-transfer doesn't, so when the saving from a risky routing is small, the safer single ticket is often genuinely cheaper once you price in the downside. A missed connection with kids in tow is especially draining, which is part of why the parents in this family that crossed Europe on points chose forgiving, well-spaced connections over the rock-bottom fares.

How much buffer do you actually need?

No magic number exists, but the logic holds: the more that can go wrong, the more cushion you want. Treat these as floors, not targets.

Is a 1-hour layover ever enough?

For a same-airline, same-terminal connection with only a carry-on, an hour is usually fine and the airline protects you if it's tight. For anything involving customs, a terminal change, or a checked bag, an hour is a gamble I wouldn't take.

What happens if I miss a connection on a self-transfer ticket?

You buy a new ticket for the missed leg at the going price and cover any hotel or meals yourself. The second airline has no record of your first flight, so there's no automatic rebooking - which is why self-transfers need the biggest buffers.

How do I tell if my bag is checked through to the end?

Ask at the first check-in desk and read the destination on the bag tag. If it shows your final city, you're set. If it shows only the connecting airport, plan to collect and re-check it, and add roughly 40 minutes.

None of this means tight connections are a trap to avoid forever - I still book them when the saving is real and the airport is one I trust. The shift is small but it changes everything: stop reading the layover as free time, and start reading it as the thinness of your safety margin. Pad the connections that can hurt you; relax on the ones that can't.
