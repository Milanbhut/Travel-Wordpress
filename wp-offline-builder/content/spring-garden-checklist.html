Last March I stood in my plot at 7 a.m. with a mug of tea and absolutely no plan. The beds were still compacted from winter, I had seed packets scattered across three different drawers, and the frost wasn't done with us yet. I did what most of us do: started in the wrong place and spent the next fortnight fixing it.

Spring is forgiving, but it rewards the people who show up in order. Get the fundamentals done first and everything after that - the planting, the watering, the harvesting - gets easier. Skip them and you're playing catch-up until August.

Below is the checklist I actually use, built around twelve seasons of trial and error on a small north-facing plot. Work through it roughly in sequence and you'll have a solid, productive season ahead.

1. Clear Winter's Leftovers

Before anything else, walk the whole garden with a trug and be ruthless. Pull out dead annuals, cut back blackened stems, and collect any leaves that have turned slimy. This isn't just aesthetic - damp debris is where fungal problems overwinter and wait for warmer days to restart.

Check under hedges and along walls too. Slugs and vine weevil larvae like to spend winter exactly there, so disturbing those spots now reduces the population before tender seedlings appear. I don't bother with any chemicals at this stage - a torch and a bucket work fine.

Timing tip

Don't rush this step just because the sun came out. If your last frost date is still four or more weeks away, cleared soil will crust and compact before you can plant anyway. Clear what's dead; leave any perennial crowns intact until you can see green growth.

2. Assess and Amend Your Soil

Spring soil often looks fine on the surface and feels like old modelling clay two inches down. Compaction from winter rain is normal. Resist the urge to immediately rotavate - on clay soils especially, working wet soil destroys structure and creates hard pans.

The simpler test: grab a handful. If it forms a sticky ball and smears, wait another week. If it crumbles apart, you're good to go. Fork beds lightly, break up large clods, and work in a 3-5 cm layer of compost or well-rotted manure. That one action does more for your yields than almost anything else you'll do all year.

If you've never tested your soil pH, spring is the right moment. Most vegetables prefer somewhere between 6.0 and 7.0. Testing kits are cheap and the result saves you months of wondering why your brassicas are stunted. Lime raises pH if needed; adding sulfur or acidic compost brings it down. Always follow pack rates - more is not better.

3. Prune and Tidy Woody Plants

For most deciduous shrubs and roses, late winter into early spring (before leaves open) is the window you want. The structure is visible, the plant is still dormant, and you're not interrupting any growth surge. Cut out any dead, damaged, or crossing wood first - that's 80% of the job right there.

Don't prune spring-flowering shrubs like forsythia or flowering currant just yet. Those bloom on last year's wood, and cutting them now means cutting off this season's flowers. Finish enjoying them, then prune immediately after flowering.

For fruit trees, early spring pruning is also the right time if you didn't get to it in winter. If you're establishing a new tree, our guide on how to plant a fruit tree correctly covers the first-year pruning cuts in detail - getting those early shapes right pays off for decades.

4. Start Seeds at the Right Time (Not the Earliest Time)

There's a particular type of gardener - I was one for a few seasons - who starts tomatoes in late January, ends up with leggy, root-bound plants by April, and then wonders why they perform worse than the ones started in March. Rushing seeds is genuinely counterproductive.

Work backwards from your last expected frost date for your zone. Warm-season crops like tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers need 6-8 weeks of indoor growing before being hardened off and planted out. Cool-season crops - lettuce, spinach, brassicas - can go out much sooner, even with light frosts, so you can start those directly outside once the soil temperature reaches around 7°C (45°F).

Sow in good seed compost, not garden soil. Label everything, including the date. I lost an entire tray of unmarked seedlings once and still don't know if they were basil or parsley. They were almost certainly parsley. Probably.

What to direct sow now

Peas, spinach, radishes, and broad beans are all happy with cool soil and light frosts. Sow them directly into prepared ground in early spring. Save the warm-season crops for indoor starts until frost risk has passed.

5. Feed Perennials and Overwintered Plants

Perennials emerging from dormancy are hungry. A balanced, slow-release granular fertiliser worked into the soil around the crown in early spring sets them up for the whole growing season. I use a general-purpose granular feed and follow the label rate - there's no benefit to doubling the dose, and you'll risk burning tender new roots.

Overwintered plants in containers deserve particular attention. Pot compost loses nutrients faster than garden soil, especially after months of winter rain leaching through. Repot if roots are circling the base, or top-dress with fresh compost and begin liquid feeding as growth picks up.

Garlic and autumn-planted onion sets - which you may have got in the ground following a solid autumn planting plan - will appreciate a nitrogen-rich feed now to push leafy growth before they bulk up.

6. Get on Top of Weeds Before They Set Seed

The old saying about one year's seeding being seven years' weeding isn't far off. Early spring annual weeds - chickweed, hairy bittercress - go from germination to seed in a matter of weeks when the weather warms. Hoe on a dry day when the soil surface is loose, and the seedlings desiccate before they can establish.

For perennial weeds like bindweed or couch grass, physical removal of the root system is slow but effective. If you use a herbicide, choose a product appropriate for the weed and the site, read the label thoroughly, and keep it away from areas where edible crops are established. There are no shortcuts here that don't carry trade-offs.

A 5-7 cm mulch layer after weeding is the most efficient weed-suppression tool you have. It keeps existing seeds from germinating, conserves moisture, and improves soil structure. Worth the bags of bark or the barrow-loads of compost every time.

7. Plan What This Season Is Teaching You

Spring checklist tasks are finite. But the best thing you can do in this season - and the one most gardeners skip - is to write down what you're doing and why.

Note where last year's brassicas were and where you're moving them this year. Note which varieties you're trying. Mark the spots that stay waterlogged. If you did a good job of preparing your garden last autumn and winter, you'll already have notes to refer back to. That continuity is what separates gardeners who improve every year from those who start over each spring.

A cheap notebook, a pencil tied to a nail in the shed, and five minutes at the end of each session. That's the whole system. It genuinely works.

When exactly should I start my spring garden tasks?

It depends on your hardiness zone and local last-frost date rather than the calendar month. In general, start clearing and soil prep as soon as the ground thaws and isn't waterlogged. Cool-season sowings can begin 6-8 weeks before your last frost; warm-season crops go out after it. Your local cooperative extension service can give you zone-specific timing.

Do I need to add compost every spring even if I did it last year?

Yes, and it's worth repeating every season. Soil biology breaks organic matter down continuously, and a growing season removes significant nutrients through crops and plant growth. An annual top-dressing of compost keeps biology active and nutrients available without over-applying synthetic fertilisers.

Is it safe to use slug pellets near vegetable beds?

Ferric phosphate-based slug pellets are generally considered lower-risk around edibles and wildlife than older metaldehyde formulations, but always read and follow the product label for rates and safety precautions. Keep any products away from pets - some formulations are harmful to dogs. Physical barriers like copper tape or grit, and encouraging natural predators like ground beetles, are a good first line of defence.

Spring doesn't wait for you to feel ready, but it's also more patient than most gardeners give it credit for. Work through these steps in roughly this order, get comfortable with the pace your soil and climate set, and resist the urge to plant everything at once. The seeds you sow carefully in April usually outperform the ones crammed in with optimism in February. One job done properly beats three done hurriedly - and that's as true in the garden as anywhere.
