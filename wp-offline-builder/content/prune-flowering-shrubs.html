Last spring I watched a neighbor cut back her lilac hard in April, certain she was being diligent. It was a thorough job - clean angles, all the deadwood gone. Then May came and went without a single bloom. The buds she'd removed had been sitting there all winter, quietly waiting. One well-meant hour with the loppers, and a year of flowering was gone.

Timing is almost everything with flowering shrubs. The actual pruning technique matters, but if the calendar is wrong, even perfect cuts will set you back. The rule that makes sense of all of it is simple: find out whether a shrub blooms on old wood or new wood, and that tells you exactly when to touch it.

I've been working with shrubs for long enough to know that most pruning mistakes aren't about skill - they're about impatience. The good news is that once you understand the rhythm of a plant's year, you stop second-guessing and the whole job becomes almost intuitive.

Old Wood or New Wood: The One Question That Changes Everything

Flowering shrubs fall into two broad camps. Old-wood bloomers set their flower buds the previous summer, carry them through autumn and winter, and open them the following spring. Lilacs, forsythia, azaleas, rhododendrons, and most viburnums all work this way. If you prune them in autumn or early spring, you're cutting off next year's flowers before they've had a chance.

New-wood bloomers wait until the current season's growth hardens up before flowering. Buddleja, hydrangea paniculata, rose of Sharon, and most summer-flowering spiraeas fit here. Because their buds form on shoots that haven't even emerged yet when winter ends, you can cut these hard in late winter or early spring without losing a single flower.

A third group - reblooming or remontant shrubs - can flower on both old and new wood. Some modern hydrangea cultivars sit in this category. With these, light shaping is usually safer than hard cutting; you may sacrifice some early blooms but keep the later flush.

Quick Test

If you're unsure which camp a shrub belongs to, note when it flowers. Blooms appearing before midsummer on last year's stems? Old wood. Blooms appearing from midsummer onward on this year's new growth? New wood. When in doubt, observe for a season before making big cuts.

When to Prune Spring-Flowering Shrubs

The window for old-wood bloomers is short and specific: prune them as soon as the flowers fade, while the plant is still actively growing and has the whole summer ahead to set next year's buds. For most gardeners in temperate zones this means sometime between late spring and early summer, depending on your local frost dates - the timing shifts with your last frost date and the arrival of warm weather.

For lilacs, that means getting in there within three to four weeks of the last flower dropping. For forsythia, which blooms very early, you have more time but still want to finish before midsummer. Azaleas and rhododendrons - I try to deadhead them and do any shaping within six weeks of bloom.

What you're doing in this window is removing spent flowers, thinning out crossing branches, cutting back a third of the oldest stems to ground level to encourage fresh growth from the base. You are not cutting everything back hard. These are not plants that respond well to drastic renovation every year.

Pruning Summer-Flowering Shrubs in Late Winter

New-wood bloomers reward hard pruning in late winter, before the sap starts rising in earnest. Buddleja - the butterfly bush - can be cut to within 30 cm of the ground and will come back twice as vigorous, with bigger flower spikes than it would have produced if left alone. Hydrangea paniculata responds similarly.

The practical window is usually late winter to very early spring, once the worst hard freezes are behind you but before growth begins. Getting the timing right here means checking your local conditions rather than following a fixed calendar date. If you garden in a climate with variable late frosts, let the last-frost calendar guide you - you don't want fresh growth emerging into a hard freeze.

One thing I've noticed: gardeners often leave buddleja too tall because it feels drastic to cut it low. The resulting plant is leggy, with flowers at the tips of long bare stems. Cut it hard. It genuinely does not mind.

Renovation Pruning: When a Shrub Has Been Neglected

Overgrown flowering shrubs are one of the more satisfying problems to solve, but they require patience. With most species, severe renovation - cutting everything hard all at once - risks stressing the plant badly and sometimes kills it outright.

The gentler approach is a three-year renovation. Remove roughly one-third of the oldest, woodiest stems to ground level each year. By year three, the shrub has been entirely replaced by newer growth without ever suffering a catastrophic cut. The plant flowers (at least partially) throughout the process.

Old roses, deutzia, weigela, and mock orange all respond beautifully to this phased approach. I've seen neglected mock orange go from a tangled, barely-flowering mass to an open, floriferous shrub over three seasons without ever losing a year of bloom entirely. Good long-term planning like this pays compounding dividends in the garden.

Signs That a Shrub Needs Renovation

  • Fewer flowers each year despite adequate sun and water
  • Dense, congested interior with a lot of dead wood
  • New growth emerging only from the tips of old, bare stems
  • The plant has simply grown far outside its intended space

Tools, Safety, and Keeping Cuts Clean

Sharp tools matter here more than almost anywhere else in the garden. Ragged cuts from blunt secateurs don't heal cleanly and invite disease. Before you start any pruning session, wipe your blades with a cloth dampened with a dilute disinfectant solution - something as simple as 70% isopropyl alcohol works - and sharpen if needed. This is especially worth doing if you're moving between plants or if you've been cutting diseased material.

For shrubs taller than head height or growing against a wall where you need a ladder, take reasonable precautions: stable ground, someone nearby, no overreaching. Powered hedge trimmers make quick work of formal hedges but demand hearing and eye protection. Always follow the manufacturer's guidance for any tool you use.

Some flowering shrubs are worth noting for toxicity. Oleander is severely toxic to people, pets, and livestock - wear gloves, don't burn the prunings, and keep children and animals away from the cut material. Rhododendron and azalea are also toxic if ingested. If you have pets who browse the garden, check the toxicity of anything you're growing. For any suspected plant poisoning in people or animals, contact your local poison control line or vet rather than treating at home.

Tool Tip

Keep three tools to hand: bypass secateurs for stems up to 1.5 cm, loppers for stems up to 3 cm, and a pruning saw for anything thicker. Using the right tool for the diameter gives cleaner cuts and saves your wrists.

Pest and Disease Checks While You Prune

Pruning time is one of the best moments to inspect a shrub closely - you're already in there, moving branches, looking at bark. I always pause to check for scale insects on stems, for signs of canker, for the webbing of spider mites, or for any unusual discolouration.

Catching problems early makes them far easier to manage. A small colony of aphids on new growth can often be removed with a strong jet of water; a serious scale infestation on woody stems may need a horticultural oil treatment applied according to the product label. If pests spread to fruiting plants nearby, the same vigilance applies - the principles that protect your fruit trees from pest pressure apply to ornamental shrubs in the same garden.

Remove and bin (don't compost) any material that shows signs of disease. Diseased wood sitting in a compost heap can reinfect plants the following season.

A Simple Seasonal Schedule

Rather than memorising every species, a seasonal rhythm keeps things manageable:

  1. Late winter - Prune new-wood bloomers hard (buddleja, hydrangea paniculata, rose of Sharon, summer spiraeas). Check tools, sharpen blades.
  2. Late spring to early summer - Deadhead and lightly shape spring-flowering old-wood shrubs (lilac, forsythia, azalea, viburnum) within a few weeks of bloom fading.
  3. Midsummer - Tidy up as needed; avoid heavy pruning on anything that hasn't yet flowered.
  4. Autumn - Resist the urge to cut back old-wood bloomers. Remove storm damage only, and check for pests before plants go dormant.

Can I prune flowering shrubs in autumn to tidy the garden before winter?

For new-wood bloomers like buddleja, a light tidy in autumn is fine, though the main hard cut is better left until late winter. For old-wood bloomers - lilac, forsythia, rhododendron - avoid any significant pruning in autumn; you'll remove the buds that have already formed for next spring's flowers. If the plant genuinely needs tidying, restrict yourself to removing dead or damaged wood only.

My hydrangea didn't flower this year even though I didn't prune it. What went wrong?

For old-wood hydrangeas (like Hydrangea macrophylla, the classic mophead), a late frost in spring is the most common culprit - it kills the buds that were already formed. Siting the plant somewhere sheltered, or covering it with fleece during a forecast frost, helps. Also check whether the plant is getting enough sun; most hydrangeas need at least a few hours of direct light daily to flower well.

How hard can I cut back an overgrown lilac?

Lilacs tolerate fairly drastic pruning, but cutting everything hard at once will mean no flowers for two or three years while the plant recovers. The three-year renovation approach - removing one-third of the oldest stems to ground level each year - is gentler and keeps the plant flowering throughout. If the lilac is very old and heavily suckering, consult a local arborist or extension service before making major cuts.

Flowering shrubs are patient teachers. Get the timing right once and the lesson sticks for every similar plant you ever grow. Start by identifying what's already in your garden, note when each shrub blooms, and work from there - the right moment to prune will be obvious. There's no rush in a well-tended garden.
