A few summers ago, I stood at the edge of a client's back garden watching her neighbour's patio umbrella swing into view every time the breeze picked up. She'd been meaning to plant something for three years. The fence quotes kept coming back too high, and somehow the right shrubs never quite made it into the ground.

A living screen takes longer than a fence, yes. But it gives back in ways timber never does - habitat for birds, a buffer against wind noise, seasonal interest, and roots that will still be doing their quiet work decades from now. The key is choosing plants that fill in fast enough to feel like progress, without outgrowing their space in a decade.

Below are seven shrubs I'd reach for first, arranged roughly from quickest-to-establish to most architecturally refined. Most of them work in a mixed hedge, which gives you redundancy - if one plant struggles, its neighbours carry the line.

1. Cherry Laurel (Prunus laurocerasus)

Cherry laurel is the workhorse of the privacy hedge world. It puts on 30-60 cm of growth a year in reasonable soil, holds its large, glossy leaves through winter, and tolerates shade that would kill most alternatives.

Clip it once in late summer with secateurs rather than shears - shears slice the big leaves in half and the brown edges linger all season. The variety 'Rotundifolia' is the one I plant most often: upright, vigorous, and not prone to the leggy gaps that plague some forms.

Toxicity note

Cherry laurel leaves, berries, and seeds are toxic to people, dogs, cats, and horses if ingested. Wear gloves when pruning, dispose of clippings carefully, and keep children and pets away from fallen berries. If ingestion is suspected, contact your vet or local poison control line immediately.

2. Photinia 'Red Robin'

Photinia earns its place by being ornamental as well as dense. The new growth flushes bright red in spring and again after each trim, so even a functional screen looks intentional. Growth rate is a solid 30-45 cm per year once established.

It prefers a well-drained soil and full sun, though it tolerates partial shade. The main enemy is photinia leaf spot, a fungal disease that causes black blotches in wet summers. Good air circulation and avoiding overhead watering keep it mostly in check - I've rarely needed anything beyond that. If you do reach for a fungicide, follow the product label and prefer the least-toxic option available.

3. Western Red Cedar (Thuja plicata)

For a truly tall screen - above 3 m - western red cedar is hard to beat. The foliage is dense, aromatic, and naturally resistant to many pests. The variety 'Atrovirens' holds a rich dark green all year, unlike the golden cultivars that look washed-out in strong sun.

Plant in late autumn or early spring and keep well-watered for the first two seasons. Thuja responds well to formative pruning in the early years; a light shaping each August keeps the hedge tight from the base upwards. Leave it unpruned and it will develop the columnar shape on its own, though more loosely. Before planting a very tall hedge along a boundary, it's worth checking with your local authority or a qualified arborist - some areas have bylaws about boundary planting height.

4. Viburnum tinus

Viburnum tinus is the unsung hero of the winter garden. It flowers from November through to April - white or faintly pink clusters that pollinators actually use on mild winter days - and produces metallic blue-black berries that birds eat greedily. The evergreen foliage is dense enough to screen a boundary year-round.

It's slower than cherry laurel in the first year or two, but once its root system finds its feet it catches up quickly. It also happens to be one of the best shrubs for a mixed edible or wildlife hedge, and if you're already thinking about planting berry bushes alongside your privacy screen, viburnum makes an excellent structural backdrop that won't compete aggressively with fruiting neighbours.

5. Leylandii (× Cuprocyparis leylandii)

I know, I know. Leylandii has a reputation. But here's the honest case for it: nothing else establishes a screen as fast in poor or exposed conditions. It gains 60-90 cm a year, shrugs off coastal wind and heavy clay, and provides dense cover from ground level if you never let it get away from you.

The key phrase is never let it get away from you. Clip it twice a year - May and August - and keep the height to what you actually need. Left alone it becomes the 10-metre monster that has broken more neighbours' friendships than any other plant. Kept in check, it's genuinely useful. Just make a note in your calendar and keep the commitment.

Hedge spacing

For most screening shrubs, plant at 60-90 cm apart in a single row. If you want faster cover, a double staggered row at 90 cm in-row spacing fills gaps quickly - but it also means twice the competition for water and nutrients, so soil preparation matters more. Incorporate plenty of garden compost at planting time and mulch heavily to retain moisture in the first summer.

6. Portuguese Laurel (Prunus lusitanica)

Where cherry laurel would be too vigorous, Portuguese laurel is the refined alternative. Smaller leaves, a more graceful branching structure, and happy on chalk soils where many shrubs sulk. It also tolerates hard pruning with real aplomb - you can renovate a neglected specimen quite aggressively in early spring and it will regenerate without complaint.

Growth is slower at around 20-30 cm a year, which means it takes more patience upfront. I'd always plant it in combination with something faster-growing if you need coverage within three or four seasons. The red stems and dark green foliage look genuinely good even before the plant is big enough to screen anything.

Spacing and companion planting

Portuguese laurel pairs particularly well with Viburnum tinus. Plant them alternately at 90 cm spacing and the viburnum fills in fast while the laurel slowly takes over as the more permanent structural plant. In five years you can remove some of the viburnum if the laurels have closed the gaps.

7. Hornbeam (Carpinus betulus)

Hornbeam is technically deciduous, but it holds its brown dead leaves through winter, which means a well-maintained hornbeam hedge still provides reasonable screening in January. It's also one of the best choices for a wildlife-friendly boundary that slots into the wider garden ecology - something worth thinking about if you've taken the time to plan a garden from scratch with biodiversity in mind.

It grows 30-60 cm a year, tolerates wet clay better than beech (its usual comparison), and responds beautifully to a single annual clip in August. The pleated, ribbed foliage has a texture that looks far more deliberate than a clipped conifer - it reads as a hedge rather than a wall, which suits some settings better.

Pollination and berries in a hedge context

If you're weaving any fruit-bearing species into your mixed hedge - and it's worth doing - bear in mind that tree and shrub pollination works differently at hedge density than in open orchard conditions. Most self-fertile berry shrubs will produce without a partner, but yields improve with cross-pollination, so plant at least two varieties of any fruiting species you include.

Planting time matters

Bare-root shrubs go in between November and March and are considerably cheaper than pot-grown equivalents - often half the price. If you're screening a long boundary, bare-root stock makes the budget workable. Container-grown plants can go in any time of year, but they need more aftercare in summer. Either way, the first season's watering is the most important investment you'll make.

How long does it take for a privacy hedge to actually screen a view?

It depends on the plant and starting size, but as a rough guide: fast growers like cherry laurel and leylandii can provide usable coverage in two to three seasons from a 60 cm transplant. Slower species like hornbeam or Portuguese laurel typically take four to six years to form a solid screen at 1.5-2 m. Buying larger transplants speeds this up but costs significantly more - it's a genuine trade-off between time and budget.

Can I mix different shrubs in the same hedge?

Yes, and mixed hedges are often more resilient than single-species ones. If a pest or disease targets one species, the others carry the screen. The main challenge is managing different growth rates - you may need to hold back the vigorous ones with more frequent trimming while the slower plants catch up. Plant in groups of three rather than alternating every plant, which tends to look patchy when the two species grow at different speeds.

Do I need planning permission to plant a privacy hedge?

In most cases, no - but high hedges along shared boundaries can trigger disputes, and some areas have specific bylaws or covenant restrictions. In England and Wales, the High Hedges legislation allows neighbours to complain to the local council about evergreen hedges over 2 m that block light. The best approach is to plan the final height before you plant, choose a species you're willing to maintain at that height, and have a conversation with affected neighbours early. When in doubt, check with your local planning authority or a qualified arborist.

A living screen asks for a different kind of commitment than a fence: a few hours at planting time, a little water through the first summer, and a clip once or twice a year after that. In return it keeps improving. The hedge my client finally planted - a mix of cherry laurel and viburnum - was already knee-high by the following spring and shoulder-height the summer after. She stopped mentioning the umbrella entirely.
