Marcus planted fourteen things in his first garden. By the end of summer, five had genuinely thrived, four had limped along, three had been eaten down to stubs by slugs, and two he still cannot identify from the seed packet photos. That is, honestly, a pretty decent first season.

I walked through his plot in early October - a raised bed roughly 2.4 metres by 1.2 metres tucked against the south-facing fence of his terrace - and what I saw was a garden that had taught him more than any book could. The courgettes had bolted to the size of baseball bats. The basil was purple with stress. The tomatoes were finally, stubbornly, turning red in the last gasp of warmth.

This is his story, and it belongs to every new grower who has ever stared at a seed packet in February and thought: how hard can this be?

The Starting Point: One Raised Bed, High Hopes

Marcus built his raised bed in late March using untreated pine planks and a bag of topsoil mixed with multipurpose compost. He filled it to about 20 cm deep - enough for most crops, though not ideal for deep-rooted carrots, as he later discovered.

He ordered seeds online in a burst of enthusiasm: tomatoes (two varieties), courgettes, salad leaves, climbing French beans, basil, spring onions, and - optimistically - celery. The celery never made it past seedling stage. Celery is a slow, demanding crop that needs months of steady moisture and warmth. It is not a beginner crop. Nobody warns you about that.

Elena's note

When you are choosing your first crops, lean toward things that forgive impatience: salad leaves, courgettes, French beans, radishes, and cherry tomatoes. They grow fast enough to keep you encouraged and resilient enough to survive the inevitable beginner errors.

What the Numbers Actually Looked Like

Marcus kept rough notes - nothing formal, just a phone photo each week and the occasional jotted weight. Over the season, he harvested around 3 kg of cherry tomatoes, roughly 2 kg of salad leaves (cut-and-come-again, so harvested in batches), about eight courgettes before he gave up keeping track, and a handful of French beans that were genuinely the best vegetables he says he has ever eaten.

The spring onions were thin but usable. The basil provided three decent harvests before it bolted. The climbing beans were the real winners: planted late, forgotten about, and then suddenly producing more than he could use in a week.

What he did not harvest: the two mystery plants, the celery, and the second tomato variety that produced exactly zero fruit before the frosts arrived. A determinate variety crammed into a small pot with no support. Lessons cost nothing in a garden - just time.

The Mistakes That Taught Him the Most

Overcrowding

Marcus planted his tomatoes about 25 cm apart. The label said 60 cm. He thought that seemed excessive. By July, the bed was a tangle of leaves blocking the light from everything underneath. Overcrowding is the single most common first-season mistake, and it compounds quickly - poor airflow invites disease, and shaded soil stays cold and damp.

If you find yourself unsure whether plants look too close together, they almost certainly are. Spacing recommendations exist for good reason, and a spaced-out bed will outyield a crammed one every time.

Inconsistent Watering

Work trips meant the bed went unwatered for five or six days twice during the season. The tomatoes responded with blossom end rot on a few fruits - the classic dark, sunken patch on the base that looks alarming but is caused by calcium uptake disruption from uneven moisture, not disease. If you see that in your own plot, diagnosing plant problems early can save the rest of the crop.

A simple fix for next year: Marcus set a reminder on his phone and bought a cheap moisture meter. Low-tech, but effective.

Sowing Everything at Once

Marcus direct-sowed his salad leaves all in the same week. He had more salad than he could eat for three weeks, then nothing. Understanding succession planting - sowing small batches every two to three weeks - would have given him a steady supply through the whole season instead of one overwhelming flush followed by a gap.

What Went Surprisingly Right

The French beans were a revelation. Marcus sowed them directly into the bed in late May, pushed a few sticks in for support, and watered them in. They asked very little and gave a lot. That first handful of beans - eaten warm, straight from the pod, ten steps from his kitchen - is what made him certain he would do this again.

The cut-and-come-again salad leaves were similarly low-effort. He snipped the outer leaves once a week, and they kept regrowing. By the middle of the season he had stopped buying bagged salad entirely. That felt significant.

He also discovered mid-season that his kitchen windowsill could do real work. A few herb pots tucked there - parsley, chives, a small pot of thyme - stretched his harvests considerably, and that windowsill herb garden cost almost nothing to maintain once established.

Quick win for year two

Start a simple garden notebook - even a notes app works. Record what you sowed, when you sowed it, and how it performed. One season of notes is worth more than ten books for making decisions about your own plot in your own climate.

The Soil Situation

By September, Marcus noticed the bed had sunk about 5 cm. Compost settles and breaks down - this is normal, and it is good news, because it means the organic matter is doing its job. What he needed to do was top the bed up before winter with a few centimetres of compost, and maybe add a handful of balanced organic fertiliser the following spring before planting.

Soil health is a long game. One season of growing actually improves a bed if you add organic matter back. Marcus now has a small compost bin in the corner of his garden. It accepts his vegetable peelings and spent plants. By spring, it will give him something back.

The Takeaways: What Marcus Would Do Differently

  • Grow fewer varieties and give each more space.
  • Sow salad leaves in small batches, two to three weeks apart, from April through August.
  • Grow climbing beans every year, without question.
  • Skip celery until he has a few seasons under his belt.
  • Set up a simple watering routine - a calendar reminder, a watering can by the back door, maybe a soaker hose for dry spells.
  • Top up the bed with compost each autumn so it is ready to go in spring.

None of these are complicated. None require specialist equipment or expert knowledge. They are the things you only learn by doing it once.

Season summary

Plot size: 2.4 m x 1.2 m raised bed. Crops attempted: 14. Strong performers: climbing French beans, cherry tomatoes, cut-and-come-again salad, courgettes, spring onions. Crops to skip next time: celery, overloaded pot tomatoes. Estimated total harvest weight: around 8-10 kg. Cost of seeds and compost: roughly £35. Value of the experience: considerable.

How big does a first vegetable garden need to be?

Smaller than you think. A raised bed of around 1.2 m by 2.4 m is manageable for a beginner and large enough to grow a meaningful variety of crops. Starting too large is one of the most common ways new gardeners get overwhelmed in their first season.

What are the easiest vegetables for a complete beginner?

Climbing French beans, cherry tomatoes, courgettes, cut-and-come-again salad leaves, and radishes are all excellent starting points. They are fast-growing, forgiving of minor mistakes, and satisfying to harvest. Avoid slow crops like celery, celeriac, or large brassicas until you have a season or two of experience.

What should I do to the bed at the end of the season?

Clear spent plants, top up the bed with 5-8 cm of compost, and leave it covered if possible - a mulch of compost or straw will protect the soil structure through winter. Do not leave bare soil exposed to heavy rain, which can compact and deplete it. Your bed will be in noticeably better shape come spring.

Marcus is already planning next year. He has sketched out which corner will get the beans and where he will give the tomatoes the space they actually need. That planning instinct - the willingness to reflect, adjust, and go again - is the thing that separates gardeners who stick with it from those who abandon the bed by August. He has it. Most first-timers do, once they see that first harvest come in.
