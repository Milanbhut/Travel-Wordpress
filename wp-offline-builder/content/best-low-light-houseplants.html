Picture the north-facing sitting room: white walls doing their best, a window that catches maybe two hours of indirect glow on a good day. Most plant labels claim their occupants will handle it. Most are lying. I've killed enough peace lilies in dingy hallways to know the difference between "tolerates low light" written by an optimist and a plant that actually, genuinely, gets on with shade.

The good news is that a handful of species really do perform in these conditions - not just survive with two yellow leaves and a prayer, but grow, look lush, and make a room feel designed rather than neglected. I've grown all seven of the plants below in rooms with no direct sun, and I'd recommend every one of them.

A quick note on expectations before we start: "low light" doesn't mean "no light." We're talking about bright enough to comfortably read a paperback without turning on a lamp. Darker than that, and even these troopers will stall. Keep that benchmark in mind as you walk around your home assessing spots.

1. Pothos (Epipremnum aureum)

If I were handing a plant to someone who'd killed everything they'd ever owned, it would be a pothos. The trailing stems adapt to an astonishing range of conditions, the leaves stay glossy green even several feet from a window, and when something does go wrong the plant gives you plenty of warning before it gives up entirely.

Golden pothos is the most forgiving variety, but marble queen and neon both handle shade nearly as well. Let the top inch of soil dry out between waterings - roots sitting in wet compost is the one thing that will finish them off. If you want more plants for free, propagating pothos cuttings in a jar of water is one of the most satisfying things you can do on a Sunday afternoon.

2. Cast Iron Plant (Aspidistra elatior)

The Victorians put this one in the darkest corners of their parlours, and if it survived gaslight and coal dust, it will survive your north-facing spare room. The cast iron plant earns its name: wide, architectural, deep-green leaves that look like they were placed by a set designer, and a tolerance for neglect that borders on the supernatural.

Water sparingly - roughly every two to three weeks in winter, a little more in summer. Don't rush to repot; aspidistras actually prefer being slightly pot-bound. Growth is slow, so if yours hasn't put out a new leaf in a few months, that's completely normal and no cause for alarm.

Toxicity note

Cast iron plants are considered non-toxic to cats and dogs, which makes them a rare double-win for dim rooms in pet households. Always double-check with your vet if you're concerned, since individual animals can have unusual sensitivities.

3. ZZ Plant (Zamioculcas zamiifolia)

The ZZ plant handles low light through an almost unfair biological advantage: fat rhizomes underground that store water and nutrients like a camel. You can forget it for a month and come back to find it looking perfectly composed. I once left one on a shelf above a bookcase - about as far from a window as you can get - for six months and it didn't flinch.

Water even less than you think you should, and use a well-draining potting mix. The main mistake people make with ZZ plants is overwatering once they've read that it's "easy." It is easy - easy to overwater, specifically. Check the soil at root depth before adding more moisture.

One important heads-up: ZZ plants are toxic if ingested and the sap can irritate skin in sensitive people. Keep them away from toddlers and curious pets, and wash your hands after handling them. If you suspect your pet has eaten any part of the plant, contact your vet promptly.

4. Snake Plant (Dracaena trifasciata)

Ask any interior photographer what they put in a dim corner to make it look like a curated design shoot, and the answer is almost always a snake plant. The vertical, architectural silhouette works beautifully against walls, and the variegated leaf patterns - cream edges, banded greens - hold their visual interest even in poor light.

Snake plants photosynthesize via a water-efficient pathway called CAM, which means they do most of their gas exchange at night. This doesn't make them magic air purifiers (that research has been substantially overstated), but it does mean they cope with dimmer conditions better than many leafy plants. Water once every two to three weeks, less in winter. Also worth knowing: moderately toxic to cats and dogs - keep them elevated in pet homes.

5. Peace Lily (Spathiphyllum)

Here's where I have to defend myself for the losses mentioned earlier. Peace lilies can handle low light - what they can't handle is inconsistent watering combined with low light. Get the watering right, and these are genuinely beautiful plants: dark glossy leaves, elegant white spathes in spring, and a lush tropical feeling that makes a grey room feel like somewhere interesting.

They wilt dramatically and visibly when thirsty, which some people find alarming but is actually useful - they're telling you exactly what they need. Water thoroughly when the top inch of compost dries out. If the leaves are developing yellow patches, it's worth reading up on why houseplant leaves turn yellow, since peace lilies can yellow from overwatering, underwatering, or drafts. Toxic to people and pets if ingested; contact a poison control line or vet if any is consumed.

6. Chinese Evergreen (Aglaonema)

Aglaonemas offer something most low-light plants don't: genuine colour. The darker-leafed varieties - deep green with silver or grey markings - handle the dimmest conditions best, while the pink and red cultivars need a little more light to hold their colour. For a truly dark room, stick to green-dominant varieties like 'Maria' or 'Silver Bay.'

They're slow-growing but steady, and a mature specimen with its layered, spreading habit has a real presence in a room. Water when the top inch of soil is dry, and wipe the leaves occasionally to keep them looking their best. Like many aroids, chinese evergreens are mildly toxic if eaten - relevant if you have cats who like to chew.

Designer tip

Pair a dark-leaved aglaonema in a low terracotta pot with a taller snake plant behind it - the layered height and contrasting leaf shapes create a planting that looks intentional even in the dimmest corner. Think of it as vertical composition on a small scale.

7. Heartleaf Philodendron (Philodendron hederaceum)

If pothos is the practical choice, heartleaf philodendron is the romantic one. The leaves are softer in texture, a richer green, and the trailing habit has a more generous, cascading quality. Draped from a high shelf or spilling over the side of a tall plant stand, it looks effortlessly lush.

Like pothos, it adapts well to lower light levels, though the internodal gaps between leaves will lengthen slightly as it reaches for light - a sign it could use a slightly brighter spot, but not a crisis. Water when the top inch of compost is dry. It propagates just as easily as pothos does, making it a good candidate if you want to fill several spots around a dim room without spending a fortune.

If you enjoy low-maintenance trailing plants indoors but also want something colourful for a sunnier windowsill or a pot near the door, easy annual flowers for beginners are worth exploring once warmer months arrive - a way to extend the green-thumb habit outside.

How dark is too dark for low-light houseplants?

A useful rule of thumb: if you can comfortably read a book by the natural light in a spot, most low-light plants will manage there. If you need to turn on a lamp to read comfortably during the day, even shade-tolerant plants will struggle long-term and will slowly decline. Supplementing with a simple grow light for a few hours a day can make otherwise borderline spots workable.

Do low-light plants still need watering as often as bright-light ones?

Generally, no. Plants in lower light photosynthesize more slowly, which means they use less water overall. Overwatering is the single most common cause of death for shade-tolerant houseplants, especially ZZ plants and snake plants. Always check the soil at a depth of an inch or two rather than watering on a fixed schedule - conditions vary too much from room to room and season to season.

Can I use artificial light to supplement a dark room?

Yes, and it works well. A simple LED grow light positioned 30-60 cm above the plant for around 10-12 hours a day can make a real difference, especially through winter when natural light is scarce. You don't need anything expensive - a broad-spectrum LED strip or clip-on bulb is enough to keep most of the plants listed here looking good. It's worth doing rather than pushing these species beyond their actual tolerance in deep shade.

Dim rooms are genuinely workable once you stop fighting the conditions and lean into plants that embrace them. Any of the seven species above will reward you with steady, reliable growth - and a few of them will make a dark corner look like it was designed that way on purpose. Start with one, get a feel for the watering rhythm, then add another. There's no rush; shade gardening is a slow and satisfying kind of pleasure.
