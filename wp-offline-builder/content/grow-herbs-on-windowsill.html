Last February I set three small pots of basil on my south-facing kitchen windowsill, fully expecting them to limp along until spring. By week four, I was cutting enough leaves for fresh pesto twice a week - and I hadn't moved them an inch. That experience taught me that a windowsill herb garden succeeds or fails on a handful of decisions made right at the start, not on special equipment or a green thumb.

The good news is that most culinary herbs are forgiving once you understand what they actually want: bright light, decent drainage, and harvesting done in a way that encourages the plant to keep producing rather than slow down. Miss one of those and you end up with leggy, flavourless stems. Get all three right and your kitchen bench becomes genuinely useful garden space year-round.

This is a practical walkthrough of every step, from choosing which herbs to start with to the exact way to cut them so they don't give up on you after the first handful.

Choose the Right Herbs for Indoor Life

Not every herb thrives indoors, and starting with the wrong ones is the most common reason windowsill gardens fail. Woody Mediterranean herbs - rosemary, thyme, sage - can work, but they need extremely bright light and excellent air circulation to avoid fungal problems. For most homes, the easier wins are basil, chives, parsley, mint, and coriander (cilantro).

Mint deserves a mention because it is almost indestructible indoors. It will tolerate lower light than the others and keeps producing through short winter days. The one rule with mint: always grow it in its own container. It spreads aggressively and will outcompete everything else in a shared pot within a few weeks.

Basil, by contrast, is light-hungry and warm-loving. If your windowsill is cold or only gets a couple of hours of direct sun, start with chives or parsley instead - both are cooler-season herbs that cope much better with a north or east-facing window.

Pick the Spot Before You Pick the Pot

The single biggest lever you have is light. Culinary herbs generally want at least four to six hours of direct sun per day. In the northern hemisphere, a south-facing windowsill is ideal; west-facing works reasonably well. North-facing windows make herbs sulk - the stems stretch toward the glass, go pale, and the flavour thins out.

If your flat doesn't have a sunny window, a modest LED grow light suspended about 15-20 cm above the pots for 12-14 hours a day makes a genuine difference. I'd rather you try that than abandon the idea entirely. You can use the same grow-light setup you'd use for starting vegetable seeds indoors - same spectrum, same timing rules apply.

One thing often overlooked: radiators. Many windowsills in older homes sit directly above a radiator, which creates hot, dry air that stresses herbs and dries compost out in 24 hours in winter. If that's your situation, either raise the pots on a small tray or set them back from the glass on a nearby shelf.

Get the Containers and Compost Right

Drainage is non-negotiable. Herbs sit in soggy compost for more than a day or two and the roots begin to rot - it happens faster than you'd expect, especially in low light when the plant isn't drinking much water. Whatever pot you choose, make sure it has a drainage hole. A saucer underneath is fine, just don't let water pool in it for more than an hour after watering.

For pot size, a 12-15 cm pot works well for a single herb. Go larger if you're planting two or three chive plants together, because chives bulk up considerably. Terracotta is lovely for herbs and breathes well, but it dries out quickly - check moisture more often if you use it.

For compost, a peat-free multi-purpose mix is a solid starting point. If you have sandy or gritty compost on hand, mixing in about a third by volume improves drainage further, particularly for thyme and rosemary. Avoid heavy potting mixes designed for trees or shrubs - they hold too much moisture for most herbs.

Tip: Scratch-test before watering

Push your finger about 2 cm into the compost. If it comes out clean and dry, water thoroughly until it drains from the bottom. If it comes out damp, wait another day. Overwatering kills more windowsill herbs than underwatering.

Sow or Buy? How to Get Plants Established

Buying small plants from a supermarket or garden centre gets you to harvest faster, but those supermarket herbs are often grown in ideal commercial conditions - crammed with many plants per pot, fed on liquid fertiliser, and moved into low-light homes where they rapidly decline. They're not designed to last; they're designed to get you through a recipe or two.

To salvage a supermarket herb, pot it up into a larger container almost immediately, water it in, and put it in your best-lit spot. Within a couple of weeks, the roots spread into fresh compost and the plant stabilises. I've rescued dozens of supermarket basil plants this way.

Starting from seed takes longer - basil is up in about a week, chives in two - but you get much sturdier plants and more variety. If you're starting seeds on a windowsill and wondering about timing and light management, the principles are essentially the same as when you start vegetable seeds indoors: warmth for germination, then light as the priority once they emerge.

How to Harvest So the Plant Keeps Going

This is the step that separates a productive windowsill garden from a collection of steadily declining pots. The way you harvest determines whether the plant keeps pushing out new growth or starts to shut down.

For basil, always cut just above a leaf node - the point where two leaves join the stem. The plant will branch at that node and give you two stems where there was one. Never strip leaves from the bottom up; always cut whole stems from the top. If you see flower buds forming, pinch them off immediately. Once basil flowers, the leaves shrink and the flavour drops sharply.

Chives are simpler: cut the leaves down to about 2-3 cm above the soil, like trimming grass. They'll regrow from the base within a week or two. Parsley should be harvested from the outside stems first, leaving the inner cluster to develop. Never take more than a third of any herb plant at one cutting - this is the rule that matters most for keeping production steady over months rather than weeks.

Feeding, Watering, and Keeping Things Healthy

A monthly liquid feed during the growing season (roughly spring through early autumn) keeps potted herbs producing well. A general balanced liquid fertiliser diluted to the manufacturer's recommended rate is fine - follow the label instructions and don't be tempted to use more than directed. Excess nitrogen gives you lush, pale growth that's watery in flavour and prone to aphid attack.

Aphids and whitefly are the main pests indoors. If you spot either, a gentle spray of insecticidal soap (again, follow the label) or simply wiping the leaves with a damp cloth tackles them early. Good air circulation reduces the likelihood in the first place - don't crowd pots together against the glass with no space between them.

If you're also growing food in other containers, many of the same soil and nutrition principles apply. The lessons from reviving depleted container soil translate directly: drainage, organic matter, and not letting compost become compacted and waterlogged over time are what keep roots happy.

Troubleshooting the Most Common Problems

Leggy, pale stems

Almost always a light problem. Move the pot closer to the window or supplement with a grow light. If you can't provide more light, switch to chives or parsley, which cope better with lower levels.

Yellowing lower leaves

Could be overwatering, or a plant sitting in cold draughts. Check that the compost dries out slightly between waterings, and make sure the pot isn't pressed against cold glass on winter nights.

Herbs that bolt quickly

Coriander is notorious for running to seed fast, especially if it gets warm. Sow a small pinch of seed every three weeks rather than one large batch - staggered sowings give you a continuous supply rather than a glut followed by nothing. The same succession-planting logic that works for container tomatoes applies just as well to fast-cycling herbs like coriander and basil.

Safety note

Most common culinary herbs are safe for humans in food amounts, but several - including chives, garlic chives, and many ornamental herbs - are toxic to cats and dogs if eaten. Keep pots out of reach of pets, and if you suspect a pet has eaten a plant, contact your vet or a pet poison helpline rather than waiting to see how they respond.

Can I grow herbs on a north-facing windowsill?

You can try, but results will be modest. Chives and parsley are your best options for low-light spots - they're more tolerant than basil or rosemary. A small LED grow light above the pots for 12 hours a day will make a bigger difference than any other change you can make in a north-facing room.

How often should I water windowsill herbs?

There's no fixed schedule - it depends on pot size, light levels, and season. The reliable method is the finger test: push a finger 2 cm into the compost. Water only when it comes out dry. In summer that might mean every other day; in a cool winter it might be once a week.

Why does my basil keep dying after a few weeks?

The two most common reasons are overwatering and insufficient light. Supermarket basil plants also tend to be rootbound and stressed from the start - repot them into fresh compost immediately, give them your brightest spot, and water only when the compost feels dry. Most "dying" basil plants recover well once those basics are addressed.

A windowsill herb garden is one of the most immediately rewarding things you can grow - the return on a pot of basil or a clump of chives is felt every single time you cook. Start with one or two herbs, learn how they respond in your particular light conditions, and add more once you have a feel for the rhythm. The plants will tell you what they need; you just have to pay attention.
