There is something quietly deflating about a mature apple tree that flowers beautifully every May and then produces almost nothing by autumn. I have seen it more times than I can count - in back gardens, on allotments, even in small orchards that otherwise look well-managed. The tree is healthy. The soil is fine. The pruning has been done. What is missing is simply another tree nearby.

Pollination is one of those subjects that sounds complicated but follows a clear logic once you sit with it. Fruit trees set fruit when pollen moves from the stamens of one flower to the pistil of another - usually of the same species but often a different variety. Whether that transfer needs to happen within one tree or between two depends entirely on the species, and sometimes on the variety within that species.

Getting this right before you plant saves years of frustration. So let us walk through how tree pollination actually works, where the lines fall between self-fertile and partner-needing trees, and how to build a planting plan that gives your orchard a real chance.

What pollination actually does for a fruit tree

When a bee or other insect visits a flower, it picks up pollen on its body and carries it to the next flower it visits. If that pollen lands on a compatible pistil, fertilisation can occur and a seed begins to form. The fruit is, at its most basic, the plant's way of protecting and dispersing those seeds.

Without successful pollination, the flower simply drops off. The tree may look like it is flowering well, but fruitset - the proportion of flowers that go on to become fruit - stays very low. Gardeners sometimes mistake this for a pest problem, a nutrient deficiency, or a late frost event. Often it is just a missing pollinator partner.

It is worth remembering that weather plays a role too. A cold, wet spring with few insects flying will reduce pollination even for a tree with a perfect partner nearby. You cannot control the weather, but you can control your planting choices.

Self-fertile versus self-sterile: where trees fall

A self-fertile (or self-compatible) tree can pollinate itself - its own pollen can fertilise its own flowers, and it will fruit reasonably well when growing alone. A self-sterile (or self-incompatible) tree needs pollen from a different variety of the same species to set fruit. Some trees fall somewhere in the middle: technically self-fertile but producing far more fruit when cross-pollinated.

Here is a rough guide to where common fruit trees sit:

Fruit tree General pollination need Notes
Apple Usually self-sterile Needs a compatible variety flowering at the same time; exceptions include 'Cox's Self-Fertile' and 'Braeburn'
Pear Usually self-sterile Some ('Conference') set a modest crop alone but fruit far better with a partner
Sweet cherry Mostly self-sterile 'Stella' and 'Sunburst' are reliable self-fertile exceptions
Sour/Morello cherry Self-fertile Good choice for single-tree situations; tolerates shade
Plum / Damson Mixed Many plums are self-fertile; 'Victoria' sets well alone but benefits from company
Peach / Nectarine Self-fertile Flowers early when few insects are around; hand-pollination is often worthwhile
Quince Self-fertile Reliable solo performer
Fig Self-fertile (in UK conditions) UK varieties do not need the specific wasp required in Mediterranean climates

These are general rules, not absolutes. Always check the specific variety you are planting, because exceptions exist within most species. Your local nursery or a fruit-tree specialist will know which varieties perform well in your climate.

How pollination groups work for apples and pears

For apples and pears, nurseries and reference guides assign varieties to pollination groups numbered roughly one to six (or sometimes labelled A to F), representing their typical flowering period from early to late in the season. Two varieties must overlap in flower for cross-pollination to work.

Adjacent groups will often overlap enough - a group 3 tree and a group 4 tree will usually have a week or two of simultaneous flowering. But a group 1 variety (early) and a group 6 variety (late) may not share a single day of open flowers in a given spring.

Planning note

When choosing two apple or pear varieties, aim for the same pollination group or adjacent groups. If you have room for only one apple but want fruit, look specifically for self-fertile varieties labelled as such by the nursery - not all self-described "easy" apples are actually self-fertile.

There is also the matter of triploid varieties - certain apples like 'Bramley's Seedling' and 'Blenheim Orange' have three sets of chromosomes rather than two. Their pollen is largely infertile, so they cannot act as pollinators for others. If you grow a triploid, you need two other compatible varieties nearby: one to pollinate the triploid, and one to pollinate that pollinator.

The role of insects - and what to do without them

Honeybees and bumblebees are the primary pollinators of most fruit trees in temperate gardens. Solitary bees - including mason bees and mining bees - are often even more effective per flower visit. If you are working on improving your fruit tree management and wondering why crops are light despite good pruning, the pollinator picture is worth examining.

Encouraging pollinators is mostly a matter of not discouraging them. Avoid spraying open flowers with any pesticide or insecticide - this is not just good practice, it is usually stated on product labels. Always read and follow the label for any product you use, and favour the least-toxic option available. Dandelions, early flowering shrubs, and wildflower patches near the orchard all help to support bee populations throughout the season.

For peaches and nectarines - which flower in late winter or early spring when few insects are active - many gardeners hand-pollinate by moving pollen between flowers with a soft artist's paintbrush or a cotton swab. It takes about ten minutes per tree and makes a real difference to fruitset.

Space constraints and clever solutions

Not every garden has room for two full-sized trees. There are a few options worth knowing.

Family trees are single rootstocks onto which two or three compatible varieties have been grafted. You get the diversity of multiple varieties in the footprint of one tree. The trade-off is that careful pruning is essential - if one variety is more vigorous than the others, it will eventually crowd them out. If you are brushing up on how pruning timing affects different woody plants, the same principles apply here: understand the growth habit before you cut.

Espaliers and cordons trained against a wall or fence are another way to fit two or more trees into a narrow space. A pair of cordon apples in compatible pollination groups can sit as little as 75cm apart.

And if a neighbour has a fruit tree of the same species, it may well be close enough to contribute pollen - bees routinely forage 500 metres or more. Worth a conversation over the fence.

Common mistakes people make when planning for pollination

Buying two trees of the same variety is perhaps the most frequent error. 'Cox's Orange Pippin' and another 'Cox's Orange Pippin' are genetically identical - they cannot cross-pollinate each other. The nursery label will often say "plant with a companion variety" without making clear that the companion must be a different variety.

Planting too far apart is another one. Most fruit tree pollen is carried by insects with limited range in domestic gardens. Trees more than around 50 metres apart may not reliably share pollination, though this depends on local bee populations. For small gardens, keeping partner trees within 15-20 metres of each other is a safer target.

Timing problems compound everything. Arriving at an allotment site in February feeling ambitious is a common scenario - and the temptation is to buy whatever trees are left in the shop rather than planned-for varieties. Poor winter decisions about orchard structure can follow gardeners for decades. On that note, watching out for the broader winter gardening mistakes that affect long-term garden health is worth the time.

Quick check before you buy

Before purchasing any fruit tree, confirm three things: (1) whether it is self-fertile or needs a partner, (2) its pollination group if it is an apple or pear, and (3) whether any tree already in your garden or a nearby neighbour's garden could serve as a compatible companion. A five-minute check at the nursery saves years of disappointment.

A patient approach to a long-lived plant

Fruit trees are not like annual vegetables - you are making a commitment that may last thirty, fifty, or a hundred years. The pollination decisions you make now will shape every harvest you and the people after you get from that ground. That is reason enough to get them right, not to rush.

Start small if you are new to this. Two compatible apple trees on a semi-dwarfing rootstock is a perfectly respectable beginning. Learn what they need in the first few years - how they respond to pruning, where they set fruit, what pests find them. Then expand the planting once you have a feel for the site. The orchard grows at its own pace, and patience is genuinely a skill here.

Can a crab apple pollinate a dessert apple?

Yes, in most cases. Crab apples flower prolifically and overlap with many dessert apple pollination groups, making them excellent pollinators. Varieties like 'John Downie' and 'Malus x robusta' are often planted in orchards specifically for this reason. Check that the flowering periods overlap with your chosen dessert variety.

My plum tree had a heavy crop last year and almost nothing this year. Is that a pollination problem?

Probably not, if it cropped well the year before. Plums are prone to biennial bearing - exhausting themselves with a large crop one year and resting the next. Thinning the fruit in heavy years and consistent pruning can help even out the cycle. If the tree has never cropped well, then pollination compatibility is worth investigating.

Do I need to worry about pollination for a cherry tree in a pot?

If it is a self-fertile variety like 'Stella' or 'Sunburst', a potted tree can fruit well on its own, provided it gets enough sun, water, and nutrients for the rootstock size. If it is a self-sterile sweet cherry variety, a partner tree must be within range - a pot on a balcony ten floors up may not have enough passing pollinators to do the job reliably, so self-fertile varieties are the sensible choice for container growing.

Pollination is one of those quiet foundations of an orchard that you only notice when something goes wrong. Get it right at the planning stage and it mostly looks after itself - season after season, tree after tree. That, for me, is the real pleasure of working with perennial plants. The thinking you do once pays you back for years.
