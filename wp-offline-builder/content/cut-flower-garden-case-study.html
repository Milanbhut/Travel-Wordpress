Forty-seven vases. That's how many times I filled something from my own cutting patch between late spring and the first hard frost. I kept a notebook - obsessively, some would say - and by the end of the season I had pages of scrawled bloom dates, vase-life tallies, and notes like "zinnias heroic, celosias mysteriously sulky." It was the most satisfying experiment I've run in years of gardening.

The patch itself was modest: a strip of south-facing ground roughly four metres by two, carved out of what used to be a struggling lawn. Not glamorous. Entirely ordinary soil with a bit of added compost. But by midsummer it was producing more flowers than I could use, and I was turning people away at the gate with armloads of dahlias.

If you've ever thought about setting up your own cutting garden but felt the scale seemed overwhelming, I want to share what actually happened - the numbers, the surprises, and the changes I'd make next time.

What I Planted (and Why)

I chose varieties based on three criteria: long stems, good vase life, and a reasonable spread of bloom times so I wouldn't have everything peaking at once. Getting that succession right takes some reading - knowing how bloom times layer through the season is genuinely the single most useful skill you can develop before you sow a single seed.

The lineup was: zinnias (Benary's Giant mix), cosmos (Purity and Dazzler), sweet peas (Spencer types for length), lisianthus, celosia, and dahlias from tubers. I also tucked in a short row of basil and mint - not for cutting, but because I'd been exploring growing herbs indoors over winter and wanted fresh material to compare.

Sweet peas went in earliest, direct-sown under a cloche in early spring. Everything else waited until after the last frost date for my zone. Dahlias came last, once the soil was genuinely warm - rushing them gains nothing.

The First Flush: What Came In Fast

Cosmos were first. About nine weeks from sowing, they erupted into a cloud of white and deep rose that lasted, with regular cutting, until October. Zinnias followed close behind - the first proper cut came around week eleven. Both are what I think of as "obliging" flowers: the more you cut, the more they give. I cannot stress this enough. Leaving blooms on the plant signals it to slow down. Keep harvesting.

Lisianthus, on the other hand, required patience I didn't know I had. They took nearly fourteen weeks from transplant to the first usable stem. When they came, though - deep purple ruffled blooms that lasted ten to twelve days in a vase - they were worth every slow week. I'd grow them again without hesitation.

Field note

Cut flowers in the early morning when stems are fully hydrated. Strip the lower leaves, plunge into water immediately, and let them condition in a cool spot for a few hours before arranging. This one habit added two to three days to nearly every variety's vase life in my trials.

The Dahlia Chapter

Dahlias are the drama queens of the cutting garden, and I mean that as a compliment. I grew four varieties from tubers - a café-au-lait, a deep burgundy ball type, a spiky cactus form, and a cheerful orange decorative. By August they were the backbone of every arrangement I made.

Vase life was around five to seven days, which is shorter than some flowers but more than acceptable given how spectacular they are. The trick is cutting them when the outermost petals are fully open but the centre still has some give. Too early and they won't open fully in the vase; too late and they're already declining.

Dahlia tubers are tender and won't survive a hard freeze in most climates. I dug mine in late autumn, dried them for a week, and stored them in barely-damp vermiculite in a cool garage. If you're in a very mild zone you may be able to mulch and leave them - worth checking your local extension service guidance rather than taking my word on that.

The One That Surprised Me Most

Celosia. I planted it half-heartedly, filling a gap, and it became one of my favourite cuts. The feathery plume types (I grew 'Fresh Look Orange') held their colour beautifully - not just in the vase but also when dried. A bunch hung upside-down in my shed stayed vibrant for months. If you're interested in growing flowers in a smaller footprint, growing flowers in containers works exceptionally well for celosia - a deep pot in a sunny spot produces excellent stems.

What I'd do differently: plant twice as many. I had one short row and it wasn't enough.

Mistakes I Made (So You Don't Have To)

Underestimating spacing

I crammed too many plants into the strip. Zinnias especially need airflow - poor circulation invited powdery mildew by late summer. I'd space them at least 30 cm apart next time, probably more. Healthy plants just above the soil line beat crowded ones by a wide margin.

Skipping the succession sowing

For cosmos and zinnias I sowed once and crossed my fingers. A second sowing three to four weeks later would have extended the season by a month. I know this. I keep forgetting to do it. Write it in your planting calendar now.

Forgetting about cut-and-come-again timing

In the first weeks I was precious about the flowers and didn't cut aggressively enough. The plants responded by slowing down. As soon as I started cutting hard - whole stems, not just faded blooms - production accelerated. Trust the process.

Safety note

Some popular cut flowers are toxic if eaten or if sap contacts eyes and skin. Sweet peas are mildly toxic if ingested; lisianthus and celosia are generally considered safe for humans but always keep cut flowers away from pets and children. If you suspect plant ingestion, contact your local poison control line or vet rather than guessing at a remedy.

What I'd Grow Again Without Question

Lisianthus, celosia, and dahlias. The effort-to-reward ratio on all three was exceptional. Cosmos earned a permanent spot purely on volume - one packet of seed produced hundreds of cuts. Zinnias are the workhorses; I'd never give up on them.

Sweet peas I'll keep too, even for their brief vase life. Walking past them in the morning with their scent drifting out across the garden is reason enough. Some things aren't only about the data.

How big does a cutting garden need to be to supply flowers for the house all summer?

A 3m x 1.5m strip is workable if you choose high-yield varieties like zinnias and cosmos. Staggered sowings and consistent cutting will keep a patch that size producing from early summer to frost. Bigger is better, but small and well-managed beats large and neglected.

Do I need to feed the plants during the season?

A general-purpose balanced fertiliser every few weeks supports steady growth, but avoid going heavy on nitrogen mid-season - it encourages lush foliage at the expense of blooms. Follow the product label for rates, and check your soil first if you can; overfed soil is a real thing.

Can I start a cut-flower patch if I only have pots and no ground space?

Absolutely. Zinnias, cosmos, and celosia all perform well in deep containers (at least 30 cm deep) in full sun. Dahlias work in large pots too - choose compact varieties bred for containers. The key is watering more frequently than you think you need to, since pots dry out fast.

A cutting garden won't look like much in March. By July you'll wonder how you ever lived without it. Start small, track what you grow, and let the notebook - or even just a back-of-packet note - guide what you change next year. That's the whole method, really, and it's a good one.
