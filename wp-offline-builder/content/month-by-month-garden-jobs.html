February is when I usually find myself standing in my allotment with a cold coffee, staring at bare beds and wondering where to start. I have a list somewhere. I always have a list. But somehow the list never quite maps to what the garden is actually doing right now. Sound familiar?

The gardening year doesn't run January through December the way a calendar does. It runs soil temperature, daylight, and rainfall. Once you stop thinking in months and start thinking in seasons - early spring, late summer, deep winter - the right jobs become obvious. The list writes itself.

This is how I broke it down on my own plot after a rather disastrous first-year garden experiment taught me that trying to do everything at once is the same as doing nothing. One season at a time. Roughly twelve checkpoints. Works a treat.

Early Spring: Wake the Plot Up Gently

Roughly late February through March in temperate zones (adjust a month earlier if you're several hardiness zones warmer). The soil is still cold. Don't rush it.

Your jobs here are mostly preparatory. Test your soil if you haven't recently - pH drifts over winter, especially if you've been adding organic matter. Top-dress beds with compost, but don't dig it in yet if the ground is waterlogged; walking on wet soil compacts it, and compacted soil is a problem that costs you all year.

  • Clear dead stems and leaves from beds (leave some for overwintering insects until temperatures are reliably above 10 °C)
  • Check stored tools for rust and sharpen blades
  • Start seeds indoors: tomatoes, peppers, aubergines - anything that needs a long head start
  • Prune autumn-fruiting raspberries to ground level
Timing tip

Soil thermometers cost almost nothing and tell you far more than the calendar. Most vegetable seeds won't germinate reliably below 7-10 °C. Check before sowing direct.

Mid-Spring: The Busiest Two Weeks of the Year

April in most temperate gardens is controlled chaos. Everything wants doing at once. This is when your earlier prep pays off - or when you discover you skipped it.

Start hardening off anything you started indoors. Don't rush the transition outside; a sudden cold night after a warm week can set seedlings back badly. An hour outside on day one, then gradually increase over ten days to two weeks. It feels slow. It isn't.

Direct sow fast crops: salad leaves, radishes, spinach, early beetroot. These tolerate cool soil and give you something to harvest while you wait for summer crops to establish. Get your drip irrigation system set up now, before the beds are full - threading lines through established plants later is a miserable job I've done twice and won't do again.

  • Plant onion sets and shallots
  • Divide congested perennial clumps (hostas, daylilies, geraniums)
  • Apply mulch to fruit trees and shrubs - keep it away from the trunk
  • Check apple and pear trees for overwintered pests before buds open

Late Spring Into Early Summer: Hold the Line on Pests

May and June are when your plants are most vulnerable. They're growing fast, which means pest populations are growing fast too. Aphids, slugs, cabbage white butterflies - they all show up roughly the same week everything else does.

Walk the garden daily if you can. Catching problems early means you deal with ten aphids instead of ten thousand. Hand-picking is unglamorous but genuinely effective. For soft-bodied pests, a strong jet of water from a hose dislodges most of them. Save chemical controls for genuine infestations, and always read and follow the product label - mixing or increasing doses doesn't help and can harm beneficial insects.

Fruit trees especially need attention now. Protecting fruit trees from pests and disease in late spring prevents the problems that show up at harvest - codling moth, woolly aphid, and scab are far easier to manage preventively than retroactively. Net brassicas before butterflies arrive, not after.

Safety note

Many pesticide products are harmful to bees and aquatic life. Apply in the early morning or evening when bees are less active, and never spray near water. For queries about specific products or accidental exposure, contact your local agricultural extension service or poison control line.

Midsummer: Maintain, Don't Overwork

July is the month I used to over-manage. I'd be out there every evening fiddling. The garden mostly wants water, feeding, and for you to get out of the way.

Water deeply and infrequently. Shallow daily watering trains roots upward. Deep watering twice a week drives roots down where they're more drought-resistant. If you set up that drip system in April, you're already ahead - just check emitters haven't clogged.

Keep on top of succession sowing: sow more salad leaves, dwarf beans, and quick turnips every three weeks so you're not drowning in lettuce for two days and then nothing. Deadhead flowering plants regularly to extend bloom time. Cut back the first flush of hardy geraniums completely - they'll come back fresh.

Feeding in Midsummer

Tomatoes, courgettes, and peppers in containers need feeding weekly once they're flowering. A high-potassium liquid feed (the kind marketed as tomato feed) suits fruiting crops. Follow the label rates - overfeeding can cause leafy, unproductive plants. Don't guess.

Harvesting on Time

Courgettes become marrows in about three days if you stop looking. Check daily. A courgette plant that's left to produce giant fruits stops producing new ones. Harvest smaller and more often. Same rule applies to beans and cucumbers.

Late Summer and Early Autumn: Bank the Harvest, Plant for Winter

August through September is a strange overlap: you're still harvesting summer crops while simultaneously sowing for winter. It feels contradictory but it's just how it works.

Clear finished crops promptly. A rotting courgette plant is a slug hotel. Compost healthy material; bin or burn anything that shows signs of disease - potato blight residue left in the bed re-infects next year.

Sow hardy winter salads, spinach, land cress, and overwintering onions now. Plant garlic from mid-autumn onward - it needs a cold period to develop properly. Order spring bulbs. Yes, already. Good varieties sell out earlier than you'd think.

  • Dry and store onions and garlic in a cool, dry, ventilated spot
  • Save seeds from squashes, beans, and tomatoes (let them dry fully before storing)
  • Take cuttings from tender perennials: pelargoniums, fuchsias, salvias
  • Plant strawberry runners for next year's crop

Winter: The Season That Actually Matters Most

Most gardeners treat winter as a gap between real gardening. I used to. Then I realised that what you do (or don't do) between November and February is the difference between a season that runs and one that lurches.

This is planning time. Review what worked this year and what didn't. Rotate your crops on paper before you move anything. Order seeds before February - the interesting varieties go fast, and being forced to buy whatever's left at the garden centre in March is a form of punishment.

Protect vulnerable plants before hard frosts arrive rather than scrambling after the forecast. Fleece, straw mulch, and cold frames extend the season significantly. Pot-grown plants are more frost-vulnerable than their in-ground counterparts - move pots against a sheltered wall or into an unheated greenhouse.

Winter tool care

Clean and oil metal tools before storing for winter. A wire brush, a wipe-down with a lightly oiled rag, and dry storage keeps tools lasting decades. Blunt tools make every task harder - sharpen spades and hoes now so you're ready come spring.

The Jobs That Run All Year

Some tasks don't belong to one season. They run as a background process regardless of the month.

Weeding is the obvious one. Little and often beats once-a-month marathons every time. Annual weeds pulled before they set seed are weeds you won't have next year. Perennial weeds like bindweed and couch grass need their roots removed, not just the tops - cutting them back feeds the roots and makes them stronger.

Composting runs year-round. A healthy compost pile wants roughly equal parts green (grass clippings, kitchen veg scraps, fresh weeds) and brown (cardboard, woody prunings, dried leaves). Turn it when you think of it. It doesn't need to be complicated.

  • Keep a garden notebook - what you planted, when, and how it performed
  • Check for pests and disease on every visit, not just scheduled ones
  • Top up mulch as it breaks down
  • Water newly planted trees and shrubs in their first two years, even in winter during dry spells

When should I start sowing vegetable seeds?

It depends on the crop and your climate zone, but most tender crops like tomatoes and peppers benefit from an indoor start six to eight weeks before your last expected frost. Hardy crops like broad beans and peas can go direct much earlier. A soil thermometer is more reliable than any calendar - aim for at least 7 °C for most vegetable seeds.

Can I do all the prep in one weekend instead of spreading jobs through the year?

Some jobs are genuinely flexible and can be batched. Others are time-sensitive - you can't plant garlic in spring and expect a crop, and you can't harden off seedlings faster than the plant will tolerate. A useful rule: plan and prep jobs (ordering seeds, sharpening tools, making compost) are flexible. Sowing and planting windows are not.

My garden skips seasons - very mild winters, very hot summers. Does this framework still apply?

The framework is seasonal, not calendar-based, so it stretches. In mild climates, "winter prep" might happen in late autumn; in hot climates, your main growing windows may shift to spring and autumn. Local extension services and gardening groups for your specific region are genuinely the best resource for timing - they've tested what works in conditions like yours.

The gardening year rewards people who think one season ahead. Get that right and you stop firefighting and start actually enjoying the plot. My notebook from year one is mostly a record of things I forgot to do; my notebook from last year is mostly a record of harvests. That's the whole trick, really.
