Last summer, a reader sent me a photo of her tomato plant with a subject line that read: "Is this normal?" The leaves had small, dark brown rings - almost bullseye-shaped - surrounded by a yellowing halo. She'd been told it was a nutrient deficiency. She'd been fertilising for weeks, and the plant was getting worse.

It wasn't a nutrient problem. It was early blight, a fungal disease, and the extra feeding was making things worse by pushing lush new growth that the pathogen could easily colonise. Once we identified it correctly, she stripped the affected leaves, improved airflow, and applied a copper-based spray following the label instructions. The plant recovered well enough to give her a decent harvest.

That story captures why diagnosis matters. A plant showing distress is telling you something - but the same symptom can come from half a dozen different causes. Learning to read the signs carefully, rather than guessing, is the single most useful skill you can build as a gardener.

Start with the Leaf, Not the Label

When something looks wrong, the first instinct is often to reach for a product. Resist that. Walk over to the plant, get low, and actually look. Where exactly is the problem showing up? The very tips of leaves? The centres? Along the margins? Is it spreading upward from older lower leaves, or appearing first on new growth?

Location is already useful information. Many fungal diseases start on older, lower foliage and move upward as spores spread. Nutrient problems, by contrast, often appear first on new growth (for mobile nutrients like calcium) or older growth (for mobile ones like nitrogen). Even before you identify the specific pathogen, location narrows your list considerably.

Also check the underside of leaves. Spider mites leave tiny speckling on top but cluster invisibly below. Powdery mildew sometimes starts underneath before it's obvious on top. Aphids and scale insects cluster on leaf undersides too. A hand lens or even a phone camera macro lens helps enormously here.

Reading Spots: What Colour and Shape Tell You

Leaf spots are probably the most common symptom gardeners bring me, and they're genuinely variable. Colour, shape, edge definition, and any concentric rings all point in different directions.

Fungal spots

Most fungal leaf spots have defined, often circular or angular edges. They may have a darker border and a lighter centre, or show those concentric rings I described above. Look for tiny black dots in the lesion - these are fungal fruiting bodies, called pycnidia, and they're a very reliable sign of a fungal pathogen. Black spot on roses and septoria leaf spot on tomatoes both behave this way.

Bacterial spots

Bacterial lesions tend to look more water-soaked initially - almost translucent when you hold the leaf up to light. They often spread to angular shapes because they're bounded by leaf veins. If you see lesions that look greasy or water-soaked and then turn brown with a yellow halo, bacterial blight or speck is a good candidate.

Viral mosaic patterns

Viruses rarely cause discrete spots. Instead, they create irregular patterns - mosaic-like patches of light and dark green, sometimes with puckering or distorted leaf shape. Mosaic virus on cucumbers and peppers is the classic example. There's no chemical treatment; infected plants should be removed and not composted.

Quick rule of thumb

Spots with defined edges and tiny black dots = likely fungal. Water-soaked angular patches = likely bacterial. Mosaic discolouration with leaf distortion = likely viral. Each has a different management approach, so getting this right early saves a lot of effort.

Wilting: When It's Not Just About Water

A wilted plant on a hot afternoon is often just thirsty. But wilting that persists after watering, or that shows up in the morning before heat builds, is a different signal entirely. This is where I always ask: did the plant wilt suddenly, or gradually?

Sudden, dramatic wilting in otherwise healthy-looking plants often points to a vascular pathogen. Fusarium wilt and verticillium wilt both colonise the plant's water-conducting tissue. If you cut the stem near the base and see brown or tan discolouration in the vascular ring just inside the outer layer, that's strong evidence of a wilt disease. These are soil-borne, so crop rotation - growing that plant family in a different bed for three or more seasons - is the most reliable long-term fix.

Gradual wilting with yellowing, especially if the roots look brown and mushy when you pull the plant, points to root rot from overwatering or a water mould like Phytophthora. This is common in containers and heavy clay soils. The best starting point is improving drainage; if you're regularly building life back into compacted or waterlogged soil, you'll have far fewer root rot problems in the first place.

Powdery and Downy: Two Moulds Worth Telling Apart

White or grey fuzzy coatings on leaf surfaces trip up a lot of gardeners, because powdery mildew and downy mildew look superficially similar but behave very differently.

Powdery mildew sits on the surface of the leaf - you can almost rub it off with your thumb. It tends to coat the tops of leaves and thrives in warm days with cool nights and dry air. Squash, courgettes, and cucumbers are particularly susceptible. Good airflow is your best preventive, and for established infections, a dilute bicarbonate spray or a sulphur-based product (always follow the label) can slow spread.

Downy mildew is more sinister. It lives inside the leaf tissue, not just on the surface. You'll see yellow patches on top, with a grey-purple fuzzy growth on the underside only. It loves cool, wet conditions - the opposite of powdery mildew. Infected leaves deteriorate quickly. Remove them, avoid overhead watering, and check whether your plant variety has any resistance before next season.

Rust, Cankers, and the Ones That Are Easy to Miss

Rust diseases produce orange, yellow, or rust-red pustules - almost always on leaf undersides. They're striking once you know what you're looking at. Leek rust, rose rust, and hollyhock rust are the ones I see most often. Rust spreads via windborne spores, so infected debris left over winter is the primary source the following spring. Clear it up thoroughly and keep the bed well-ventilated.

Cankers show up on woody stems rather than leaves - sunken, dead patches of bark, often girdling a branch. They're common on fruit trees and can be bacterial or fungal in origin. If you're growing an edible garden for the first time and including fruit trees or woody herbs, learn to spot canker early. Prune affected wood back to clean tissue (cut well below the discolouration), sterilise your tools between cuts, and dispose of the material - don't compost it.

Tool hygiene tip

A simple dip in diluted bleach solution (one part bleach to nine parts water) or isopropyl alcohol between cuts prevents you from spreading disease yourself. It's easy to skip when you're busy pruning, but it costs almost nothing and matters a great deal with bacterial and fungal cankers.

Building a Preventive Mindset

Most plant diseases thrive under predictable conditions: poor airflow, stressed plants, wet foliage overnight, or depleted soil. Address those conditions and you'll dramatically reduce how often you need to identify diseases at all.

Healthy soil is the starting point. Plants growing in biologically rich soil have better natural defences - they're less stressed, better nourished, and less prone to the kind of lesions that let pathogens in. Feeding plants with natural, slow-release inputs rather than synthetic forcing fertilisers supports that kind of long-term resilience. I've noticed over the years that my beds with the most organic matter are simply the ones with the fewest disease problems.

Space your plants properly. Many seed packets understate how much room a plant really needs for good airflow. Crowded plants are warm and humid in the canopy - perfect for mildews and blights. Water at the base, not overhead, and aim to water in the morning so foliage dries before evening. These aren't dramatic interventions. They're small habits that add up.

Can I compost leaves from diseased plants?

In most cases, no - not in a standard home compost heap. Fungal spores and some bacterial pathogens can survive composting that doesn't reach high enough temperatures. Bag and bin diseased material, or burn it if local regulations allow. Healthy leaves and trimmings are fine to compost as usual.

My plant has spots but otherwise looks healthy - do I need to treat it?

Not always immediately. A few spots on an otherwise vigorous plant can often be managed by removing the affected leaves and improving conditions - better airflow, drier foliage overnight. Monitor closely. If the problem is spreading quickly or the plant is looking stressed, identify the specific disease before reaching for any product, and always follow the label if you do use a spray.

How do I know if it's a disease or a pest causing the damage?

Look for the pest itself - check leaf undersides, stems, and the soil surface. Pest damage tends to look chewed or stippled rather than spotted or discoloured from within the tissue. Disease lesions usually start as distinct spots or patches that expand and change colour. If you're genuinely unsure, your local cooperative extension service or plant clinic can identify from photos or a physical sample.

Diagnosing plant diseases gets easier with practice, and a keen eye is really all you need to start. Take time to observe before you act, look at the whole picture - location, pattern, timing, and conditions - and you'll find that most problems come with a clear trail of clues. Your plants are communicating. The more fluent you get at reading them, the more confidently you'll garden.
