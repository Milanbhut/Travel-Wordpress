My neighbour Mike once brought over a cardboard box stuffed with twenty heads of romaine lettuce. All of them cut on the same afternoon. He'd sown the whole packet on one day in April, everything matured together, and now he had far more salad than any household could reasonably eat in a week. The rest went to compost.

It's one of the most common stories I hear from new growers. And it's almost never about laziness - it's simply that nobody explained when to sow, not just what to sow. That one missing piece of information turns a productive kitchen garden into a feast-or-famine cycle.

Succession planting is the fix. It's less a technique and more a mindset: instead of sowing everything at once, you spread your sowings across several weeks so crops mature at intervals rather than in one overwhelming wave. Once it clicks, you'll wonder how you ever grew any other way.

What Succession Planting Actually Means

The term gets used loosely, so it's worth being precise. Succession planting means making repeated sowings of the same crop at regular intervals - typically every two to four weeks - so you get a rolling harvest rather than a single large one.

It's different from companion planting or crop rotation, both of which deal with what grows where. Succession planting is purely about timing. You're essentially dividing one large sowing into several smaller ones, spread across the season.

Some gardeners also use the phrase to mean following one finished crop with a different one - pulling out spent spring peas and planting autumn kale in the same bed. That's a related idea, and a good one, but it's technically a follow-on crop rather than a true succession.

Which Crops Respond Best

Fast-maturing, leaf-and-stem crops are the best candidates. Lettuce, radishes, spinach, salad greens, spring onions, coriander, dill, and beetroot all mature in roughly six to eight weeks from sowing. Sow a short row every three weeks and you'll be harvesting something almost constantly from late spring through early autumn.

Beans - French beans especially - also suit succession brilliantly. I stagger three or four sowings of dwarf French beans about three weeks apart, starting after the last frost date for my area. The first planting is usually exhausted by the time the second is peaking.

Crops that take a long time to mature, like winter squash, celeriac, or Brussels sprouts, generally don't benefit much from succession sowing. The season simply isn't long enough to get two or three harvests, and the earlier sowing almost always outperforms later ones anyway.

How to Work Out Your Intervals

The interval between sowings depends on how quickly a crop matures and how fast you eat it. A family that goes through two lettuce heads a week needs shorter gaps than someone cooking just for themselves.

Here's a simple way to think about it. Check the days-to-maturity on your seed packet - that's usually the figure from transplant to harvest, so add two to three weeks if you're sowing direct. Then divide that number by the number of harvests you want. If lettuce takes around 45 days from transplant and you want a harvest roughly every three weeks, one new tray every 21 days is about right.

You'll tune it over a season or two. Your first year is data collection as much as gardening. Make notes on when you sowed, when you picked, and whether you had too much or too little - that record is worth more than any general guide.

Accounting for Temperature Swings

Crops grow faster in warm weather and slower in cool weather, which means a three-week interval in June can translate to a five-week interval in September. Build some flex into your plan. I tend to tighten my intervals slightly in peak summer and space them a little wider heading into autumn.

Bolt-prone crops like lettuce and spinach also shift character mid-season. If you're growing heat-sensitive herbs or greens and your summers get fierce, consider switching to bolt-resistant varieties for the midsummer sowings and returning to your preferred ones in late summer when temperatures ease.

A Practical Sowing Calendar

You don't need a complicated spreadsheet. A simple notebook page with columns - crop, sow date, expected harvest date, notes - is enough to keep things organised. Some people use sticky notes on a wall calendar. Whatever you'll actually maintain.

The key habit is setting a reminder to sow. It sounds obvious, but the gap between "I'll do another row of radishes soon" and actually doing it is where most succession plans unravel. I put a recurring note in my phone every three weeks from April through August. It takes about ten minutes to sow a short row, and those ten minutes keep the kitchen stocked.

If you're also growing tomatoes in containers or any crop that takes up significant bench space in a greenhouse or under lights, block those weeks out in your calendar so succession sowings don't compete for propagation space at peak times.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

The most frequent error is starting with too many crops at once. Pick two or three to succession-sow in your first season and do those well. Trying to orchestrate lettuce, beans, brassicas, spinach, and radishes simultaneously before you have the rhythm down usually ends in chaos - or at least a lot of confused seedlings on the windowsill.

The second mistake is ignoring variety choice. Some lettuce varieties are bred for heat tolerance, others for overwintering, others for speed. Using a single variety across your entire succession means it will all bolt or struggle at the same point in the season. Mixing a fast cut-and-come-again with a slower heading type gives you built-in variety across the same interval.

Third: not thinning properly. When you're sowing every three weeks, it's tempting to skip thinning because "more is fine." It isn't. Crowded plants compete for light and water, mature unevenly, and give you a worse harvest than fewer, well-spaced plants would.

One bed, three waves

If space is tight, divide a single raised bed into thirds. Sow the first third in week one, the second in week three, the third in week five. You harvest the first third, improve the soil with a handful of compost, and re-sow it - a neat rotating system that fits almost any size garden.

Feeding the Soil Between Sowings

Succession planting asks a lot of your soil. Repeated cropping in the same space draws down nutrients steadily, and you can end up with later sowings performing poorly not because of timing but because the soil is tired.

Between each round of sowing, I work in a small amount of well-rotted compost and occasionally a liquid feed. There's good detail on natural plant feeding methods elsewhere on this site - the short version is that compost tea, worm castings, or a balanced organic granular feed applied lightly between successions keeps the bed productive without overcooking it.

Avoid heavy nitrogen inputs right before sowing leafy crops - you'll get lush, soft growth that's more prone to pest damage. A balanced approach almost always outperforms a "more is more" one.

Making It Work in a Small Space

Succession planting is, if anything, more useful in small gardens than large ones. A glut of forty lettuces is just as wasteful in a tiny raised bed as in a large allotment plot. Proportionally smaller, but the same problem.

In confined spaces, favour cut-and-come-again varieties over heading types. A row of loose-leaf lettuce that you harvest outer leaves from will outperform three weeks of heading lettuce in terms of total harvest from the same footprint. Pair this with vertical growing wherever you can - beans on a trellis, cucumbers trained upward - to free up ground for your succession rows.

Container gardeners can absolutely run a succession system too. Pot up one container, then a second three weeks later. Radishes and spring onions are particularly good for this: fast, compact, and reliably tasty.

How many sowings should I make for each crop?

For a typical household, three to four sowings of a fast crop like lettuce or radishes usually covers the season well. Start after your last expected frost, make a sowing every two to three weeks, and stop around eight weeks before your first expected autumn frost so the final batch has time to mature.

Can I succession-sow indoors under grow lights?

Yes, and it works very well. The controlled environment means you're not fighting weather variation, so your intervals stay predictable. Just make sure you have enough light for multiple trays at different stages - seedlings from earlier sowings will still be occupying space when later ones go in.

What do I do with a glut if one sowing still produces too much?

Harvest promptly and refrigerate - most salad crops will keep for several days. Beyond that, neighbours, local food banks, and community groups are usually glad of the excess. If you find one sowing consistently overproduces, simply reduce your row length or pot size for that interval next season.

The beauty of succession planting is that it rewards small, consistent efforts over big occasional ones. A ten-minute sowing every few weeks keeps the kitchen stocked steadily, turns gardening into a reliable habit rather than an anxious scramble, and makes the most of whatever space you have. Try it with just one crop this season - lettuce is the obvious place to start - and see whether that steady rhythm suits you.
