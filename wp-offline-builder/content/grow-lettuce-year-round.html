My salad bowl sat empty for three weeks last August, and I had no one to blame but myself. I had sown one big batch of lettuce in May, harvested it all by mid-July, and then done nothing. No follow-up sowing, no plan. Just optimism and a gap you could drive a tractor through.

The fix is not complicated. It is called succession sowing, and once you build the habit into your gardening calendar, the gaps disappear. The idea is simple: instead of sowing a full packet at once, you sow small amounts every two to three weeks so the harvests stagger rather than collide.

What makes lettuce a genuinely year-round crop is that different varieties behave differently in heat, cold, and low light. Get the variety choices right alongside the timing, and you can be cutting fresh leaves in January just as easily as June.

Why Lettuce Bolts - and How Knowing This Changes Everything

Before we get into timing, it helps to understand bolting. When days lengthen and temperatures climb above roughly 75°F (24°C), lettuce reads the signals as a cue to flower and set seed. The leaves turn bitter almost overnight. This is not a failure on your part; it is the plant doing exactly what evolution shaped it to do.

Heat-tolerant varieties like 'Jericho' and 'Sierra' have been bred to delay that response. They will still bolt eventually, but they buy you several extra weeks in summer. In contrast, butterhead types like 'Tom Thumb' or 'Buttercrunch' are silky and sweet in spring and autumn but will turn on you fast once the mercury rises.

Understanding this means you stop fighting the wrong battle. You do not try to grow a butterhead through a heatwave. You choose a heat-tolerant loose-leaf for summer, switch back to butterheads in September, and let each variety do what it does best.

Building Your Succession Sowing Calendar

The most reliable approach I have found is to sow a short row - or a single module tray of 12 cells - every two to three weeks. That small amount produces enough for two people to eat salad three or four times before the next batch comes in.

Here is a rough seasonal rhythm:

  • Late winter (indoors or under cover): Start a cold-tolerant variety like 'Winter Density' or 'Arctic King' in a heated propagator or on a warm windowsill. Germination needs soil temperatures of at least 40°F (5°C), and 65-70°F (18-21°C) is ideal.
  • Spring (outdoors from last frost): Sow direct every three weeks. Cool, moist soil suits almost any variety. This is the easiest season.
  • Early summer: Switch to bolt-resistant types. Shade cloth rated 30-40% light reduction can add another two to three weeks to your harvest window during hot spells.
  • Late summer: Sow again from mid-August onward. Soil temperature drops below the germination sweet spot earlier than you expect, so starting seeds indoors and transplanting out gives you a head start.
  • Autumn through winter: Use a cold frame, low polytunnel, or row-cover fleece to keep plants productive well below freezing. 'Valdor' and 'Rouge d'Hiver' will sit happily at 28°F (-2°C) under cover.
Tip: Mark your calendar, not just your seed packets

Set a recurring reminder every 18 days to ask yourself: "Have I sown lettuce recently?" If the answer is no, take five minutes and sow a tray. Consistent small actions beat one big planting session every time.

Choosing Varieties for Each Season

I keep a short list on the inside of the shed door. Four categories, always stocked:

  1. Spring/Autumn workhorses: 'Buttercrunch', 'Little Gem', 'Forellenschluss' (Flashy Trout's Back). These are the ones you want when conditions are kind.
  2. Summer bolt-resisters: 'Jericho', 'Nevada', 'Black Seeded Simpson'. Loose-leaf types generally outlast heads in heat.
  3. Winter varieties: 'Arctic King', 'Winter Density', 'Rouge d'Hiver'. Slower growing, tougher cells, designed for short days.
  4. Cut-and-come-again mixes: Mesclun or oak-leaf blends that you harvest at the seedling stage and that resprout two or three times. Perfect for filling gaps.

Pairing cut-and-come-again sowings with your heading varieties means you always have something to cut even when a succession falls slightly behind. Think of them as your salad insurance policy.

Getting the Most from Small Spaces

You do not need raised beds and a market garden to do this well. I have grown year-round lettuce in window boxes, in the gaps between brassicas, and in a single half-barrel on a north-facing patio. The key is thinking in layers of time rather than blocks of space.

One approach I particularly like is interplanting lettuce with slower-growing crops. By the time your tomatoes or squash shade out the ground, the lettuce is already finished. If you are interested in how this works more broadly, the principles behind companion planting in the vegetable garden apply here directly - fast crops fill the gaps while deep-rooted or climbing plants use vertical space.

For gardeners working in containers, lettuce is one of the most forgiving crops you can grow. It shares space generously and has shallow roots. If you have had success growing potatoes in bags, the container mindset translates perfectly - manage your watering carefully, feed every two weeks with a balanced liquid fertiliser, and the crop will deliver even in confined spaces.

Watering - the Most Common Point of Failure

Lettuce is roughly 95% water by weight, which tells you something important about what it needs from you. Inconsistent watering is probably the single biggest reason home-grown lettuce disappoints. Plants that dry out and then get drenched develop tip burn (brown leaf edges) and are more prone to bolting in warm weather.

The goal is evenly moist soil - not wet, not bone dry. I check with my finger an inch below the surface rather than relying on how the top looks. Sandy soils drain fast and need more frequent attention; clay holds water longer but can suffocate roots if it becomes waterlogged. Mulching with straw or compost around the plants helps buffer both extremes.

It is worth reading through the most common watering mistakes if you have had trouble with lettuce before - overwatering is genuinely as damaging as underwatering, and the symptoms can look similar at first glance.

Watch out for slugs under cover

Cold frames and polytunnels create ideal slug habitat: damp, sheltered, and full of tender leaves. Check under pots and along the edges of frames every few days, especially after rain. Physical barriers like copper tape around container rims help. If you use any slug control product, follow the product label instructions and use the minimum effective amount - some products are harmful to wildlife and pets.

Harvesting to Keep Plants Producing

How you harvest matters almost as much as how you sow. For heading types, cut the whole head at the base once it feels firm and full. For loose-leaf and cut-and-come-again varieties, take the outer leaves first and leave the central growing point untouched - the plant will push new growth within a week or two.

Harvest in the morning when leaves are fully hydrated. They store better and taste sweeter than leaves cut in afternoon heat. Rinse gently, spin dry, and keep in a sealed bag in the fridge. Home-grown lettuce typically lasts three to five days this way - less than a supermarket bag, but the flavour difference makes up for it.

Once a plant starts to elongate in the centre - the first sign of bolting - cut the whole thing and use it right away. Waiting even a day or two after visible bolting begins can make the leaves unpalatable.

Simple Troubleshooting

Seedlings that Leggy and Pale

Almost always a light problem, especially with indoor winter starts. Move them to a brighter windowsill or add a grow light set 4-6 inches above the tray for 12-14 hours a day. Thin seedlings to one per cell so they are not competing for what light there is.

Leaves with Brown Edges

Tip burn is usually a calcium uptake issue caused by irregular watering or poor air circulation. It is common under cover in warm weather. Improve ventilation, water more consistently, and the next flush of leaves should come in cleaner.

Germination Failure in Summer

Lettuce seed goes dormant above about 77°F (25°C) in the soil - a mechanism called thermoinhibition. Sow in the cool of the evening, cover the seed tray with a damp cloth overnight, and move it somewhere shaded and cool. Or simply pre-germinate seeds on a damp paper towel in the fridge for 24-48 hours before sowing.

Can I grow lettuce indoors on a windowsill all year?

Yes, with some caveats. A south-facing windowsill in summer works well. In winter, most windows do not deliver enough light for strong growth without a supplemental grow light. Cut-and-come-again mixes at the seedling stage are your best bet indoors - they need less light than heading types and produce edible leaves quickly.

How cold is too cold for lettuce?

Most lettuce handles a light frost (28-32°F / -2 to 0°C) without damage, especially hardy varieties like 'Winter Density' or 'Arctic King'. Hard freezes below about 25°F (-4°C) will damage unprotected plants. Under a layer of row-cover fleece, many varieties survive considerably colder temperatures - the fleece adds roughly 4-6°F of protection on still nights.

Is lettuce safe around pets?

Yes - lettuce is non-toxic to dogs, cats, and most common household pets. The small amounts of lactucarium (the milky sap) in bolted plants are mildly sedating in very large quantities but pose no practical risk from casual nibbling. If you are ever concerned that a pet has eaten something in the garden and is showing unusual symptoms, contact your vet promptly rather than waiting to see how things develop.

A continuous lettuce harvest is less about doing something clever and more about doing something small on a regular schedule. Two to three weeks, a short row, the right variety for the season. Do that through the year and the salad bowl stays full - no three-week gaps, no empty fridge, no blaming yourself while standing in a bare bed in August.
