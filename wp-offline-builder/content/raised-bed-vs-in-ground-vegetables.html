A few years ago I stood in a friend's backyard staring at two very different vegetable plots side by side. One was a tidy cedar raised bed, soil dark and loose, draining perfectly after the previous night's rain. The other was a long in-ground row, still waterlogged, with that grey crust forming on the surface. Her comment: "I built the bed last spring and I'm never going back."

I've heard some version of that story dozens of times. And I've also heard the opposite - gardeners who tried raised beds, found the cost and watering demands frustrating, and happily returned to tilling their native soil. Both approaches grow excellent vegetables. The question is which one suits your particular patch, budget, and energy level.

Let's look at this honestly, side by side, so you can make the call with clear eyes rather than following a trend.

The Core Difference in How Plants Experience Each System

In a raised bed, you control the soil entirely. You fill it with a blend you choose - typically a mix of topsoil, compost, and sometimes perlite or aged bark. Roots never have to fight through compacted native ground. Drainage is built in from the start.

In-ground growing means working with what's already there. That's a disadvantage if your native soil is heavy clay or thin sand. It's a genuine advantage if you're gardening on deep, loamy ground, because you're essentially getting a ready-made growing medium for free.

Neither system is universally better. Context is everything.

Cost: What You Actually Spend

Raised beds have real upfront costs. A single 4×8-foot cedar frame can run $60-$150 for materials, more if you hire it built. Then you need to fill it. A bed that size holds roughly 12-16 cubic feet of soil mix, and quality blends are not cheap - budget another $50-$100 depending on your supplier.

In-ground rows cost almost nothing to start. A spade, some compost worked in, and you're growing. Over years, you'll add amendments, but the soil itself is already there.

That said, raised beds often pay back their cost in reduced weeding time, fewer pest problems at soil level, and the ability to extend the season earlier in spring (raised soil warms faster). Whether that trade-off pencils out depends on how you value your time.

Tip

If budget is tight, start with a single raised bed in your sunniest spot for heat-lovers like tomatoes and peppers - crops that really benefit from warm, loose soil - and use in-ground rows for hardier greens and root veg.

Drainage and Soil Health Over Time

This is where raised beds shine most clearly. Because the growing mix sits above grade, water drains freely and roots get oxygen. Heavy rainfall that would drown an in-ground planting rarely causes trouble in a well-built raised bed.

In-ground plots can be dramatically improved with persistent effort. Double-digging, adding generous organic matter each season, and avoiding compaction by never stepping on planting rows all help. But if your native soil has a hardpan layer or serious drainage issues, you're fighting an uphill battle. For those situations, raised beds are not just convenient - they're the practical choice.

If your soil is already decent, in-ground growing keeps getting better year after year as organic matter builds. A well-tended in-ground plot with consistent mulching to protect and feed the soil can be extraordinarily productive with less input than a raised bed requires.

Watering: The Hidden Raised-Bed Trade-Off

Here's what the raised-bed enthusiasts sometimes skip: those beds dry out faster. Because drainage is excellent and the soil is often lighter in texture, raised beds can need watering every day in midsummer, sometimes twice if the weather is fierce. In-ground soil holds moisture longer, especially if it has clay content.

I've watched gardeners build raised beds expecting less work, then spend more time at the hose than they ever did before. Adding drip irrigation helps enormously, but that's another cost to factor in.

In-ground rows in reasonable soil, covered with a thick layer of straw or wood-chip mulch, can go several days between watering even in summer heat. This matters if you travel or simply have limited time.

Pest and Weed Pressure

Raised beds sit above common soil-dwelling pests and can be more easily protected with hardware cloth on the bottom against voles and moles. Weeds are fewer in the early years because you filled the bed with weed-seed-free mix.

In-ground rows, especially in established garden plots, tend to accumulate weed seeds over time. Perennial weeds that spread by root - bindweed, ground elder, couch grass - are hard to control once established, and an in-ground plot gives them a large, connected territory to exploit.

That said, raised beds are not magic weed barriers. Wind deposits seeds, birds drop them, and after a few seasons a neglected raised bed can be just as weedy as any in-ground row. The real advantage is in the early years of a new garden or on previously weedy ground.

What Grows Best Where

Some crops genuinely prefer one system over the other.

Crops that thrive in raised beds

  • Tomatoes and peppers - benefit from warm, well-drained soil; tomato stress issues are often traced back to waterlogged roots, something raised beds help avoid
  • Root vegetables like carrots and parsnips - need deep, stone-free, loose soil that's hard to achieve in heavy ground
  • Salad greens - especially for intensive planting in small spaces

Crops that do fine in-ground (or better)

  • Squash, pumpkins, and melons - spread too wide for most raised beds and are vigorous enough to do well in decent native soil
  • Sweetcorn - needs to be planted in blocks for pollination, and those blocks take up a lot of space
  • Potatoes - often grown in long in-ground rows or purpose-built mounds; earthing up is easier at ground level
  • Lettuce and cut-and-come-again greens - if you want to grow lettuce across multiple seasons, in-ground beds with protective fleece work well and don't require the setup costs

Which should you choose?

If your native soil drains poorly, you have limited space, or mobility is a concern, start with at least one raised bed. If you have reasonable soil, plenty of room, and want to grow large-space crops economically, in-ground rows with good organic matter management will serve you well. Many experienced growers use both.

How deep should a raised bed be for vegetables?

Most vegetables do well in a bed that is 30-45 cm (12-18 inches) deep. Root crops like carrots and parsnips benefit from the full 45 cm. If you're placing a bed over concrete or compacted ground with no contact to native soil, aim for at least 40 cm so roots have room and moisture doesn't pool at the base.

Can I convert my in-ground beds to raised beds gradually?

Yes, and it's a sensible approach. Many gardeners add one raised bed each year, focusing on whichever crops benefit most from the improved conditions. You don't have to commit fully to one method - a mixed plot is practical and gives you flexibility as you learn what works best in your specific conditions.

What wood is safest for raised beds near food crops?

Untreated cedar and redwood are the traditional choices - they're naturally rot-resistant and contain no chemicals that could leach into soil. Avoid old railway sleepers or any timber treated with creosote or older preservatives, as these can contaminate soil. If you use modern pressure-treated lumber, choose products certified safe for food-growing and check local guidance, as standards vary by region.

The honest answer is that most productive kitchen gardens end up using both methods over time - raised beds for crops that need perfect drainage and warmth, in-ground rows for everything that just needs space and decent soil. Start small, observe how your ground behaves in wet and dry spells, and let your own plot tell you what it needs. Good vegetables grow in both systems when the gardener pays attention.
