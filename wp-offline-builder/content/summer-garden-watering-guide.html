Last July, I checked my courgettes at 7 a.m. and they were fine. By noon, every leaf had gone limp - the kind of dramatic wilt that looks like the plant is auditioning for a tragedy. I watered. They recovered. But the tomatoes two rows over, which I had also watered, promptly developed blossom-end rot because the soil was already wet enough and I had just made things worse.

Hot, dry summers expose every gap in a watering routine. Too little and plants stress, stop fruiting, and bolt. Too much at the wrong time and you breed fungal problems, attract pests, and flush nutrients straight through the soil. The good news is that a handful of deliberate habits - timed right and layered together - can carry a garden through weeks of heat with less water than you might think.

I have run these tactics on my own plot for three summers now, adjusting each year. What follows is the version that works. Your soil, climate, and plant mix will differ, so treat this as a framework rather than a script, and adjust as you go.

Step 1: Time Your Watering Right

Water in the early morning - ideally before 9 a.m. This gives foliage time to dry before the day's heat peaks, which cuts fungal disease risk significantly. It also means the water reaches roots before evaporation from the soil surface becomes a factor.

Evening watering is the second-best option if mornings are impossible. The ground stays moist overnight, which plants appreciate, but wet foliage going into darkness is an invitation for mildew and botrytis. If you water in the evening, aim the flow at the soil, not the leaves.

Avoid midday watering

Watering in full afternoon sun does not scorch leaves - that is a myth - but it is deeply inefficient. A large proportion evaporates before it reaches roots. Save it for genuine emergencies only.

Midday is the one exception I make for container plants, which can dry out completely in a few hours on a hot patio. Potted herbs, flowers, and anything in terracotta needs checking twice a day in a heatwave. If you grow fruit in pots, the watering demands of containerised fruit trees deserve their own attention - they are thirstier than most people expect.

Step 2: Water Deeply, Not Frequently

The most common summer watering mistake is a brief daily sprinkle. It keeps the top inch of soil damp, which trains roots to stay shallow. Shallow roots are the first to suffer when the surface dries out quickly in hot weather.

Instead, water less often but more generously. Give the soil a long, slow soak - long enough that water penetrates at least 6 inches down. Then wait. Let the top inch or two dry before watering again. This forces roots downward where soil stays cooler and moisture lingers longer.

A simple test: push your finger or a bamboo skewer 3-4 inches into the soil. If it comes out with damp particles clinging to it, hold off. If it comes out bone dry, water now. I do this every morning during heatwaves - takes ten seconds per bed and saves a lot of guesswork.

Step 3: Lay Down Mulch Before the Ground Bakes

Mulch is the single most effective thing you can do for a summer garden, and it costs almost nothing if you compost. A 2-3 inch layer of organic material over your soil surface cuts evaporation dramatically, moderates soil temperature, and suppresses the weeds that compete with your plants for water.

Good mulch options include:

  • Homemade compost - adds nutrients as it breaks down, great all-rounder
  • Straw - cheap, light, excellent for vegetable beds; just avoid hay, which contains weed seeds
  • Grass clippings - free and effective, but apply in thin layers to prevent matting
  • Bark chippings - longer-lasting, better for trees, shrubs, and permanent beds

Keep mulch an inch or two clear of plant stems and trunks. Piled against stems, it holds moisture against the base and causes rot. Pull it back a little before watering, then push it back in - or just water slowly enough that it soaks through.

Mulch after rain or a thorough watering

Applying mulch to already-dry soil traps that dryness in. Soak the ground first, then mulch immediately to lock that moisture in. You will feel the difference in how long the soil stays workable.

Step 4: Create Shade Where Plants Need It

Not every plant wants full summer sun for eight hours. Lettuces, spinach, and most leafy greens bolt fast in sustained heat. Even fruiting crops like tomatoes and peppers can drop their flowers if temperatures consistently push above 35°C (95°F) - they are fine plants, not invincible ones.

Temporary shade is a straightforward fix. Options include:

  • 30-50% shade cloth draped over hoops or a simple frame
  • Old net curtains - I use these and they work perfectly well
  • A strategically placed trellis of climbing beans on the south or west side of vulnerable beds

Position shade to block the harshest afternoon sun rather than the morning light, which is gentler. Most vegetables still need 4-6 hours of direct sun each day to produce properly. If you are still figuring out where the sun falls across your plot, reviewing how to plan a garden layout from scratch can help you make better decisions about bed placement next season.

Step 5: Group Plants by Water Needs

Watering efficiently becomes much easier when plants with similar needs grow near each other. I learned this the hard way after spending 20 minutes every other evening giving individual plants different amounts, then forgetting which ones I had already done.

Group your beds roughly into:

  1. High-water crops - courgettes, cucumbers, squash, celery; these want consistent moisture
  2. Moderate crops - tomatoes, peppers, beans, brassicas; deep but less frequent
  3. Drought-tolerant - many herbs (rosemary, thyme, oregano), chard, Swiss chard after establishment

This grouping also makes drip irrigation or soaker hoses far more practical. A single soaker hose run through a high-water bed does more good than a sprinkler trying to serve a mixed-need area. If setting up a more permanent watering system sounds like the next step, having the right tools for the job - including a decent hose connector and a timer - makes the process far less fiddly.

Step 6: Raise Your Drought Tolerance With Better Soil

Sandy soil loses moisture within hours of watering. Clay soil holds water but drains poorly, leaving roots sitting in wet anaerobic conditions when it rains after a dry spell. Both situations stress plants in summer.

The fix for both is the same: organic matter. Compost improves sandy soil's moisture retention and opens up clay soil's structure. Work it in during autumn or spring when you are preparing beds. By mid-summer it is too late to dig in bulk amendments without disturbing roots, but you can top-dress with compost (which rain and worms slowly incorporate) and you will see the effect build over time.

A garden with good soil structure - crumbly, dark, full of worm activity - holds onto rainfall and irrigation water far more efficiently. It is a slow investment that pays compounding returns every summer after.

When Heatwaves Hit: Emergency Measures

Even a well-mulched, well-watered garden can struggle in a genuine heatwave - several consecutive days above 35-38°C with no let-up at night. In those conditions, a few extra measures help.

Water twice daily for container plants and anything newly planted that has not yet established deep roots. Young transplants and seedlings are especially vulnerable; their root systems simply cannot reach the deeper soil moisture that established plants access. Shade cloth on the most vulnerable beds is worth the mild faff of putting it up and taking it down.

Do not feed plants during a heatwave. Fertiliser encourages new growth, which needs water. Stressed plants cannot handle that demand. Hold off until temperatures drop and the plants are visibly recovering before applying any feeds - and when you do feed, follow the product label for rates and timing.

Watch for heat stress signs

Wilting in the morning (not just midday) means genuine drought stress. Leaf scorch, tip burn, and flower drop are other signals. Act early - a plant rescued from mild stress recovers quickly; a severely dehydrated one may not recover before the season ends.

How often should I water vegetables in summer?

There is no single answer - it depends on your soil, temperatures, and plant type. A general rule is to water deeply every 2-3 days rather than lightly every day, and always check the soil 3-4 inches down before watering. Sandy soils may need daily watering in a heatwave; moisture-retentive clay soils with good mulch coverage can often go 4-5 days between soakings.

Can I use grey water from the house on vegetables?

Lightly soapy water from washing dishes or rinsing vegetables is generally fine on ornamental beds, but use caution on edible crops - especially anything you eat raw. Water heavily diluted with plain rinse water is lower risk, but avoid anything containing bleach, strong detergents, or other chemicals. Never use water from washing machines or dishwashers on food crops, and always water the soil rather than the leaves or edible parts.

My tomatoes look fine but the leaves curl up in the afternoon - is that drought stress?

Not necessarily. Many tomatoes roll their leaves inward on hot afternoons as a self-protective response - it reduces the surface area exposed to direct sun and cuts water loss. If the leaves uncurl by morning and the plant otherwise looks healthy, this is normal behaviour, not a distress signal. True drought stress shows up as dull, wilted foliage that does not recover overnight, along with dry soil at depth.

Hot summers do not have to be a crisis. A little early preparation - mulch down before the heat arrives, beds grouped sensibly, watering timed well - means most of the work is done before July even starts. The plants do the rest. And if a heatwave catches you off guard anyway, the emergency measures above buy enough time to get back on track. Every summer teaches me something new about my plot; the main thing is to stay curious rather than defeated when something goes wrong.
