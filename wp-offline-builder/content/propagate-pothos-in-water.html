There's a moment, usually on a quiet Sunday morning, when you notice your pothos has sent out a long, trailing vine that's practically begging to become its own plant. Those bright, heart-shaped leaves, the way the stem knuckles down every inch or so - it already knows what it wants. Water propagation lets it get there with almost no help from you.

I started propagating pothos in water years ago, almost by accident. I'd snipped a stem to tidy up a shelf plant, dropped it in a bud vase while I decided what to do with it, and forgot about it for two weeks. When I remembered, there were roots. Proper, pale, half-inch roots. That's how forgiving this process is.

Pothos - Epipremnum aureum - is one of the easiest houseplants to multiply this way. The method below works for golden pothos, marble queen, neon, and most other varieties. One thing worth noting before you start: pothos is mildly toxic to cats, dogs, and people if eaten. Keep cuttings (and the parent plant) out of reach of pets and small children, and wash your hands after handling the sap.

What You'll Need Before You Cut

Gather everything first. It sounds fussy, but having clean scissors, a clear container, and a spot already chosen for the cutting means the whole job takes under five minutes.

  • A healthy parent plant with at least one long vine
  • Clean, sharp scissors or pruning snips (wipe the blades with rubbing alcohol first)
  • A glass, jar, or clear vase - something narrow enough to hold the stem upright
  • Room-temperature tap water, or filtered water if your tap is heavily chlorinated
Tip: Go Clear

A clear container lets you watch the roots develop without disturbing the cutting. Coloured glass works, but you'll miss half the fun. I use an old jam jar - unglamorous, but perfect.

You don't need rooting hormone for pothos. It roots readily on its own, which is part of why it's such a good beginner propagation project. If you've been nervous about propagating before, start here.

Taking the Right Cutting

Look for a vine with at least three or four leaves. Find a node - that small, slightly raised bump on the stem where a leaf joins, or where an aerial root is already forming. Your cut goes just below a node, because that's where the new roots will emerge. Without a node, you have a pretty piece of stem that will simply rot.

Cut about four to six inches of stem. Remove the bottom one or two leaves so the bare stem can sit in water without the leaves rotting below the surface. Leave two or three leaves at the top. That's your cutting.

If your parent plant has been showing any yellowing leaves, don't take cuttings from stressed vines. Choose the greenest, firmest section you can find - a healthy start gives you the best roots.

Setting the Cutting in Water

Place the cutting in your jar so the node (or existing aerial root) is fully submerged but the remaining leaves sit above the waterline. Leaves in water will rot and cloud the water quickly, so check the position now rather than later.

Set the jar somewhere bright but out of direct sun. A windowsill with filtered light is ideal - intense afternoon sun will warm the water and encourage algae rather than roots. The cutting doesn't need much right now. It's gathering itself.

Water Changes

Swap out the water every five to seven days, or sooner if it turns cloudy or smells stale. Fresh water keeps oxygen levels up and slows algae. Just tip out the old water, refill with room-temperature water, and return the cutting gently.

What to Expect Week by Week

Patience is most of this process. Here's what the cutting is doing while you're going about your life.

Week 1-2: Quiet Settling

Nothing much seems to happen. The cutting may droop slightly as it adjusts. Don't panic and don't move it around. The node is beginning to callous and prepare for root growth.

Week 2-4: Roots Appear

Small white or cream-coloured nubs push out from the node. This is genuinely exciting - the first time you see it, you'll understand why people get addicted to propagation. The roots grow quickly once they start, sometimes an inch or more in just a few days.

Week 4-6: Ready to Pot (or Keep)

Once roots reach roughly an inch to two inches long, the cutting is ready to move into soil. Some people keep pothos in water indefinitely - and it works, with occasional liquid fertiliser added to the water - but soil-grown plants tend to become fuller and more vigorous over time. That's because water-adapted roots can struggle with the transition if left too long; the sooner you pot up after reaching that inch mark, the smoother the adjustment.

Moving the Cutting to Soil

Choose a small pot - around four inches - with drainage holes. A well-draining potting mix works well; you can add a handful of perlite if your mix feels dense. Make a small hole in the centre with your finger or a pencil, lower the rooted cutting in, and firm the soil gently around it.

Water it in thoroughly, then let the top inch of soil dry before watering again. For the first week or two, the plant may look a little limp as it adapts from water roots to soil roots. This is normal. Keep it out of bright direct light during this adjustment period and resist the urge to overwater.

Once it perks up and you see new leaf growth, you can move it to its permanent spot and treat it like any other pothos. If you enjoy creating green arrangements for specific rooms, the propagated plants are wonderful for filling in a layered, textural display on a shelf or mantlepiece - trailing over the edge, catching the light.

Common Mistakes Worth Knowing

A few things trip up new propagators, and they're all easy to avoid once you know they exist.

  • No node on the cutting. If there's no node, there will be no roots. Go back and look for a cutting with a visible node or aerial root nub.
  • Leaves submerged in water. Submerged leaves rot fast and foul the water. Always strip leaves below the waterline before placing the cutting.
  • Moving the jar constantly. Every time you check the roots by lifting the cutting out, you slow the process. Look through the glass. Wait.
  • Waiting too long to pot up. Very long water roots (over three inches) become brittle and break easily during transplanting. Pot up at the one-to-two-inch stage.
  • Cold water straight from the tap. Very cold water can shock a cutting. Let it sit at room temperature for thirty minutes first, especially in winter.

How long does pothos take to root in water?

Most pothos cuttings show their first roots within two to four weeks, depending on the season, light levels, and the variety. Neon pothos tends to root a little faster in my experience; marble queen can take closer to four to six weeks. Keep the water clean and the light bright-but-indirect, and the roots will come.

Can I propagate pothos in tap water, or does it need filtered water?

Most tap water is fine. If your tap water is very heavily chlorinated, it can slow rooting slightly - letting the water sit in an open container overnight before using it allows much of the chlorine to dissipate. Filtered or rain water works well if you have it, but it's not a requirement.

My pothos cutting has been in water for six weeks and still has no roots - what's wrong?

Check first that you have a node on the submerged section of stem - a cutting without a node will never root. If there is a node, the issue is usually low light or cold temperatures. Move the jar to a brighter spot (still no direct sun) and make sure the room stays above about 60°F (15°C). Warm, bright conditions speed root development considerably.

Propagating pothos is one of those small, satisfying rituals that reminds you how generous plants can be. From one healthy vine you can fill several pots, share with neighbours, or simply enjoy watching the roots appear in a jar on your windowsill. Give it a try - the cutting is already waiting.
