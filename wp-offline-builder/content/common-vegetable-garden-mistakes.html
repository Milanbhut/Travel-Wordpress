Last July, a neighbour showed me her tomato bed with a look of defeat. The plants were enormous, lush, and almost completely fruitless. She'd done everything right - or so she thought. Good soil, regular watering, full sun. What she hadn't done was thin the seedlings, stake early enough, or give each plant room to breathe. The tomatoes had basically smothered themselves.

It's one of the most disheartening things in gardening: putting real effort in and getting very little back. The frustrating part is that the mistakes behind most poor harvests aren't dramatic. They're quiet. A little too much water here. Plants crowded a few inches too close together. Seeds sown two weeks too late. Small errors that compound across the season.

I've been growing vegetables for over twenty years, and I still catch myself slipping into old habits. These are the mistakes I see most often - in my own garden and in others' - along with the fixes that actually make a difference.

1. Planting Too Close Together

Seed packets and transplant labels have spacing recommendations for a reason, and that reason is competition. Roots compete for water and nutrients. Leaves compete for light. Air circulation drops, and suddenly you've created perfect conditions for fungal disease.

I've been guilty of "just squeezing one more" into a bed more times than I'd like to admit. The short-term result looks fine. The long-term result is a crowded mess where everything underperforms.

The fix is simple but requires restraint: follow the spacing on the label, at minimum. For smaller beds, look up square-foot gardening guides, which give honest numbers for intensive planting. If you're starting from seed - say, you want to grow peppers from seed - sow two or three seeds per cell and thin to one strong seedling. Don't try to save the extras by cramming them in.

2. Watering on a Schedule Instead of Reading the Soil

Watering every day sounds responsible. It often isn't. Most vegetables prefer to dry out slightly between waterings - consistent moisture, not constant saturation. Watering by the clock, regardless of rainfall or soil condition, is one of the fastest paths to root rot and yellowing leaves.

The better approach is to check the soil before you water. Push a finger an inch or two into the ground. If it's still damp, wait. If it's dry, water deeply and thoroughly rather than giving a light sprinkle every evening. Deep, infrequent watering encourages roots to go down, which builds resilience through dry spells.

Tip: The Finger Test

Before watering, push your finger 2 inches into the soil. Still damp? Skip the session. Dry to the touch? Water slowly at the base of the plant, not the leaves, until moisture reaches the root zone.

Seedlings are the exception - they need more consistent moisture while they establish. But once plants are a few weeks old and rooted in, ease back and let the soil be your guide, not the calendar.

3. Ignoring Soil Before the Season Starts

You can do everything else right and still get a disappointing harvest if the soil is compacted, nutrient-depleted, or wildly wrong in pH. Vegetables are heavy feeders, and they'll tell you about poor soil through pale leaves, stunted growth, and poor fruit set.

The fix isn't complicated, but it does take a little time. A basic soil test - available inexpensively through most cooperative extension services - tells you what you're working with. From there, you can add compost, adjust pH with lime or sulphur, and amend for drainage. Do this before planting, not mid-season when the damage is already done.

4. Sowing at the Wrong Time

Timing is the part of vegetable gardening that trips people up most reliably. Every region, every microclimate, and every crop has a window - and outside that window, your seeds are fighting conditions they're not built for.

Cool-season crops like lettuce, spinach, and brassicas bolt in summer heat. Warm-season crops like courgettes, beans, and corn sulk in cold soil and either rot or sit dormant until conditions improve. Starting tomatoes indoors too early leads to leggy seedlings that need hardening off all over again.

The practical fix is to work backward from your average last frost date (for spring planting) or first frost date (for autumn crops). Seed packets give you a days-to-maturity figure - count from that and you'll know your planting window. Pin a simple seasonal calendar to your shed door. It sounds obvious, but having it written down stops the "I'll just try it" impulse in July.

5. Letting Pests Get a Head Start

Most gardeners notice pest damage after it's become a real problem. A few aphids become a colony. A couple of cabbage whites become dozens of caterpillars working through your kale from the inside out. By the time the damage is obvious, the population is well established.

The smarter approach is to check plants weekly - undersides of leaves included - from the moment they go in the ground. Early intervention is almost always easier and less disruptive than later treatment.

For most common vegetable garden pests, there's a non-chemical option worth trying first: physical removal, row covers, companion planting, or encouraging beneficial insects. Our guide on natural pest control methods covers the approaches that actually hold up across a season. If you do reach for a pesticide or insecticide, always follow the product label and local guidelines - more product is rarely better, and off-label use can harm the very predators that would otherwise help you.

Worth knowing

Many common garden pesticides aren't selective. Broad-spectrum sprays can kill beneficial insects - including pollinators - alongside the pests you're targeting. Spot-treat at dusk when bees are less active, and always read the label before use.

6. Underfeeding After Planting

There's a common assumption that if you've amended the soil well before planting, you're done with feeding. For heavy-cropping plants like tomatoes, squash, and peppers, that's rarely true. These plants pull significant nutrients through a long growing season, and by midsummer, even a well-prepared bed starts running low.

A side dressing of compost every four to six weeks, or a balanced liquid feed applied to the root zone during fruiting, makes a noticeable difference. Watch the leaves: yellowing from the bottom up often signals nitrogen deficiency; poor fruit set can point to low potassium or irregular watering.

Don't over-correct, either. Too much nitrogen late in the season pushes leafy growth at the expense of fruit. The goal is steady, balanced support - not a sudden burst of feeding.

7. Giving Up on Succession Planting

This one's less a mistake you make and more a habit you skip. Sowing all your salad seeds at once gives you a glut for two weeks and then nothing for the rest of summer. Planting all your bean seeds in one go means you're picking frantically in August and then it's over.

Succession planting - sowing small batches every two to three weeks - spreads the harvest across the season and keeps the kitchen well supplied. It's a small shift in habit that has a large effect on what actually makes it to the table. Set a reminder on your phone or mark your calendar. A single tray of lettuce seedlings every three weeks is easier to manage than one enormous sowing, and you'll eat better for it.

How do I know if I'm overwatering my vegetables?

Yellow leaves, wilting even when the soil is wet, and a sour or musty smell from the soil are common signs. Check drainage first - if water pools after rain or watering, your soil may be too compacted. Ease off watering and let the bed dry slightly between sessions. Most vegetables recover well once overwatering stops.

What's the single biggest mistake first-time vegetable gardeners make?

Starting too big. A small, well-managed bed almost always outperforms a large, neglected one. Begin with a 2-by-2 metre patch, a handful of easy crops, and give yourself time to learn what grows well in your specific conditions before expanding.

My plants look healthy but aren't producing much fruit - what's going wrong?

This usually comes down to one of three things: too much nitrogen (lots of leaves, little fruit), pollination problems (check for bees and, in enclosed spaces, hand-pollinate), or inconsistent watering during fruit set. Tomatoes and peppers especially need steady moisture - fluctuating between wet and dry causes blossom drop and blossom end rot.

None of these mistakes are fatal to a season, and most are fixable mid-way through. The garden is patient - much more patient than we are. Catching one or two of these habits early can shift a struggling bed into one that actually feeds you, and that shift is more satisfying than any shortcut I've found.
