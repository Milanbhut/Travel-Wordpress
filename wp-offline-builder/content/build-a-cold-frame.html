Last October, with the first hard frost three nights away, I found myself covering tomato plants with old bedsheets and wishing, not for the first time, that I had built a cold frame years earlier. A cold frame is exactly what it sounds like: a low, lidded box that traps enough of the sun's warmth to keep the air inside a couple of degrees warmer than the air outside. That gap is modest, but it is often all a seedling or overwintering plant needs.

The build itself is genuinely a weekend project - simpler than a raised bed, cheaper than a greenhouse. I have built three cold frames now using reclaimed lumber and old double-glazed windows, and the learning curve flattens fast. The principles stay the same whether you source fancy cedar or the pallet wood stacked behind your neighbour's shed.

What follows is the sequence I use: site selection, sizing, the build, and then the part most guides skip - how to actually manage the frame through the seasons without cooking or desiccating whatever is inside.

Choose the Right Spot First

Placement is the one decision you can't easily undo, so take a moment here. A south-facing slope or a wall that faces south (in the northern hemisphere) is ideal. The frame wants to catch low winter sun and shed rain.

Avoid frost pockets - those low dips in the garden where cold air settles on still nights. Even a modest cold frame won't overcome a site that collects five degrees of extra chill. If your plot is flat, a spot along a south-facing fence or brick wall is usually your best bet; the masonry absorbs heat during the day and releases it slowly at night.

Keep it within reach of water. I made the mistake once of siting a frame at the far end of the plot, which meant I skipped watering it during busy weeks. For guidance on keeping seedlings hydrated without overwatering, the summer garden watering guide has practical advice that scales down to cold-frame conditions just as well.

Measure the Lid Before You Build the Box

This is the step that saves most of the frustration. Start with the glazing - whatever old window or polycarbonate sheet you have - and build the box to fit it, not the other way around.

A single-sash window is the most common donor. Measure it exactly (width and depth), then cut your side boards so the lid seats cleanly on top. The classic cold frame has a sloped top: the back wall a few inches taller than the front, so rain runs off and the sun angle is caught throughout the day. A rough guide is a back wall of around 30-35 cm and a front wall of 20 cm, with 30 cm sides cut at an angle to bridge the gap.

If you are using polycarbonate sheeting instead of glass, cut it to match your box dimensions once the frame is assembled - it is forgiving and easy to trim with a handsaw.

Material note

Untreated or naturally rot-resistant wood (cedar, oak, or even upcycled floorboards) is fine. Avoid pressure-treated timber near edible crops unless it is rated for ground contact and food-adjacent use - check the product documentation and follow the manufacturer's guidance.

Cut and Assemble the Box

You need four boards - front, back, and two sides - plus a handful of screws and, optionally, corner brackets. That's it. Most of the hand tools you need will already be in your shed; if you are still building out your kit, the essential garden tools overview covers the basics worth having before you tackle projects like this.

Cut the side boards at an angle so they slope from back height to front height. A handsaw works; a circular saw is faster but not necessary. The cuts do not have to be surgically precise - a cold frame is not fine furniture, and a small gap at a corner can be plugged with a strip of weatherstrip foam if it bothers you.

  1. Cut your four boards to length (or angle) according to your lid dimensions.
  2. Pre-drill screw holes to avoid splitting, especially near board ends.
  3. Assemble the four walls into a rectangle on a flat surface.
  4. Check that the lid rests flat on top with minimal rocking.
  5. Add a simple handle to the lid - a scrap of wood screwed to the inside frame works well and keeps the glass from slipping when you prop it open.
  6. Attach a prop stick (a length of dowel or scrap timber) inside the box so you can vent the frame by wedging the lid open at different heights.

No floor is needed. Set the box directly on the soil and push it down a little to seal the base against drafts.

Set It Up and Settle the Plants In

Before you bring plants in, dig the base soil lightly and add a thin layer of good compost. This gives roots somewhere to go and helps retain moisture. Let the frame sit for a day or two before planting so the soil temperature inside can stabilise.

Cold frames are ideal for:

  • Hardening off seedlings in spring before transplanting
  • Starting cool-season crops (lettuce, spinach, kale, radishes) four to six weeks earlier than you could outdoors
  • Keeping root vegetables in the ground through light frosts
  • Overwintering tender perennials that are not quite hardy in your zone

Crowding is the main temptation to resist. Leave enough space between plants for air to move, especially with brassica seedlings, which are prone to damping-off in still, humid conditions.

Zone and timing note

How much season extension you actually get depends on your climate. In USDA zones 6-8, a single-pane glass cold frame typically adds four to six weeks in spring and autumn. In colder zones, a double-glazed lid or an additional layer of horticultural fleece inside the frame will push that further. Check your local extension service for region-specific timing guidance.

Manage Ventilation - This Is the Hard Part

The cold frame will overheat faster than you expect. On a bright day in early March, when it is still 4°C outside, the temperature inside a closed frame can climb above 30°C within an hour of sunrise. That will cook seedlings just as effectively as frost.

The rule of thumb I follow: if it is sunny and above 7°C outside, the lid comes off or gets propped open. By mid-morning on most clear spring days, even in cooler climates, ventilation is needed. Close the lid by late afternoon to trap residual warmth before evening.

Automatic vent openers - the wax-cylinder type that expand with heat - are a good investment if you can't check the frame during the day. They cost very little and have saved my seedlings more than once when I was away from the garden.

Watering and Feeding Inside the Frame

Soil inside a cold frame dries more slowly than open-bed soil in winter, but faster than you might think during a warm spell. Feel the soil two inches down before watering. In deep winter the plants may need water only once every ten days or so. In spring, as temperatures rise and growth picks up, check every two to three days.

Feeding is usually minimal for overwintering plants - they are resting rather than growing hard. For spring seedlings, a dilute liquid feed once growth accelerates is usually enough to see them through to transplanting.

What a Cold Frame Can't Do

A single-pane cold frame is not a heated greenhouse. It protects against light and moderate frosts, not hard freezes that go well below −8°C for several nights in a row. If that is your climate, either add insulation (old carpet over the lid at night, horticultural fleece inside) or plan to use the frame only during shoulder seasons rather than deep winter.

It also won't compensate for poor siting or unsuitable plants. I have seen gardeners try to overwinter half-hardy citrus in a cold frame with predictably sad results. The frame works best when you match it to plants with reasonable cold tolerance - think salad leaves, hardy annuals, brassicas, and established perennials that just need a little frost insurance.

If you are working with fruit trees nearby, understanding what they need in different seasons matters too. Knowing why a fruit tree might not be fruiting often comes down to the same seasonal awareness that makes cold-frame gardening work - chill hours, temperature swings, and timing all connect.

Can I use a cold frame all year, or just in spring and autumn?

You can use it year-round with a bit of planning. In spring it's perfect for hardening off seedlings; in summer, remove the lid entirely and use the frame as a low raised bed; in autumn and winter, it earns its keep protecting cool-season crops and overwintering tender plants. Some gardeners store the lid through summer to keep it from degrading in the heat.

Is glass or polycarbonate better for the lid?

An old glass window is free if you can find one and transmits light very well, but it is heavy and shatters if dropped. Polycarbonate sheeting is lightweight, shatterproof, and easier to cut, though cheaper single-wall polycarbonate transmits slightly less light than glass and can yellow over a few years. Either works well - choose based on what you have available and how the frame will be used.

How do I keep slugs out of my cold frame?

Slugs love the warm, moist environment inside a cold frame just as much as your seedlings do. Copper tape along the top edge of the frame deters many of them. A shallow tray of water pushed against the outside of the frame can also help. If you use slug pellets, choose a product labelled as safe around wildlife and pets, follow the label rates exactly, and keep any treated areas well away from areas where children or animals can reach. Removing debris and fallen leaves from around the frame removes hiding spots and is a good first step before reaching for anything chemical.

A cold frame repays the weekend you put into it many times over - in earlier harvests, later salad leaves, and seedlings that arrive at transplant time strong rather than leggy. Build it once and it will quietly work through the shoulder seasons for a decade or more. That kind of patient, incremental gain is exactly what sustainable gardening is made of.
