Picture your garden on a warm July morning. The lavender is buzzing - not metaphorically, but literally, a low steady hum from a dozen honeybees working each spike in turn. Two painted lady butterflies drift between the echinacea heads. A bumblebee, conspicuously fuzzy, disappears headfirst into a foxglove bell. That scene is achievable on almost any plot, from a sprawling back garden to a few containers on a sunny balcony.

The trick is choosing the right flowers. Not every bloom is created equal for pollinators. Heavily doubled cultivars often look spectacular to us but offer almost no nectar or pollen - the breeding that packed in those extra petals often removed the reward insects need. The plants on this list were chosen because they genuinely perform, season after season, not just because they photograph well.

I've planted all of these on my own plot over the years, and I can tell you which ones earn their keep and which ones I quietly moved on from. Let's go through the lineup.

1. Lavender (Lavandula spp.)

If you plant only one thing for pollinators, make it lavender. Bees in particular seem almost unable to walk past it. English lavender (Lavandula angustifolia) is hardiest and flowers in early summer; 'Hidcote' and 'Munstead' are reliable compact varieties. French lavender (Lavandula stoechas) blooms earlier and longer in mild climates but struggles in hard winters.

Plant lavender in full sun and very well-drained soil - it sulks in heavy, wet ground. A gravel mulch around the base does wonders. Clip the spent flower stems back to the foliage after flowering to keep plants bushy rather than woody and leggy.

Design note

A low hedge of lavender along a path edge creates a scented corridor that pollinators patrol endlessly. Five or seven plants in a loose drift look far better than a single specimen, and the buzzing is something visitors remember.

2. Echinacea (Coneflower)

Echinacea purpurea is a garden workhorse. The flat, open face of the flower is an easy landing platform for bees and butterflies, and the raised central cone is packed with pollen. It blooms from midsummer through early autumn - precisely the window when many earlier flowers have gone over.

I'd steer you toward the straight species or simple cultivars like 'Magnus' or 'White Swan' rather than the trendy orange, yellow, or double-flowered forms, which tend to have reduced nectar. Echinacea is tough once established, tolerates drought, and the seed heads that follow the flowers feed goldfinches right into winter. It's a genuinely generous plant.

Note: the root of echinacea has a long history in herbal medicine, but treat it as an ornamental in the garden and don't let children or pets chew on it without knowing what they're doing.

3. Borage (Borago officinalis)

Borage is possibly the easiest annual on this list. Direct-sow it where you want it in spring - it resents transplanting - and within weeks you'll have a sprawling, bristly plant studded with starry blue flowers. Bees visit borage constantly. The nectar replenishes fast, which is why the same flower will be visited by multiple bees throughout a single morning.

It self-seeds prolifically, which is either wonderful or annoying depending on your garden style. In my plot I let it pop up wherever it wants around the vegetable beds - it draws in hoverflies too, and those hoverfly larvae quietly mop up aphids nearby. If you're growing vegetables, borage is worth having alongside them. You might even glance at growing sunflowers for height and structure in the same border - the two plants together create a cheerful, informal pollinator patch through summer.

4. Catmint (Nepeta spp.)

Catmint is a borderline obsession of mine. It billows beautifully, softening path edges and spilling over steps, and from late spring through summer it's covered in small lilac-blue flowers that bees adore. Cut it back hard by about two-thirds after the first flush and it rebounds with a second wave in late summer. Two flushes of flowers for minimal effort - that's a good deal.

'Six Hills Giant' grows to about 90 cm and makes an impressive mound. 'Walker's Low' (despite the name, it's a decent size) is more compact and arguably flowers more reliably. Both are fully hardy in most UK and northern US climates.

Yes, cats will roll in it. That's just part of the deal.

5. Single-Flowered Dahlias

Dahlias have a reputation for being high-maintenance - and some of them, the dinnerplate giants that need staking, feeding, and careful storage, are. But single-flowered and anemone-flowered dahlias are a different proposition. The open face exposes the pollen and nectar directly, and pollinators use them heavily through late summer and autumn when other flowers are winding down.

Look for varieties labelled 'single', 'collarette', or 'anemone' on the packet. 'Bishop of Llandaff' is a classic: dark foliage, red single flowers, bees love it. 'Totally Tangerine' is spectacular and consistently draws butterflies in my garden from August until the first frost.

Lift the tubers after the first frost if you're in a colder climate, let them dry out, and store them in a cool frost-free place over winter. It's a bit of work, but these plants reward you for years.

Key principle

Aim for continuous bloom across the seasons rather than one big summer explosion. By combining spring-flowering plants, midsummer workhorses, and late-season bloomers, you keep pollinators fed from March through October - and your garden interesting for longer too.

6. Verbena bonariensis

Tall, airy, almost transparent - verbena bonariensis is one of those plants that adds presence without blocking sightlines. The wiry stems carry clusters of tiny purple flowers at around 1.5 m, and butterflies in particular seem drawn to it from surprising distances. Red admiral, small tortoiseshell, peacock - I've seen all three on it on the same afternoon.

It's a short-lived perennial but self-seeds freely, so once you have it you tend to keep it. It works beautifully woven through other planting rather than in a solid block. The flowers are also excellent for cutting if you want to bring pollinators indoors in the form of a vase arrangement.

Verbena isn't the most complex plant to keep, but it pairs wonderfully with other structural plants. If your border is also home to roses that aren't performing well, it might be worth reading about why roses aren't blooming - sorting out their health alongside a verbena planting makes for a much more rewarding border.

7. Phacelia (Phacelia tanacetifolia)

Phacelia is something of a secret weapon. It's commonly used as a green manure by vegetable gardeners, but as a cut-flower or pollinator plant it's underrated in ornamental gardens. The soft lavender-blue, coiled flower heads attract an enormous range of bees - not just honeybees and bumblebees but many solitary bee species too.

It grows quickly from seed, flowers in about eight weeks from sowing, and is genuinely undemanding. Sow in succession from spring through early summer for a long season of bloom. If you're thinking about which easy-going plants to start with indoors before moving them out, building confidence with beginner-friendly houseplants first is a good way to develop your seed-sowing instincts before you tackle a full outdoor succession.

One caveat: phacelia leaf hairs can cause skin irritation in some people. Wear gloves when handling large quantities, particularly when cutting it back or pulling it out at the end of the season.

Putting It All Together

A pollinator-friendly border doesn't need to look wild or unkempt - it just needs to be thoughtfully sequenced. Start with spring bulbs (crocus, alliums) to carry early emerging bees, move into lavender, catmint, and borage for early summer, bring in echinacea and dahlias for high summer, and let verbena bonariensis carry you through to the first frosts.

Think in drifts of three to five plants of each type rather than solitary specimens. Pollinators find it easier to navigate from one cluster to another, and the visual effect is more cohesive too. And if height is on your mind, you can take design cues from growing sunflowers to their full stature - sunflowers are themselves excellent late-summer pollinator plants and give the border real vertical drama.

Avoid pesticides where possible in a pollinator-friendly planting. If you must treat a pest problem, always read and follow the product label, choose the least-toxic option, and avoid applying anything to open flowers where bees and butterflies are actively foraging. Your local cooperative extension service can advise on integrated pest management approaches suited to your area.

Which of these flowers is best for small gardens or containers?

Lavender, catmint, and single-flowered dahlias all adapt well to containers. Choose compact varieties - 'Hidcote' lavender and 'Walker's Low' catmint stay manageable - and use a large pot (at least 30 cm diameter) with good drainage. Even a single well-planted container on a sunny balcony will attract bees and butterflies through summer.

Do I need to avoid all double-flowered cultivars?

Not entirely. Some semi-double flowers still offer accessible pollen and nectar - the issue is mainly with flowers so heavily bred that the reproductive parts are replaced by extra petals. As a rule, if you can see the centre of the flower and it has visible pollen-bearing stamens, it's likely still useful to pollinators. When in doubt, watch for a few minutes: if bees are landing on it, it's working.

When is the best time to plant for maximum pollinator benefit?

The goal is continuity rather than a single peak moment. Plant spring-flowering perennials and bulbs in autumn; sow annuals like borage and phacelia in spring; plant tender perennials like dahlias after your last frost date. This staggered approach means something is always blooming from early spring right through to the first hard frosts of autumn.

A garden that draws in pollinators is one that rewards you every time you step outside. There's real satisfaction in watching a bumblebee work its way methodically through a spike of catmint, or seeing a butterfly you can't quite identify settle on an echinacea head. Build the planting, give it a season or two to establish, and it will find its own rhythm - you're really just setting the stage.
