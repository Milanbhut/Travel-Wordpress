Last spring I stood in a garden centre car park with £400 in my pocket and a genuine argument in my head. Raised beds on one side of the aisle. A flat-pack polycarbonate greenhouse on the other. I went home with neither, spent two weeks making a spreadsheet, and eventually figured it out. You're welcome to skip the spreadsheet part.

The raised bed versus greenhouse debate is really a question about what you want your garden to do. More growing space? Earlier harvests? Year-round crops? Both options deliver on some of those goals, but not all of them, and the cost-per-benefit gap between the two is wider than most beginners expect.

Below is what I found after running both set-ups on my own plot - real costs, real yields, and a few things nobody tells you in the brochure.

What Each Option Actually Does

A raised bed is essentially a contained growing zone. You control the soil quality, drainage is predictable, and your back thanks you for the extra height. That's it. It doesn't extend your season by much unless you add a cover.

A greenhouse is a climate modifier. It buys you warmth - typically 4-8°C above ambient - which translates into earlier sowing, later harvesting, and the ability to overwinter tender crops or cuttings. It does not improve soil or drainage on its own.

These distinctions matter because gardeners often conflate the two. If you're making the classic mistake of sowing too early outdoors, a raised bed alone won't fix that. A greenhouse will.

The raised bed wins on upfront cost by a clear margin. But it's also a simpler tool doing a simpler job. If you're on a tight budget, one well-built raised bed filled with quality compost will outperform a cheap greenhouse nearly every time.

Season Extension: Where Greenhouses Pull Ahead

This is the greenhouse's genuine superpower. On my plot in the north of England, a greenhouse lets me start tomatoes and peppers six weeks earlier than outdoor conditions allow. That six weeks can be the difference between a glut of fruit and a handful of green ones.

Raised beds can extend your season too - add a cold frame lid or a row of cloches and you might gain two to three weeks in spring. That's worth doing. But a cloche is not a greenhouse, and the warmth inside a glazed structure on a sunny February afternoon is something you have to experience to appreciate.

Worth knowing

A greenhouse does nothing for you if you don't manage ventilation. Overheating is a bigger killer of seedlings than cold. Fit automatic vent openers if you're at work during the day - they cost around £20-£30 and are worth every penny.

One honest caveat: a greenhouse without heating is unheated. Hard frosts will still damage tender plants on a January night. If you want genuine year-round tropical-style growing, you'll need supplemental heat - which adds running costs. Factor that in.

Yield Per Square Foot: Raised Beds Hold Their Own

Here's the thing that surprises people. When you compare square footage of cropping space at similar spend, raised beds often produce more food. A well-amended raised bed with good compost grows vegetables faster than the same plants in a pot inside a greenhouse.

Greenhouses shine for crops that need warmth - tomatoes, cucumbers, chillies, aubergines. Raised beds shine for everything else - salads, brassicas, root vegetables, beans. My personal verdict: I grow roughly twice as many calories from my three raised beds as from my 6 × 8 ft greenhouse, but the greenhouse produces the crops I couldn't grow outdoors at all.

If you've been thinking about adding vertical growing structures to maximise space, raised beds integrate with trellises and A-frames far more easily than greenhouse interiors do.

Which Crops Belong Where

Best for raised beds

  • Lettuce, spinach, and salad leaves - they prefer cool and bolt in greenhouse heat
  • Root crops (carrots, parsnips, beetroot) - need deep, loose soil; raised beds deliver this
  • Brassicas (kale, broccoli, cabbage) - cold-hardy and space-hungry; grow them outside
  • Beans and peas - climb beautifully on outdoor structures, don't need extra warmth

Best for greenhouses

  • Tomatoes and cucumbers - need warmth and a long season; greenhouse is the right home
  • Chillies and sweet peppers - almost impossible to get a decent harvest outdoors in cooler climates
  • Seedlings and cuttings - the greenhouse earns its keep as a propagation space even if nothing else grows there
  • Overwintering tender perennials - dahlias, fuchsias, half-hardy herbs

If you're planning to establish berry bushes on your plot, raised beds are a natural pairing - blueberries especially love the acidic, well-drained compost mix you can dial in with raised beds that you can't easily achieve in open ground.

The Verdict: Who Should Buy What First

Buy a raised bed first if you're a complete beginner, have a budget under £200, or your main goal is growing more vegetables. The learning curve is gentle, the feedback is fast, and a well-made bed will still be going strong a decade from now.

Buy a greenhouse first if you're obsessed with tomatoes, live in a cooler or wetter climate, or want to propagate your own plants at scale. The season extension alone can justify the cost in a couple of years if you're growing crops you'd otherwise buy.

If budget allows, do both - but build the raised beds first. Get your soil and crop knowledge dialled in, then add the greenhouse when you know exactly which warm-season crops you want to push further.

My honest order of operations

Start with two raised beds and a bag of quality peat-free compost. Grow salads, beans, and root veg for a season. You'll quickly identify the gaps - the crops you wanted but couldn't grow outdoors. Those gaps are your greenhouse shopping list.

Can I just put a cold frame on my raised bed instead of buying a greenhouse?

Absolutely, and it's a smart move. A timber-framed cold frame with a polycarbonate lid costs £40-£80 and sits directly on top of a raised bed. It gives you 3-4 weeks of extra season in spring and autumn, which covers most of what beginners actually need. It won't grow tomatoes through summer, but for hardening off seedlings and protecting overwintering salads, it's excellent value.

How long before a greenhouse pays for itself?

Roughly 3-5 growing seasons for a mid-range model, assuming you're using it for propagation and warm-season crops. The payback is faster if you're buying chilli plants or tomato plants each year - growing your own from seed cuts that cost to almost nothing. The payback is slower if the greenhouse mainly holds empty pots and hope.

Are there any safety considerations for building raised beds or a greenhouse?

For raised beds, avoid pressure-treated timber that uses older arsenic-based preservatives - check that any treated wood is rated safe for food growing. For a greenhouse, base preparation matters: an unstable or uneven base is the most common cause of frame damage and glazing failures. If you're installing a larger structure, it's worth checking with your local council whether planning permission is needed, and consulting the manufacturer's guidelines on anchoring against wind.

Neither option is a bad investment if you match it to what you actually want to grow. The gardeners I've seen waste money are the ones who bought a greenhouse to "grow everything" and discovered it sits empty eight months a year. Know your crops, know your climate, and spend accordingly. Your plot - and your wallet - will thank you for it.
