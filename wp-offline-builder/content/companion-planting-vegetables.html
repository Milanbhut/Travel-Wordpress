Last spring, I planted basil next to my tomatoes mostly out of habit. I had read it somewhere years ago and figured it could not hurt. What actually happened was instructive: the tomatoes did fine, the basil bolted in July, and I spent more time wondering if I had imagined the benefit than I did harvesting anything impressive. So I started paying closer attention - to what the research actually supports, and to what I could observe on my own plot.

Companion planting is one of those topics that sits right at the edge of good science and confident folklore. Some pairings have solid reasoning behind them. Others have been repeated so many times they feel true even when they are not. The difference matters, because garden space is finite and you want to fill it with combinations that actually earn their keep.

What follows is a practical breakdown of the pairings worth your time, the mechanisms that make them work, and a few popular myths that deserve a sceptical look. Think of it as a checklist - the kind you can actually act on before the next sowing season.

Why Companion Planting Works (When It Does)

The logic is not magic. Plants affect their neighbours through root chemistry, canopy shape, volatile compounds, and the insects they attract or repel. Some release allelopathic chemicals that slow nearby weeds. Others fix nitrogen and effectively feed the plants beside them. A few attract specific predatory insects that feed on common pests.

The honest caveat: these effects are real but modest. Companion planting is not a substitute for good soil, good spacing, or consistent watering. It is one layer in a well-run plot. Think of it the same way you would think about using organic soil amendments - a meaningful contribution that works best when the foundations are already solid.

Key concept

Companion planting works through four main mechanisms: nutrient sharing, physical structure, pest confusion, and beneficial insect attraction. Most successful pairs do at least two of these at once.

The Three Sisters: Still the Most Reliable Trio

Corn, beans, and squash have been grown together by Indigenous farmers across North America for centuries. The system earns its longevity because each plant genuinely serves the others. Corn provides a vertical structure for beans to climb. Beans fix atmospheric nitrogen, feeding the heavy-feeding corn and squash. Squash spreads wide, shading the soil beneath and suppressing weeds while its rough leaves deter some ground-level pests.

On my plot, I run a loose Three Sisters bed in a 1.5 m square section each year. The squash does most of the heavy lifting in terms of weed suppression - honestly, I barely need to hand-weed that bed once the squash canopy closes up around week four.

A note on spacing: plant corn first and let it reach about 20 cm before adding beans. Squash goes in a week or two later. Rush the sequence and everything competes rather than cooperates. Also keep in mind that how you lay out your beds matters as much as what you plant in them - thinking through your kitchen garden layout before you commit to companion combinations saves a lot of replanting.

Tomatoes and Carrots: A Quieter But Useful Pair

This one flies under the radar. Carrots loosen the soil around tomato roots as they grow, improving drainage and root penetration without any digging on your part. In return, tomato foliage provides light shade in warm climates that keeps carrots from bolting early in the season.

There is also some evidence that the aromatic compounds in carrot foliage can confuse aphids looking for a clear scent trail. Whether that rises to the level of meaningful pest control is debatable. What is not debatable is that the two plants simply do not compete for the same resources, which is more than you can say for a lot of combinations people try.

What to avoid nearby

Do not plant fennel near tomatoes. Fennel is allelopathic - it releases compounds that suppress growth in many plants, tomatoes included. I learned this one by planting a large fennel directly into an existing tomato bed and watching three plants slowly underperform for an entire season before I figured out why.

Brassicas and Nasturtiums: Pest Management That Actually Pays Off

Nasturtiums are one of the few companion plants where the pest-control mechanism is well-documented. They act as trap crops for aphids, drawing black bean aphids and other species away from nearby brassicas. Rather than repelling pests, they attract and concentrate them - which sounds counterintuitive until you realise it is far easier to deal with an aphid infestation on one sacrificial plant than across an entire cabbage row.

Plant nasturtiums at the edges of your brassica beds and check them weekly. When you find a heavy infestation, remove and dispose of the worst-affected stems. Do not compost them if aphids are still active on the foliage.

Timing tip

Sow nasturtiums two to three weeks before your brassica transplants go in. You want the trap crop established and attractive before the brassicas are even in the ground.

For organic pest management on brassicas, nasturtiums work best as part of a broader strategy - keep an eye on soil health too, since stressed plants in depleted soil attract pests far more readily than vigorous ones.

Beans and Potatoes: A Classic With Caveats

Beans and potatoes are traditionally paired because beans fix nitrogen, which potatoes use in large quantities. The pairing holds up in practice, particularly for bush beans rather than climbing varieties, which can shade potato foliage and reduce tuber yield.

The caveat is spacing. Potatoes need room to develop underground, and crowded companions above ground often lead to crowded roots below. If you are growing potatoes in containers - and if you have not tried growing potatoes in bags, the yield-per-space ratio is genuinely impressive - you will want to keep beans in adjacent beds rather than the same vessel.

One thing beans and potatoes share: neither does well next to onions or garlic. Alliums produce sulphur compounds that inhibit the growth of legumes in particular. Keep those families apart.

Myth Check: The Pairings That Disappoint

Basil and tomatoes. I will save you my summer of ambiguous results: the evidence that basil improves tomato flavour or meaningfully repels pests is weak. They grow well together because they like similar conditions, not because they actively help each other. If you like basil, grow it. Just do not expect it to do a job it was never reliably proven to do.

Marigolds and everything. Marigolds do produce a nematocidal compound (alpha-terthienyl) from their roots, but it only builds up in soil after several seasons of dense planting and requires the soil to be worked to release it. One row of marigolds around a bed does not create a pest barrier. French marigolds (Tagetes patula) do attract whitefly - some gardeners use them as trap crops, which is legitimate - but the general "marigolds repel everything" advice is overclaimed.

Making the Layout Work

Companion planting only delivers results if the physical arrangement makes sense. Tall plants need to go where they will not shadow shorter, sun-hungry neighbours. Wide spreaders like squash need clear lanes. And you need to be able to reach everything for harvesting and maintenance without trampling adjacent plants.

A few practical notes for the planning stage:

  • Sketch your bed dimensions before choosing companions - a combination that works in a 2 m bed may not in a narrow 60 cm strip.
  • Rotate companion groups each season along with the main crop. The same pest pressures that make rotation important for vegetables apply to the companion plants too.
  • Keep records. Note which pairings performed well and which felt like they were just coexisting. Your own plot's observations are more useful than generalised advice.

Does companion planting actually increase yield, or is it mainly about pest control?

Both, depending on the pairing. Nitrogen-fixing combinations like beans and corn have a measurable yield effect for the heavy feeder. Trap crop pairings like nasturtiums and brassicas work mainly through pest management rather than direct feeding. Some pairings - like carrots and tomatoes - deliver mostly structural and resource-sharing benefits. The honest answer is that companion planting usually improves conditions rather than dramatically increasing yields on its own.

Are any common companion plants toxic to pets or children?

Yes - several plants commonly used in companion planting are worth keeping in mind. Tomato foliage and green parts of the potato plant are toxic if ingested. Nasturtiums are edible and generally safe, but some ornamental companions are not. If a child or pet ingests any plant material and you are unsure, contact your local poison control line or a vet rather than searching for a home remedy.

How close do companion plants need to be to have an effect?

For root-based effects - like the Three Sisters' nitrogen sharing - the plants typically need to be within normal spacing range, around 30-60 cm. For trap crops like nasturtiums, planting along the bed perimeter is usually sufficient. For volatile compound effects, the evidence for distance thresholds is less clear; planting within the same bed is a reasonable rule of thumb.

Companion planting is worth the small amount of extra planning it takes - not because every pairing is transformative, but because the reliable ones genuinely earn their space. Test a few combinations that match your main crops, keep notes on what you actually observe, and build from there. Your plot will tell you more than any list can.
