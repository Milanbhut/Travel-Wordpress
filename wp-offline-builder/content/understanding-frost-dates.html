Last April, I direct-sowed tomatoes into the bed on a warm Saturday afternoon, feeling very pleased with myself. The following Tuesday brought a sharp frost. I lost the lot. Not because I didn't know my last frost date - I did - but because I didn't understand what that date actually means or how much margin to build around it.

Frost dates are probably the single most referenced piece of gardening information, and also one of the most misunderstood. They're not guarantees. They're not hard cut-offs. They're probabilities - and once you understand that, the whole planting calendar starts to make a lot more sense.

Whether you're planning your seed-starting schedule or trying to squeeze one last autumn harvest from the beds, getting your head around first and last frost dates is worth the 10 minutes it takes.

What a Frost Date Actually Is

A frost date is a statistical average - the date on which, historically, your area has a 50% chance of experiencing a frost (air temperature at or below 0°C / 32°F). Some sources use a 10% or 30% probability threshold instead, which is worth checking when you look up your local figures.

Every growing season has two frost dates: the last spring frost (after which frosts become unlikely) and the first autumn frost (when they start creeping back in). The gap between these two dates is your frost-free growing window - what most gardeners think of as "the season".

In a cool temperate climate, that window might be only 100-120 frost-free days. In warmer zones it can stretch to 200 days or beyond. In particularly mild coastal areas, the concept of a hard frost date barely applies at all - which is its own kind of planning puzzle.

Key term

A "killing frost" is generally colder than a light frost - temperatures of -2°C (28°F) or below that damage or destroy tender plant tissue. Your local frost date data usually specifies which threshold they're using.

How to Look Up Your Frost Dates

In most countries, your national meteorological service publishes historical frost date data by region or postcode. In the US, the Old Farmer's Almanac tool and the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone map are widely used starting points. In the UK, the RHS provides regional guides. For everyone else: your national weather service is the most reliable source.

Type in your nearest town or postcode, and you should get two dates: the average last spring frost and the average first autumn frost. Write them down somewhere you'll actually look at them - I tape mine inside the shed door alongside the month-by-month planting schedule I follow throughout the year.

It's also worth noting that frost dates vary significantly with microclimate. A garden at the base of a valley will collect cold air and frost much harder than one on a slope 30 metres away. Raised beds tend to warm faster; low-lying areas stay cold longer. No database will capture your exact microclimate, so local observation matters.

Last Frost Date: Planning Your Spring Sowing

The last frost date is your anchor point for spring planting. Most seed packets give you a "sow X weeks before last frost" instruction - this is exactly what they mean.

Here's how I think about it in practice:

  • Tender crops (tomatoes, peppers, courgettes, basil) should only go outside after your last frost date - and even then, I'd give it a week or two of buffer. These plants don't just dislike frost; they can stall in cold soil even when it's not freezing.
  • Half-hardy crops (French beans, cucumbers, squash) can go out around your last frost date with some cloches or fleece for insurance.
  • Hardy crops (peas, broad beans, brassicas, most root veg) tolerate some frost and can often go in several weeks before the last frost date.

Count backwards from your last frost date to find your seed-starting dates for indoor sowings. Tomatoes, for example, typically need 6-8 weeks of indoor growth before they're ready to harden off and plant out. Start too early and you'll have enormous, root-bound plants sitting on your windowsill with nowhere to go.

First Autumn Frost: Stretching the Season

The first autumn frost date is equally useful, though it gets less attention. This is when you need to start protecting tender crops or making decisions about what to harvest now versus what to leave out.

Most tender crops can survive a light frost if you cover them the night before with fleece, an old bedsheet, or a cloche. That can buy you another week or two of harvest. I've kept tomatoes and courgettes going well into October in a UK garden with this approach - though there's a point where the flavour and effort stop being worth it.

The first frost date is also your cue to think about overwintering. Root vegetables like carrots and parsnips can actually stay in the ground through frost - some say parsnips sweeten with a frost - while tender perennials need lifting and storing before the ground freezes hard. If you're planning any quick weekend garden projects before the season closes, getting cold frames or fleece structures in place before the first frost date is a sensible priority.

Frost protection tip

Cover plants the evening before a forecast frost, not the morning of. Heat accumulated in the soil during the day is your best ally - trapping it under fleece overnight can prevent frost damage even when temperatures dip briefly below freezing.

The Probability Problem (and Why You Need a Buffer)

Here's the thing I wish someone had told me earlier: a 50% probability frost date means there's still a one-in-two chance of frost after that date in any given year. That's not reassuring odds for a tray of seedlings you've been nurturing for six weeks.

Many experienced gardeners work to the 10% probability date - the date after which there's only a 10% chance of frost - for their most precious plants. This is usually 2-3 weeks after the average last frost date depending on your location. For anything you've grown from seed and can't easily replace, that extra buffer is worth it.

Equally, in autumn, the 10% probability first frost date gives you a clearer window for how long you genuinely have. Weather apps with a 10-day forecast become your friend as the season shifts - a sudden cold snap can arrive earlier than average in any given year.

How Frost Dates Connect to Your Whole Planting Year

Once you anchor your year to frost dates, everything else slots into place. You can work backward from your first autumn frost to know when to sow a final succession of salad leaves. You can count forward from your last spring frost to plan when brassica transplants should be ready for the beds after the tomatoes have vacated them.

This kind of whole-year thinking is exactly what makes the difference between a reactive gardener and a proactive one. If you're thinking about when to prune flowering shrubs, for instance, frost timing matters there too - pruning too late in autumn and cutting back to fresh growth just before a hard frost can set tender shrubs back significantly.

Your frost-free window is essentially your production calendar. Work with it, not around it, and the garden becomes a lot less stressful to manage.

Common Mistakes Worth Avoiding

A few patterns I see (and have made myself) that are worth flagging:

  • Trusting the date blindly. It's a statistical midpoint, not a firm promise. Keep an eye on the long-range forecast around your last frost date, especially if the spring has been unusually cold or warm.
  • Ignoring microclimates. Your neighbour three streets over might have a different effective frost date depending on local topography. Track your own frosts for a season or two and adjust accordingly.
  • Skipping hardening off. Even after the last frost date, plants raised indoors need 7-10 days of gradual outdoor exposure before they can cope with unprotected life outside. Skipping this step - even in mild weather - causes transplant shock.
  • Forgetting about ground frost. Air temperature and ground temperature are different. Soil can stay frozen longer than air temperatures suggest, affecting root crops and what you can usefully plant out.

What's the difference between my last frost date and the date I should actually plant out?

Your last frost date is the statistical average - there's still roughly a 50% chance of frost after that date in any given year. For tender plants, it's safer to wait 1-2 weeks beyond it, or to work from the 10% probability date if your local data provides one. If you can't find the 10% figure, a two-week buffer after the average last frost date is a reasonable rule of thumb.

Can I still plant out if a light frost is forecast?

It depends on the plant. Hardy crops like brassicas, peas, and most root vegetables will shrug off a light frost. Tender crops - tomatoes, courgettes, beans, basil - should stay protected. Cover tender plants the evening before with horticultural fleece or a cloche; don't leave it to the morning. Even a brief dip below freezing can damage soft tissue on unprotected tender plants.

My frost dates don't seem to match what's happening in my garden. What should I do?

Trust your observations over the averages. Frost date databases are based on regional weather station data, which may not reflect your specific microclimate - a sheltered walled garden frosts later than open ground nearby, while a frost pocket at the bottom of a slope may frost earlier than any database suggests. Keep a simple garden journal noting actual frost events for a season or two, and you'll develop a far more accurate picture of your own conditions.

Frost dates are one of those pieces of gardening knowledge that seem simple until you actually try to use them - then you realise there's quite a bit of nuance underneath. But once you understand them as probabilities rather than certainties, and start building the right buffer around them, your sowing and planting decisions get a lot more confident. My tomatoes, at least, have had a much better track record since I learned that lesson the hard way.
