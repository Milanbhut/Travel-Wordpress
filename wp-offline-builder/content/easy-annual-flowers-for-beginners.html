Picture a window box absolutely stuffed with zinnia blooms in tangerine, coral, and deep burgundy, planted by someone who had never grown a flower from seed before. That was my neighbour Helen last summer, working with nothing more than a packet of seeds, some decent compost, and a sunny south-facing sill. By July the display was genuinely stopping people in the street.

Annuals are the secret weapon of the beginner gardener. They germinate fast, bloom in their first season, and ask for very little in return. You do not need raised beds, a polytunnel, or years of experience. You just need the right varieties - the ones bred to be nearly impossible to fail with.

Below are six of my absolute favourites. I have grown all of them in containers, cut-flower patches, and a more structured flower border. Every single one earns its place by being both gorgeous and reliably beginner-proof.

1. Zinnias - the fastest payoff in the garden

Zinnia elegans might be the single most rewarding annual for a newcomer. Sow the seeds directly into warm soil - they do not love being transplanted - and blooms can appear in as little as six weeks. The colour range is dazzling: everything from pale blush to almost-black burgundy.

Zinnias thrive in heat and full sun. They actually sulk in cold, wet summers, so if you are gardening in a cooler climate, give them the warmest spot you have and start them a little later once the soil has genuinely warmed up. Pinching the first flower bud will encourage more branching and a longer display.

Deadheading tip

Cut zinnia stems right back to a set of leaves rather than just snapping off the head. You will get a longer side-shoot and a fresh bloom within a week or two.

2. Cosmos - airy, elegant, and almost indestructible

Cosmos bipinnatus is the variety most seed packets refer to, and it is extraordinary for how little effort it demands. The feathery foliage is attractive even before the flowers open. Plants can reach a metre and a half tall in good conditions, so they work beautifully at the back of a mixed planting scheme.

Sow thinly - they germinate freely and you will be thinning seedlings if you are not careful. I direct-sow cosmos where I want them to flower, which avoids any check in growth. They tolerate poorer soil surprisingly well; too much fertility produces masses of leaf and fewer flowers.

Which cosmos variety to try first

'Sensation Mixed' is the classic starter choice, with large flowers in white, pink, and cerise. For something a little different, 'Cupcakes' has unusual cup-and-saucer-shaped blooms that look like no other annual in the border.

3. Sunflowers - tall ambitions, minimal fuss

There is something almost theatrical about a sunflower. Children love them for the race-to-be-tallest novelty, but I love them for the way a single stem of 'Velvet Queen' - a rich bronze-crimson variety - can anchor an entire summer planting.

Sow sunflower seeds an inch deep, directly where they are to flower, once frosts have passed. They resent root disturbance and bolt upright with little encouragement. Standard tall varieties need staking in exposed spots; the shorter multi-branching types like 'Sunbright Supreme' need no support at all.

A small note on wildlife: sunflower seeds are beloved by squirrels and birds. If they are raiding your seedlings, a fleece cloche over the emerging plants for the first fortnight usually solves it. Once established, the plants are tough enough to be left alone.

4. Nasturtiums - grow them even in poor soil

Nasturtiums (Tropaeolum majus) are almost aggressively easy. Push a seed into the ground and step back. They flower better in poor, well-drained soil than in rich compost - a quality that makes them ideal for gaps in paving, dry banks, and container arrangements where other plants struggle.

Both the flowers and the leaves are edible, with a pleasantly peppery flavour. They make a genuinely pretty garnish. That said, keep nasturtiums away from young children who might eat them in quantity, and check with your vet if you have grazing livestock - as with most ornamental plants, they are not intended for regular consumption by animals.

Trailing varieties will cascade over a container edge beautifully. Bush types like 'Alaska' have attractive variegated foliage and stay compact. Either way, you are looking at flowers within roughly eight weeks of sowing.

Pest watch

Aphids adore nasturtiums. Some gardeners use them deliberately as a sacrificial trap crop to draw aphids away from vegetables. If populations get out of hand, a firm jet of water from a hose knocks most of them off. Reach for an insecticide only if other methods fail, and always follow the product label.

5. Sweet Alyssum - a low carpet that hums with bees

Lobularia maritima sounds fancy, but sweet alyssum is one of the simplest plants you can grow. It forms a low, billowing mat of tiny white, pink, or purple flowers that smell lightly of honey and attract hoverflies and small bees in impressive numbers. If you care about pollinators - and most gardeners increasingly do - alyssum earns its space on that basis alone.

Scatter seeds on the surface of moist compost and barely cover them with a fine dusting of more compost or vermiculite; they need light to germinate well. They work brilliantly as edging, between paving slabs, or as a weed-suppressing ground cover under taller plants. Cut them back hard if they go straggly in midsummer and they will typically resprout and flower again by early autumn.

6. Marigolds - the reliable workhorse

French marigolds (Tagetes patula) have been a staple of beginner gardens for generations, and for good reason. They are almost impossible to kill, they flower from early summer right through the first frosts, and certain varieties are thought to help deter some soil pests when planted near vegetables - though I would not stake my tomato crop on it alone.

Sow indoors six to eight weeks before the last frost, or buy plug plants and harden them off before planting out. They are not fussy about soil but do want sun. Deadhead regularly to keep them producing. Colours range from lemon yellow to deep burnt orange, and they are vigorous enough that even a child who forgets to water occasionally will still get flowers.

Note that marigold sap can irritate sensitive skin. Wearing gloves when handling large quantities is sensible, especially for anyone with known skin sensitivities.

Bringing it all together

These six annuals do not need to live in isolation. Combine tall cosmos or sunflowers at the back, mid-height zinnias and marigolds in the middle, and alyssum or trailing nasturtiums at the front edge, and you have a genuinely designed planting rather than a jumble of random seeds.

If you are planning something more permanent around these annuals - perhaps adding bulbs for spring colour before the annuals take over - it is worth reading about how to plant spring-flowering bulbs to fill the gap between seasons. And if your outdoor space is limited, several of these annuals do surprisingly well on a bright indoor windowsill, though for true indoor success you might also explore lower-light houseplant options to complement the sunniest spots.

Start small. One variety, one pot, one packet of seeds. The satisfaction of cutting your own zinnias or cosmos and bringing them indoors is completely disproportionate to the effort involved. That is the thing about annuals - the return on investment, in colour and pleasure, is almost unfairly generous.

Can I start annual flowers indoors if I do not have a greenhouse?

Absolutely. A bright windowsill works well for most annuals. A south-facing window is ideal. Sow in small pots or module trays, keep the compost moist but not waterlogged, and move seedlings outdoors gradually over a week or two once the weather is warm enough - a process called hardening off. Zinnias and nasturtiums are actually better started directly outdoors once the soil is warm, as they dislike root disturbance.

How often do annual flowers need watering?

It depends on your soil, climate, and container type. As a general rule, stick your finger an inch into the compost: if it feels dry, water thoroughly. Plants in containers dry out much faster than those in the ground, especially in hot weather - checking daily in summer is not over-cautious. Avoid wetting foliage late in the day, as damp leaves overnight can encourage fungal issues.

Are any of these annual flowers toxic to cats or dogs?

French marigolds (Tagetes) are considered mildly toxic to cats and dogs if eaten in quantity, potentially causing mild gastrointestinal upset. Nasturtiums are generally regarded as low-risk but are not intended as pet food. If you suspect your pet has eaten a significant amount of any garden plant, contact your vet or a local animal poison control service promptly - they are better placed than any article to advise on your specific situation.

Growing annuals from seed is one of the most direct ways to feel connected to the gardening cycle - you hold the seed, you sow it, you watch it bloom, all in one season. Pick one of these six to try this year. Chances are, you will be ordering six more varieties by winter.
