My neighbour knocked on the gate in mid-October, pointed at my raised beds, and asked why I was still out there digging when the growing season was obviously over. I handed him a garlic clove, pointed at the patch I'd just finished, and told him to come back in June. He did. He's been planting garlic every October since.

Autumn planting is one of gardening's more satisfying tricks. You do the work now, in cool, manageable weather, and then the cold does the real labour over winter - vernalising bulbs, establishing roots, chilling seeds that need it. By the time spring rolls around, your autumn-planted beds are already weeks ahead of anything you'd put in the ground in March.

Below is my working list: the things I actually plant every autumn, in roughly the order I'd tackle them if the season were short. All of them have earned their place through repetition, not theory.

1. Garlic - The Non-Negotiable First Job

Garlic needs a period of cold to split into a full, multi-cloved bulb. Plant too late and you get rounds instead of heads. Plant in autumn - typically six to eight weeks before the ground freezes hard in your zone - and you get the genuine article by midsummer.

Split a bulb into individual cloves, plant them pointed-end up about 5 cm deep and 15 cm apart, and mulch well. Hardneck varieties like 'Chesnok Red' or 'Music' suit colder climates; softneck types store longer and suit milder winters. Either way, the effort is about twenty minutes per bed.

Tip: Choose Your Own Seed

Keep the largest cloves from your best bulbs for replanting each year. Over three or four seasons you'll end up with a strain that's quietly adapted to your exact conditions. It sounds like plant breeding; it's actually just observation.

2. Spring-Flowering Bulbs - Delayed Gratification Done Right

Tulips, daffodils, alliums, hyacinths - this entire category is waiting for you at the garden centre right now, and most gardeners still underplant them. A single 25 cm pot can hold fifteen tulip bulbs layered in two tiers, which sounds fussy until you see the result in April.

The rule of thumb is to plant bulbs at roughly two to three times their own depth. Tulips go in last (they actually prefer a November chill before the ground freezes). Daffodils and alliums can go in earlier. One thing worth knowing: daffodil bulbs are toxic to people and pets if ingested, so wash your hands after handling them and keep curious dogs away from freshly planted beds.

If you've been dealing with a dry summer and haven't refreshed your soil moisture, this is a good moment to review your irrigation habits from the warmer months - bulbs want moisture to get going, but they'll rot in waterlogged ground.

3. Broad Beans - The Overwintering Vegetable Worth the Bet

Autumn-sown broad beans (varieties like 'Aquadulce Claudia' are specifically bred for this) go in the ground before the first hard frost and sit there all winter, emerging as stocky, established plants in late winter or early spring. Compare that to a spring-sown crop and you're looking at harvests that arrive three to five weeks earlier.

They do carry some risk - a truly brutal winter without snow cover can knock them back, and slugs love the young shoots. But on my plot, autumn-sown broad beans have out-performed spring sowings eight out of ten years. I'll take those odds.

Sow seeds about 5 cm deep, 20 cm apart. If your plot gets really cold, a simple cold frame over the row will take the edge off the worst nights without any additional heat.

4. Hardy Annual Flowers - For a Spring Cut Garden

Sweet peas, larkspur, cornflower, and nigella all germinate better after a cold period. Sow directly into the ground in autumn (or into root trainers in an unheated cold frame), let them establish, and they'll flower weeks earlier than any spring-sown equivalent - and with noticeably stronger stems.

The usual mistake is sowing too early and getting leggy plants that then get knocked back by a hard frost. Aim for late autumn, when the soil is still workable but temperatures are consistently cool. You want seedlings to be small and tough going into winter, not lush and vulnerable.

Common Mistake

Sowing autumn flowers too deep. Most hardy annuals want barely a dusting of compost over the top - roughly 5 mm. Larkspur especially tends to fail if buried. When in doubt, scatter on the surface and press in gently.

5. Overwintering Onion Sets - A Quicker Route to Early Bulbs

Japanese overwintering onion sets (look for varieties labelled 'Senshyu Yellow' or 'Electric') are planted in autumn rather than spring. They establish roots before winter, then bolt into growth early the following year and are ready to lift by June - a solid month before a spring-planted crop.

Plant sets about 10 cm apart, just below the soil surface. Firm them in well; birds will pull shallow-planted sets straight out. The yield per set isn't quite as large as spring-planted onions, but the earliness more than compensates if you're cooking fresh rather than storing.

What About Spring Onions?

Some hardy spring onion varieties, like 'White Lisbon Winter Hardy', can also be sown in autumn for early spring harvests. Thin to 2-3 cm apart and they'll bulk up as soon as temperatures lift.

6. Bare-Root Fruit Trees and Shrubs - The Best-Value Window

From late autumn through to early spring (while plants are dormant), nurseries sell trees and shrubs as bare-root stock - no pot, no compost, just roots. They're cheaper, easier to transport, and they establish faster because they go straight into the ground without any pot-bound root adjustment needed.

Autumn planting gives roots the whole winter to settle in before any leaf growth makes demands on them. If you've been wondering about adding a productive tree to a smaller plot, this is a good moment to look at compact fruit tree varieties that suit tighter spaces - many are available as bare-root stock from autumn onwards.

Stake new trees firmly, water in well even in cool weather, and mulch around the base without touching the trunk. That's genuinely most of the job.

Timing Is Everything - Know Your Zone

Everything above assumes you're working in a temperate climate with a real winter. If your winters are mild (think USDA zones 9-10), your timing shifts later, and some of these crops - garlic, broad beans - may underperform without the cold they need. Conversely, in very cold zones (below zone 5), you'll want to get garlic and broad beans in earlier, and protection like a cold frame becomes less optional.

Local extension services and experienced gardeners in your area are genuinely worth talking to. Regional knowledge beats general guidance every time.

Can I plant garlic in pots if I don't have garden beds?

Yes, garlic does reasonably well in deep pots - at least 25-30 cm deep to give roots room. Use a gritty, free-draining compost mix, plant cloves at the usual depth, and keep the pot somewhere it will still experience cold winter temperatures. Pots on a sheltered patio may stay too warm in mild climates, which can prevent proper bulb formation.

What if I miss the autumn window - can I still plant bulbs in early winter?

Tulips are actually quite forgiving here and can be planted even into December in many climates, provided the ground isn't frozen solid. Garlic and broad beans are more time-sensitive; a late start means a later harvest, but it's rarely a total write-off unless the ground freezes before roots can establish. Get them in as soon as you can.

Do autumn-planted crops need feeding before winter?

Generally, no - and sometimes a high-nitrogen feed in autumn actively works against you, promoting soft leafy growth that's then vulnerable to frost damage. Garlic and onions prefer to establish quietly. A balanced, low-nitrogen feed in early spring, as growth resumes, is far more useful. Always follow the product label for any fertiliser you use.

Autumn gardening has a reputation for being end-of-year housekeeping - clearing up, putting the garden to bed. It's actually the opposite. The beds you tend in October are the ones that reward you in April, while everyone else is still waiting for the soil to warm up. Put in the hours now, and spring will feel like it arrived with something already accomplished.
