There is something quietly urgent about a plant whose roots have begun to spiral around the inside of the pot, pressing against the drainage holes, pushing up through the soil surface. It has run out of room. It is not dying - not yet - but it is telling you, in the only language it has, that it needs more space to breathe.

I repotted my fiddle-leaf fig last spring after ignoring those signals for about six months longer than I should have. The root ball came out in one solid mass, perfectly shaped to the inside of its old pot. The plant had been surviving, but not growing. Once it settled into a new container with fresh soil around it, the difference was visible within weeks.

Repotting does not need to be a dramatic event. Approached calmly and with a little preparation, it is one of the most satisfying things you can do for a houseplant - and for your own sense of being a capable, attentive plant keeper.

How to Tell Your Plant Has Outgrown Its Pot

The clearest sign is roots emerging from the drainage holes at the base of the pot. When you see a network of roots actively pushing outward, the plant has filled every available inch of soil and is looking for somewhere else to go.

Other signals are subtler. Water might run straight through the pot and out the bottom without seeming to absorb - that usually means the root mass has become so dense there is little soil left to hold moisture. Or you might notice the plant drying out far faster than it used to, even though nothing else has changed.

A plant that has stopped growing during its active season, or one whose leaves look pale and wan despite regular feeding, may also be root-bound. The roots are competing with each other for limited nutrients and there simply is not enough soil volume to support the canopy above.

Quick check

Tip the plant gently on its side, support the stem, and ease the pot away. If you see a dense mat of roots with little visible soil, it is time. If the root ball is loose and soil falls away easily, it probably needs more time - and possibly a look at whether overwatering is the real issue.

Choosing the Right New Pot

This is where many people go wrong: they pick a pot that feels generously sized and end up with a container two or three sizes too large. Excess soil around the roots stays wet for too long and can lead to rot. The goal is a gentle upgrade, not a leap.

Go up by roughly 5 cm (about 2 inches) in diameter - one pot size. For a plant in a 15 cm pot, a 20 cm pot is usually right. For larger floor plants, you can go up to 5-8 cm, but no more than that at once.

Material matters, too. Terracotta is breathable and forgiving; it dries out faster, which suits drought-tolerant plants like succulents and snake plants. Plastic and glazed ceramic retain moisture longer, which works well for ferns and tropical foliage. If you think about how your houseplants are arranged in the room, the pot style also contributes to the look - but function should come first.

Getting Your Supplies Together Before You Start

A little preparation means the plant spends the minimum time with its roots exposed to air. Lay out everything before you unpot anything.

  • Fresh potting mix suited to the plant type (standard all-purpose for most foliage plants; cacti and succulent mix for drought-tolerant species; orchid bark for epiphytes)
  • The new pot, clean and dry, with drainage holes
  • A small piece of mesh or a coffee filter to cover the drainage hole and stop soil escaping
  • A trowel or spoon for scooping soil
  • A watering can ready to go
  • Newspaper or a drop cloth if you are working indoors

Wear gloves if you are working with plants that have irritating sap - euphorbias, for instance, exude a milky latex that can sting skin and eyes. Several popular houseplants are also toxic if ingested by children or pets; if you have a pothos, philodendron, or dieffenbachia, keep curious helpers out of the workspace and wash your hands when you are done.

Step by Step: Moving the Plant

Water the plant lightly a day or two before repotting. Moist soil holds the root ball together better than bone-dry soil, which tends to crumble and scatter. Avoid repotting when the soil is waterlogged, though - a saturated root ball is heavy and harder to handle without tearing roots.

Place your hand over the top of the pot, fingers either side of the stem, and tip it upside down. Let the plant slide out into your hand. If it resists, squeeze the sides of a plastic pot gently, or run a butter knife around the inner edge of a rigid pot. Do not yank the plant by the stem.

Once the root ball is free, spend a moment looking at it. Gently loosen the outer layer of roots with your fingers - this encourages them to grow outward into the new soil rather than continuing to circle. Trim any roots that are dead (brown and mushy) or excessively long and tangled. Sharp, clean scissors are fine for this.

Add a layer of fresh potting mix to the bottom of the new pot. Set the plant in and check that the top of the root ball sits about 2-3 cm below the rim - this leaves space for watering without it spilling over. Fill in around the sides with fresh mix, pressing gently to remove large air pockets. Avoid packing it too tightly; roots need some air movement in the soil.

Important

Do not fertilise immediately after repotting. Fresh potting mix usually contains enough nutrients to carry the plant for four to six weeks. Adding fertiliser to stressed roots can burn them. Wait until you see active new growth before resuming your usual feeding routine.

Watering and the First Few Weeks

Water thoroughly right after repotting, until water drains from the bottom. Then wait. The plant needs to settle, not to be fussed over. Check the soil every few days and water only when the top 2-3 cm feels dry - the same way you would normally judge it.

You may notice the plant doing very little for a few weeks. A few lower leaves might yellow and drop. This is normal. The plant is directing its energy underground, establishing contact with the new soil. Think of it as quiet work rather than a setback.

Keep the plant out of direct harsh sun for the first week or two. Bright indirect light is ideal while it recovers. If you are in the habit of removing spent growth to tidy a plant up, hold off for now - the plant does not need extra stress while it adjusts.

Common Mistakes That Set Plants Back

Going too big with the pot

I know the large terracotta pot looks beautiful and seems like it will give the plant "room to grow." But roots sitting in a large volume of wet soil with no warmth or airflow around them tend to rot before the plant can catch up. Resist the urge to jump sizes.

Repotting at the wrong time

Spring, as growth resumes, is generally the best window for most houseplants. Repotting in autumn or winter - when many plants are resting - adds stress at the worst possible moment. There are exceptions (repotting a truly suffering plant in any season is better than leaving it), but if the situation is not urgent, wait for the growing season.

Forgetting to water beforehand

Dry soil shatters when the plant comes out. You end up with a bare root ball and a cloud of dust, and the roots dry out quickly. A light water the day before keeps everything cohesive.

How often do houseplants need repotting?

Most houseplants are happy to be repotted every one to two years during the spring growing season. Fast growers like pothos and philodendrons may need it annually; slow growers like cacti and snake plants can go three years or more. Watch the plant rather than the calendar - when you see roots escaping the drainage holes or growth slowing during the active season, that is your signal.

Can I reuse old potting mix when I repot?

It is best not to. Old potting mix loses structure over time, compacting into a dense layer that restricts root growth and drains poorly. It may also harbour old fertiliser salts or pest eggs. Fresh mix gives the plant a clean start and provides a small boost of nutrients it can draw on in the first weeks.

My plant looks wilted after repotting - is something wrong?

Some drooping in the first few days is expected. The roots have been disturbed and are not yet fully in contact with the new soil, so the plant may struggle to take up water efficiently. Keep it in bright indirect light, water if the soil is dry, and give it a week before worrying. If it has not perked up after ten days, check for root damage or signs of overwatering.

Repotting is one of those tasks that sounds more complicated than it actually is. Once you have done it a few times, you start to read the signals easily, move through it calmly, and feel a quiet satisfaction in knowing you have given the plant exactly what it needed. That is really all good plant keeping amounts to - paying attention, responding gently, and trusting the process.
