Saturday morning, I carried four cedar boards into my backyard and two hours later I had a raised bed ready for soil. My neighbor watched over the fence with obvious skepticism - she had tried raised beds years ago and quit after spending a fortune on lumber that rotted inside three seasons. By the time I had my second cup of coffee in hand and was mixing soil, she was asking if I could build her one too.

A raised bed is one of those projects that sounds technical until you actually do it. The bones are simple: a rectangular frame, a good soil mix, and a flat patch of ground. What trips most people up is not the carpentry - it is choosing the wrong materials, the wrong dimensions, or filling the thing with whatever bagged dirt was on sale. Get those three decisions right and you will be harvesting in the same season you build.

This is a genuine weekend build. You do not need power tools, and you do not need to be handy. What you need is a plan, which is exactly what follows.

Choose the Right Wood (and What to Avoid)

Cedar is the classic choice, and for good reason. It contains natural oils that resist rot, and a well-built cedar bed typically lasts eight to twelve years without any treatment. The main downside is cost - cedar runs about twice the price of standard pine boards.

Untreated pine works fine and is far cheaper. Expect it to last three to five years before the ground-contact boards begin to soften. Seal the inside face with linseed oil if you want to stretch that a little further.

What you want to avoid is pressure-treated lumber from older stock. Modern ACQ-treated wood is considered safe for vegetable gardens, but I still prefer to keep any treated lumber well away from edible crops where children or pets might be digging around. Reclaimed railway sleepers are similarly best avoided - they often contain creosote, which absolutely does not belong near food.

Quick sizing tip

For a first build, go with a 4 × 8 foot rectangle. You can reach the center from either side without stepping in, and a standard 8-foot board requires no cutting - just buy four of them.

Settle on the Right Dimensions

The 4-foot width rule exists for a reason: your arm reaches about 24 inches comfortably. Go wider than 4 feet and you will find yourself leaning awkwardly to weed the middle, which is fine in your twenties and less fine later. Keep it to 4 feet.

Length is flexible. Eight feet fits most yards and divides neatly for plant spacing. Twelve feet is manageable if you have the room. Much longer and the frame starts to bow outward under soil pressure - you will need a cross-brace or a center support stake to keep the walls straight.

Depth is where beginners often go too shallow. Six inches sounds like plenty until you plant tomatoes or carrots and watch them struggle. Aim for 10 to 12 inches as your minimum for vegetables. Two stacked 2×6 boards gets you exactly that. If your site has hard clay or compacted subsoil below - something covered well in our look at signs of unhealthy soil - going to 16 inches gives roots an even more comfortable home.

Gather Your Materials and Tools

For a standard 4 × 8 foot bed, 10 to 12 inches deep, you will need:

  • Four 2×6 cedar or untreated pine boards, 8 feet long (buy eight total for a double-stacked 12-inch depth)
  • Four corner posts - either 4×4 posts cut to 14 inches, or purpose-made corner brackets
  • Galvanized or stainless deck screws, 2.5 to 3 inches long
  • Hardware cloth (half-inch mesh) if gophers or moles are an issue in your yard
  • A drill, a speed square, and a tape measure

That is honestly the whole list. No saw required if you buy 8-foot boards and leave them at full length. The lumber yard will often make one or two cuts for free if you ask.

Build the Frame

Lay out two long boards parallel to each other on a flat surface, then position the two short end boards between them. This creates the rectangle. The corner posts sit inside each corner - drill two screws through each long board and two through each short board into the post, for eight screws per corner total.

Check for square by measuring diagonally corner to corner. Both diagonal measurements should match within about a quarter of an inch. If they do not, give the frame a gentle push at the long corner until they even out, then re-drill.

Repeat the process for the second tier if you are double-stacking, then set the top frame on the bottom and screw them together through the overlapping boards. Simple as that.

Level matters more than you think

Set the frame on your prepared ground and use a long level along each side. A bed that sits even slightly off-level will pool water at one end during heavy rain and dry out too fast at the other. Spend five minutes on this step and you will save yourself a lot of head-scratching later.

Prepare the Site

Clear the area of turf and weeds. You do not need to dig out the soil below - just cut through the grass with a spade and remove it. If the site slopes noticeably, cut into the high side and use the removed soil to level up the low side before setting the frame down.

If you are worried about grass or weeds growing up through the bottom, lay a few sheets of cardboard inside the frame before adding soil. Cardboard suppresses growth, breaks down within a single growing season, and costs nothing. Skip landscape fabric - it blocks drainage and makes it harder for earthworms to move between your bed and the native soil below, and those worms are doing real work for you.

If your yard has a gopher or mole problem, staple a layer of half-inch hardware cloth to the bottom of the frame before placing it. This is worth the extra hour if you have lost crops to them before.

Fill the Bed with the Right Soil Mix

This is the step that makes or breaks a raised bed. Fill it with topsoil alone and your plants will survive. Give them a proper mix and they will thrive.

The classic formula, often called Mel's Mix after its popularizer, is one-third compost, one-third peat moss or coco coir, and one-third coarse vermiculite or perlite. The compost feeds the plants, the peat or coir holds moisture, and the vermiculite opens up the structure so roots can breathe and water drains freely without sitting.

For a 4 × 8 × 12-inch bed, you will need roughly 32 cubic feet of material - about 9 cubic feet of each component. Most garden centres sell these by the bag, though buying compost by the cubic yard from a local landscape supplier is much more economical once you are filling more than one bed.

Avoid garden soil from bags labelled simply "topsoil." It is often dense, slow-draining, and full of weed seeds. If budget is tight, a 50/50 blend of good quality compost and purchased topsoil is still far better than topsoil alone.

Plant Strategically from the Start

A 4 × 8 bed gives you 32 square feet - more productive space than most people expect once you stop planting in rows and switch to a grid. Divide the bed into a 16-square grid (each square roughly one foot) and assign crops by how much space each one needs. Tomatoes need four squares, lettuce takes one, carrots can share a square twelve deep.

Because you control everything that goes into a raised bed, it is also a great place to start with crops that reward careful soil management. Can I build a raised bed directly on concrete or paving?

Yes, with one adjustment: go deeper. On an impermeable surface, aim for at least 18 to 24 inches of depth so roots have enough room and drainage can exit through the open sides of the frame rather than pooling at the bottom. Drill a few weep holes in the lowest board if you are concerned about standing water.

How long will the bed last before I need to replace it?

Cedar typically lasts 8 to 12 years. Untreated pine is more like 3 to 5. The ground-contact boards deteriorate first, so some gardeners replace just those lower boards rather than rebuilding the whole frame. Keeping the soil surface slightly below the top of the boards helps by reducing moisture contact near the rim.

Do I need to water a raised bed differently than an in-ground garden?

Raised beds drain faster and dry out more quickly than in-ground soil, especially in hot or windy weather. Check soil moisture more often than you think you need to - stick your finger two inches deep; if it feels dry there, water. A layer of mulch on the surface (straw, shredded leaves, or wood chips) cuts moisture loss dramatically and is well worth adding once your seedlings are established.

A raised bed built this weekend can feed you for a decade. Start with one, fill it with a proper mix, and plant something you actually want to eat. The second bed - and there is almost always a second bed - goes up faster once you have done it once. Your neighbor will probably want one of those too.
