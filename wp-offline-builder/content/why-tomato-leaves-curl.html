It was mid-July, 40 plants in the ground, and I walked out to find half of them looking like someone had tried to roll every leaf into a tight little green tube. Panic is the wrong response. I know that now. Back then, I spent twenty minutes Googling increasingly alarming search terms before I settled down and actually looked at the plants.

Tomato leaf curl has around half a dozen distinct causes, and most of them are either harmless or straightforwardly fixable. The trick is telling them apart before you reach for anything drastic. Each cause leaves a slightly different fingerprint - the direction of the curl, which leaves are affected, whether the color is off - and once you learn to read those clues, diagnosis becomes routine.

I've worked through all six of these on my own plot at one point or another, usually by making the underlying mistake first. Here's what each one looks like and how to sort it out.

1. Heat and Water Stress - the Most Common Culprit

On a hot afternoon, tomato leaves roll upward and inward along their length. The lower, older leaves usually curl first. Come evening, when temperatures drop, they often relax back to normal. This is called physiological leaf roll, and it's the plant protecting itself - reducing surface area to slow water loss.

It looks alarming. It isn't. The fix is consistent, deep watering rather than frequent shallow sprinklings. Aim for roughly an inch of water per week, delivered at the base of the plant. A thick layer of mulch - straw, wood chip, anything that holds moisture - makes a real difference on sandy or fast-draining soil.

Quick check

If leaves curl on hot afternoons but recover by morning, it's almost certainly heat stress. No treatment needed beyond steadying your watering schedule.

If your soil drains poorly and the roots are sitting wet, that can also trigger the same curling response - waterlogged roots can't actually move water into the plant efficiently. If that sounds like your situation, the longer fix is improving drainage; take a look at our notes on improving clay or compacted soil before next season.

2. Overly Aggressive Pruning

Here's a mistake I made enthusiastically for two full seasons: heavy suckering. I removed every lateral shoot in sight, convinced I was focusing the plant's energy. What I was actually doing was stressing it.

When you remove a lot of foliage at once, the plant sometimes responds by rolling the leaves it has left. The curl tends to appear on the remaining leaves closest to where you pruned. It's a stress response, not a disease.

The fix is to ease off. Remove no more than a third of the foliage at any one time, and spread larger pruning jobs over several sessions. Let the plant settle between cuts. Healthy plants recover quickly - usually within a week or two.

3. Inconsistent Watering and Fertiliser Imbalance

Boom-and-bust watering - dry for days, then a thorough soaking - is rough on tomatoes. It disrupts nutrient uptake, particularly calcium, which shows up as blossom end rot in fruit but can also contribute to leaf symptoms including curl and edge browning.

Too much nitrogen makes this worse. If you've been feeding heavily with a high-N fertiliser and your leaves are curling, thick, and unusually dark green, back off the nitrogen and switch to a balanced tomato feed. Always follow the product label for application rates and don't assume more is better.

Installing a soaker hose or drip system is the single most effective thing I've done for consistent tomato production. Even pressure, even frequency, every time.

4. Viral Infections - Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus

This one is different in character. Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus (TYLCV) causes the edges of young leaves at the top of the plant to curl upward and inward, while the leaves themselves turn pale yellow-green. Growth slows or stops. It doesn't look like the other kinds of curl.

TYLCV is transmitted by whiteflies, so its presence usually means you have a whitefly problem too. Check the undersides of leaves for the tiny white insects and their sticky eggs.

Important

There is no cure for TYLCV once a plant is infected. Remove and bin (do not compost) infected plants to protect the rest of your crop. Control whiteflies with yellow sticky traps, reflective mulch, or an insecticidal soap - following the product label instructions carefully. Resistant varieties exist and are worth seeking out if TYLCV is common in your area.

Keeping whitefly numbers down matters for the rest of your vegetable beds too. Strategically placed companions can help - basil near tomatoes is a classic, and there's more on which pairings actually work in our piece on companion planting in the vegetable garden.

5. Broad Mite or Herbicide Damage

Broad mites are tiny - invisible to the naked eye - and they cause a distinctive downward curl on new growth at the shoot tips. The affected leaves often look shiny, hardened, or slightly twisted rather than just rolled. Growth points may appear bronze-tinted or stunted.

Herbicide drift causes something similar: distorted new growth, twisted or strap-like leaves, unusual downward curling. If you or a neighbour has recently sprayed anything nearby and your tomatoes suddenly look wrong, that's worth considering.

For broad mites, options include sulphur-based miticides - check labels, check local regulations, and avoid spraying in high heat. For herbicide drift, the honest answer is that recovery is slow and depends on exposure level. Remove badly affected growth, keep the plant well watered, and watch new growth carefully over the coming weeks.

6. Root Problems and Soil Conditions

Damaged, diseased, or restricted roots can't supply the plant with water even when the soil is moist, and the result looks almost identical to drought stress. Symptoms include wilting during the day and leaf curl that doesn't recover overnight.

Root knot nematodes are the most common culprit in warmer climates. They create small galls on the roots - you can gently pull a plant and check. Soil-borne fungal diseases like Fusarium or Verticillium wilt also interrupt water movement and show similar symptoms, often with yellowing that travels up one side of the plant.

Good crop rotation is the primary defence - don't plant tomatoes in the same spot two years running. Healthy, well-structured soil supports the kind of root growth that resists these problems. Managing soil health is a long game but a worthwhile one.

Which Type of Curl Do You Have?

Run through this list before you do anything else:

  • Upward curl, lower leaves, afternoon heat, recovers overnight - heat stress. Mulch and steady watering.
  • Curl after heavy pruning, otherwise healthy - pruning stress. Wait it out.
  • Dark green, thick leaves, boom-bust watering pattern - fertiliser/water imbalance. Ease off nitrogen, even out irrigation.
  • Young leaves at the top, yellow-green, whiteflies present - suspect TYLCV. Remove plant, control whiteflies.
  • Downward curl on new growth, shiny/hardened texture - broad mites or herbicide drift. Inspect closely.
  • Wilting that doesn't recover, yellowing up one side - root or soil problem. Check roots, review rotation history.

If you're still unsure, your local cooperative extension service or master gardener programme can often help identify the issue from a photo or sample - worth the five-minute call.

Is it safe to eat tomatoes from a plant with curling leaves?

For most causes - heat stress, pruning stress, watering issues - yes, the fruit is completely fine to eat. If the plant has a confirmed viral infection like TYLCV, the tomatoes themselves are not harmful to people, but fruit quality is often poor. Tomatoes are not toxic to humans, though the leaves and stems contain alkaloids and shouldn't be eaten.

Can tomato leaf curl spread to my other vegetables?

The non-infectious causes - heat stress, pruning, watering - don't spread at all. TYLCV can affect other plants in the nightshade family (peppers, aubergine) via the same whitefly vector. Soil-borne diseases can persist in the soil and affect subsequent crops, which is why rotation matters. If you're growing a diverse edible garden, including a year-round lettuce patch, keeping nightshades away from areas with known disease history is sensible - there's more on managing space in growing lettuce year-round.

My tomato leaves curl every year in summer. Should I switch varieties?

If the curl reliably appears on hot afternoons and goes away overnight, that's ordinary heat stress and variety won't change much - though heat-tolerant varieties do handle stress more gracefully. If you're dealing with recurrent TYLCV or nematode pressure, then yes, switching to resistant varieties (look for the letters V, F, N, or T on the seed packet) is a practical long-term move.

Tomato leaf curl earns its reputation for alarm, mostly because it looks so dramatic - a plant that looked fine at breakfast can look dire by lunch on a hot day. In practice, the majority of cases sort themselves out with steady watering and a bit of patience. Work through the checklist, fix what you can fix, and let the plant tell you if something more serious is going on.
