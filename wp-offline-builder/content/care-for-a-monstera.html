There is a particular moment every monstera owner knows. You water the plant, step back, and notice a new leaf unfurling - tight, pale green, and still whole. Then it opens. No holes, no splits, just a smooth heart-shaped blade that looks nothing like the glossy plant you bought. You wonder if something has gone wrong.

Nothing has. The plant is simply waiting on you. Fenestrations - those signature splits and holes - are a response to conditions, not a guarantee that comes with the pot. Get the light, support, and humidity right, and the leaves will reward you.

I have grown monsteras in a north-facing flat and a sun-drenched conservatory, and I can tell you: the difference in leaf form between those two environments is startling. What follows is what actually moved the needle.

Understanding What a Monstera Is Actually Trying to Do

Monstera deliciosa is a hemi-epiphyte. In its native Central American rainforest, it germinates on the ground, then climbs the nearest tree toward the canopy light. The splits in mature leaves are thought to let light and wind pass through so the plant can photosynthesize even when partially shaded by a canopy above.

This matters because it tells you what the plant expects: somewhere to climb, bright indirect light, and warmth and humidity that loosely mimic a forest understory. You are not trying to create a jungle - you are just giving the plant enough of those cues that it behaves like a confident adult rather than a nervous seedling.

Younger leaves are always less fenestrated than older ones. That is normal. What you are aiming for is a plant that produces increasingly complex leaves over time, not one that stalls at the juvenile stage because conditions never quite clicked.

Getting the Light Right

Bright, indirect light is the most repeated advice, and it is also the most misunderstood. "Bright indirect" does not mean a dim corner three metres from a window. It means the kind of light that would make you squint slightly if you sat there for an hour - near a window, but without direct sun scorching the leaves.

A spot within one to two metres of a south- or east-facing window (in the northern hemisphere) is a good starting point. A north-facing window can work, but you will see fewer fenestrations and slower growth. I moved mine from a north sill to an east-facing spot and within a season the new leaves arrived with clear splits for the first time.

Light tip

If your monstera is producing lots of leaves but they are all plain and small, inadequate light is usually the culprit - not the soil, not the pot size. Move it closer to the window before reaching for fertiliser.

Direct afternoon sun through glass can bleach or scorch the leaves. A sheer curtain between the plant and a west-facing window is usually enough to soften it.

Giving It Something to Climb

This step is skipped far more often than it should be. A monstera without support sprawls outward and produces smaller, flatter leaves. A monstera with a moss pole or coir totem to grip reaches upward, and the leaves it puts out tend to be larger and more deeply fenestrated.

The reason is aerial roots. Mature monsteras produce thick, brown aerial roots from their nodes. Given something moist to grip - a moss pole kept lightly damp is ideal - those roots anchor the plant and allow it to direct more energy upward rather than into horizontal stems.

Push the pole deep into the pot when the plant is still young enough to train. Use soft ties or plant clips to guide the main stem upward, and mist the pole occasionally to keep the surface moist. It is a slow process, but once the plant starts climbing in earnest you will notice a clear shift in leaf size.

Watering Without Overthinking It

Monsteras are not succulents, but they are not ferns either. Overwatering is the most common way people lose these plants - not because monsteras need drought, but because sitting in soggy soil long-term leads to root rot before any other symptom is visible.

Check the top two to three centimetres of soil. If it is dry, water thoroughly until it drains from the bottom. If it is still damp, wait a few more days. The frequency will vary with season, pot size, and humidity - there is no universal schedule. For a more detailed approach to this, the full guide on watering houseplants explains how to read your specific plant and environment rather than relying on a fixed timetable.

Always use a pot with drainage holes. A heavy, decorative cache pot is fine as an outer container, but the nursery pot or an inner grower pot should drain freely.

Humidity and Temperature

Monsteras prefer humidity of around 50-60%. Most homes run drier than that, especially in winter with heating on. You do not need to obsess over this, but low humidity does slow growth and occasionally causes brown leaf tips.

A small humidifier near the plant is the most effective solution if your home is very dry. Grouping plants together helps a little - they raise ambient humidity through transpiration. Misting the leaves directly is often recommended but provides only brief relief and can promote fungal spots if the leaves stay wet overnight.

Temperature-wise, keep your monstera above 15°C (59°F) at all times, and away from cold draughts from doors and windows in winter. They are surprisingly tolerant of warm rooms but will sulk badly in cold conditions.

Toxicity note

Monstera deliciosa contains calcium oxalate crystals and is toxic to cats, dogs, and humans if ingested. Keep the plant out of reach of pets and small children. If you suspect ingestion, contact your local vet, poison control line, or a medical professional promptly - do not attempt home remedies.

Feeding Through the Growing Season

A balanced liquid fertiliser applied roughly once a month during spring and summer will support steady growth. I use a general houseplant feed diluted to half strength - over-fertilising is surprisingly easy to do, and salt build-up in the soil causes its own problems.

In autumn and winter, most monsteras slow down noticeably. Reduce feeding to every six to eight weeks or stop altogether until growth resumes. Follow the label on whichever product you choose, since concentration and application rates vary between brands.

Monsteras are one of those plants that respond visibly when conditions improve - you can almost watch a healthy plant decide to push a new leaf. If yours is stalling, check light and watering first; feeding is rarely the missing piece on its own. If you are just starting out with indoor plants, it helps to know which species are most forgiving while you find your footing - the guide to the best houseplants for beginners is a good place to establish a baseline before you bring home a plant with more specific needs.

Repotting and Long-Term Care

Repot when roots start circling the base of the pot or pushing through drainage holes - usually every one to two years for a young plant, less often once it matures. Move up one pot size at a time. A pot that is too large holds excess moisture around the roots and invites rot.

Use a well-draining potting mix. A standard indoor potting compost with some added perlite (roughly a 3:1 ratio) works well. Some growers add a small amount of bark chip to aid drainage further.

Wipe the leaves down occasionally with a damp cloth. Dust reduces the amount of light reaching the leaf surface and makes the plant look dull. It is a small thing, but a clean monstera is genuinely a happier monstera.

Unlike most annual bedding plants that are replaced each season, a monstera is a long-term resident. Caring for it is less like tending a seasonal annual and more like developing a relationship - one that pays back in increasingly spectacular leaves the longer you stay patient.

Why are my monstera's leaves not splitting?

Juvenile leaves are naturally uncut - fenestrations develop as the plant matures and conditions improve. The most common reasons for a lack of splits in older plants are insufficient light and the absence of a support structure to climb. Move the plant closer to a bright window and add a moss pole, then give it one full growing season to respond before drawing conclusions.

How do I get my monstera to grow faster?

The biggest lever is light. A monstera in bright indirect light will grow several times faster than one in a dim corner. Consistent watering, a damp moss pole to climb, and monthly feeding in the growing season will all contribute. Most growth happens between spring and early autumn; winter slowdown is normal regardless of what you do.

Are the aerial roots on my monstera a problem?

Not at all - they are a sign of a vigorous, maturing plant. You can guide them into the soil for extra moisture uptake, press them onto a damp moss pole so they anchor the stem, or trim them back neatly with clean scissors if they are getting unruly. Cutting them does not harm the plant.

A monstera rewarded with the right conditions will grow into one of the most dramatic plants you can keep indoors - big leaves, visible fenestrations, and that quiet sense of a living thing doing exactly what it evolved to do. Start with the light, add a pole, and let the plant show you what it is capable of.
