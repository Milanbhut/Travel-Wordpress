Last spring I counted twelve grow bags lined up along the south-facing wall of a student's narrow rental patio. Each one held a different variety - some floury, some waxy, one purple-skinned just for the novelty of it. By mid-summer she had more potatoes than she knew what to do with. Not bad for a space barely two metres wide.

Growing potatoes in bags has become my go-to recommendation for anyone who gardens without a proper plot. The method works on balconies, driveways, flat roofs, and patios. Slugs are easier to manage. Harvesting is as simple as tipping the bag over. And because you control the growing medium entirely, you sidestep the compacted, stony soil that plagues so many garden beds.

The technique is also forgiving for beginners. There are a few steps you need to get right - chitting, layering, earthing up - but none of them are complicated once you understand the reason behind each one. That's what we'll work through here.

Choosing Your Bags and Setting Them Up

Proper potato grow bags hold around 35-50 litres. That volume matters: potatoes need room to develop tubers below the surface, and a cramped bag produces a disappointing harvest. Garden centres sell purpose-made fabric bags with handles, which work beautifully - the fabric breathes, reduces waterlogging, and makes lifting easy.

You can also use large builders' rubble sacks or even stacked car tyres, but I'd stick with fabric bags if budget allows. They're reusable for several seasons and, unlike plastic, they don't overheat in a sunny spot.

Position the bags where they'll get at least six hours of sun a day. A south or west-facing wall is ideal. If your patio surface is prone to waterlogging, raise the bags on bricks or wooden slats to allow drainage from the bottom.

Chitting Your Seed Potatoes First

Chitting - letting seed potatoes sprout short, stubby shoots before planting - gives your crop a head start of a few weeks. Stand the tubers rose-end up (the end with the most eyes) in an egg box or shallow tray, and leave them somewhere cool, bright, and frost-free for four to six weeks.

You're aiming for shoots roughly 2-3 cm long, dark green and sturdy, not pale and spindly. Pale shoots mean not enough light; put them closer to a window.

Tip: Pick the Right Variety

First early varieties like 'Swift' or 'Rocket' suit bags well - they're compact plants and you harvest them around 10 weeks after planting, which keeps the bags in use for a shorter time. Maincrop varieties can work too, but they need a full-sized 50-litre bag and a longer season.

Seed potatoes are certified disease-free, which matters more in a bag than in open ground because you're working with a closed growing system. Don't be tempted to plant supermarket potatoes - they're often treated to suppress sprouting and may carry disease.

Filling and Planting: The Layering Method

This is the step that confuses people most, so let me walk through it carefully.

Start by rolling the top of the bag down so it sits roughly 20 cm tall - a squat pot shape. Add 15 cm of your growing medium. A good mix is one part good-quality multipurpose compost to one part topsoil, with a generous handful of well-rotted garden compost stirred through. If you've been building your own compost, this is a great moment to use it - homemade compost improves drainage and feeds the plants gently over the whole season.

Place two or three chitted tubers on this base layer, shoots pointing upward, spaced evenly. Cover with another 10 cm of compost mix. That's your starting point.

Why Layer?

Potatoes produce tubers along the buried section of stem, not just at the base of the plant. Every centimetre of stem you bury is potential crop. The layering and earthing-up process encourages the plant to keep growing upward while you keep building up the compost below - maximising the tuber-producing zone inside the bag.

Earthing Up as the Plants Grow

Once the shoots are around 20 cm above the compost surface, unroll the bag a little, add another 10 cm of compost, and leave just the top few centimetres of foliage showing. Repeat this every two to three weeks as the plants grow, gradually unrolling the bag each time.

By the time the bag is fully unrolled and filled, you should have a column of buried stem packed with developing tubers. It sounds fussy written out like this, but it quickly becomes habit - a two-minute job every few weeks.

One mistake I see regularly: people earth up too enthusiastically and bury the leaves entirely. Leave some foliage above the compost each time so the plant can still photosynthesize. A plant that can't feed itself won't produce much of a crop. If you want to avoid other common pitfalls at this stage, it's worth checking a list of common vegetable garden mistakes - several of them apply directly to container growing.

Watering and Feeding Through the Season

Bags dry out faster than open soil, especially in warm weather. Check moisture levels every day or two - push a finger into the compost a few centimetres; if it's dry at that depth, water thoroughly until it runs from the bottom.

Inconsistent watering is the main cause of a condition called hollow heart, where the centre of the tuber develops a cavity. It's also linked to scab. Neither is dangerous, just disappointing on the plate.

Begin feeding with a high-potash liquid fertiliser once you see flower buds forming - roughly six to eight weeks after planting for early varieties. Follow the rates on the product label. I prefer a seaweed-based feed as a gentler first option; switch to a dedicated tomato fertiliser if growth looks slow.

Signs Your Plants Are Happy

  • Dark green, upright foliage with no yellow patches
  • Flowers forming right on schedule (most varieties flower before harvest)
  • Compost that smells earthy and fresh, not sour

Harvesting Without the Drama

First early varieties are ready roughly 10 weeks from planting, or about two weeks after the flowers drop. Maincrop types need a full 20 weeks. The surest way to check is simply to plunge your hand into the bag and feel around - if the tubers are marble-sized, wait another week or two. If they're golf-ball sized or larger, they're ready.

To harvest, simply tip the bag onto a tarpaulin or into a wheelbarrow. You don't even need a fork. Work through the compost with your hands and collect every tuber, including the tiny ones (cook those right away; they don't store well). Let the potatoes dry on the surface for a couple of hours before brushing them clean.

After harvest, the spent compost is spent only for potatoes - it's still excellent for adding to a bed or rethinking your kitchen garden layout for next season. Don't use it to grow potatoes again in the same bag without replacing it; you'll risk carrying over disease.

Keeping Pests and Problems in Check

Grow bags reduce but don't eliminate pest pressure. Slugs can still travel up a bag from the ground, especially at night. A copper tape ring around the base helps, or apply a pet-safe slug barrier following the product's label instructions.

Potato blight - the same disease that devastated historical harvests - can still strike if you're growing maincrop types in a humid summer. It appears as brown patches on leaves with a pale halo, often spreading fast. Remove affected leaves immediately, and if the plant is heavily infected, harvest what's there and don't compost the haulm. Certified blight-resistant varieties like 'Sarpo Mira' are worth considering if blight is a recurring problem in your area.

There are no reliable chemical controls for home gardeners against blight once it's established, so early removal of affected material is your main tool. For pest sprays of any kind, always read and follow the product label, and choose the least-toxic option suited to the pest.

How many potatoes will I get from one grow bag?

From a 40-50 litre bag with two or three seed potatoes, you can reasonably expect 1-2 kg of first earlies, sometimes more if conditions are good. Maincrop varieties in a large bag can yield 2-3 kg. Results vary with variety, watering consistency, and the length of your growing season - treat any figure as a guide, not a promise.

Can I reuse the compost from my potato bags?

Yes, but not for potatoes or any other member of the Solanaceae family (tomatoes, peppers, aubergines) in that same compost. There's a risk of carrying over disease spores, particularly blight. The used compost is perfectly good for brassicas, salad crops, or mixing into a raised bed. Refreshing it with a scoop of fresh compost and a handful of slow-release fertiliser helps too.

When should I plant potatoes in bags?

First earlies can go into bags from early spring once the risk of a hard frost has passed - typically late March to April in temperate climates, though this varies by zone and year. Bags are easier to protect than open ground: if frost is forecast, move them against the house wall or cover with fleece overnight. Check your local last-frost dates for the most accurate timing in your area.

A couple of bags in a sunny corner is all you need to get started. Plant a first early this season, harvest it in ten weeks, and I suspect you'll be measuring up your patio for more bags before the season is out. The method is straightforward, the results are genuinely satisfying, and fresh-dug potatoes taste nothing like the ones in a supermarket bag.
