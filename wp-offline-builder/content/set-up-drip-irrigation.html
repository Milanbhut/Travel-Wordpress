Last July I stood in my garden at 6 a.m., hose in hand, watering the same raised bed I'd watered the evening before because we'd had a hot, dry night. Fifteen minutes wasted before coffee. That was the morning I finally installed a drip system, and I haven't touched the hose for routine watering since.

A drip irrigation setup sounds more complicated than it is. The core idea is simple: slow, steady water delivered right at the root zone rather than sprayed across the foliage. Plants drink what they need, less water evaporates, and you get your mornings back.

This guide walks through the whole process - from choosing parts at the hardware store to turning on the tap for the first time. You don't need plumbing experience. You need a tape measure, a pair of scissors, and about two hours on a weekend.

What You'll Need Before You Start

Drip irrigation kits exist, and they're fine for a small bed. But buying components separately usually works out cheaper and gives you more flexibility. Here's the core shopping list:

  • Main supply line - 13 mm (½ inch) polyethylene tubing, sold by the metre. Get enough to run from your tap to the furthest corner of the garden, plus a bit extra.
  • Emitter tubing or driplines - thinner 4 mm (⅛ inch) tubing that branches off the main line to each plant.
  • Drip emitters - the small plastic nozzles that go on the end. They're rated by litres per hour (LPH). Start with 2 LPH for most vegetables and herbs; use 4 LPH for thirstier crops like tomatoes and squash.
  • Fittings - barbed connectors, tees, elbows, and end caps. These snap into the tubing without glue.
  • Backflow preventer - attaches to the tap and stops garden water from being siphoned back into the drinking supply. Non-negotiable if you're connecting to a mains tap.
  • Filter - a simple inline mesh filter stops grit from clogging your emitters.
  • Pressure regulator - domestic mains pressure is often too high for drip systems. A regulator set to around 1.5 bar protects your tubing.
  • Timer (optional but transformative) - a mechanical or digital hose timer turns the whole thing into a hands-off system.
Tip: Sketch Before You Shop

Draw a rough map of your beds and measure the distances. Add 20% extra tubing to account for curves, mistakes, and future expansion. It's far easier to trim excess tubing than to find you're 50 cm short mid-installation.

Step 1: Connect to Your Water Source

Thread the backflow preventer directly onto the outdoor tap, hand-tight. Attach the filter next, then the pressure regulator. The order matters - filter before regulator means grit doesn't reach the delicate pressure parts.

If you're using a timer, it goes between the tap and the backflow preventer. Most timers have a female inlet and male outlet, so the assembly reads: tap → timer → backflow preventer → filter → pressure regulator → main line.

Give everything a gentle quarter-turn with pliers once it's hand-tight. Don't overdo it - the fittings are plastic and can crack.

Step 2: Lay the Main Supply Line

Unroll the 13 mm tubing and run it along the edge of your bed or between rows. Use tubing stakes - U-shaped pegs sold alongside drip parts - to pin it flat every 60 cm or so. This keeps the line tidy and stops it being a trip hazard.

At corners, don't kink the tubing. Either use a barbed elbow fitting, or form a gentle curve (drip tubing has enough flexibility for a smooth bend if you keep the radius generous).

Cap the far end with an end cap or a figure-eight end closure. If you use an end cap, leave a short tail of tubing - 10 cm is enough - so you can flush the line by pulling the cap off at the end of each season.

Step 3: Add Your Emitters

This is the part that takes the most time, but it's satisfying work. Use a hole punch (usually included in kits, or sold separately for a couple of pounds) to pierce the main line where you want a drip point. Push a barbed connector into the hole - it should click in with firm thumb pressure.

Attach a short length of 4 mm tubing to the connector and run it to the base of the plant. Push an emitter onto the end and stake it so the tip sits just above soil level, ideally under the canopy of the plant.

Space emitters to match your plant spacing. For a bed of tomatoes 50 cm apart, one emitter per plant is usually enough. For densely planted herbs or salad crops, you might use two emitters per 30 cm section or switch to a pre-made drip tape instead.

Common Mistake

Placing emitters right against the stem. Keeping moisture pooled at the base of a stem encourages rot, especially in tomatoes, squash, and courgettes. Position the emitter 5-10 cm away from the main stem, at the drip line of the foliage.

Step 4: Test the System Before Burying Anything

Turn on the tap at a trickle first. Walk the whole line and check every connection. Look for spraying at barbed joints (the fitting isn't fully seated), dripping at threaded connections (add thread-seal tape), or emitters that aren't dripping at all (may be clogged - remove and rinse).

Let it run for ten minutes and watch where the wet patches form in the soil. They should appear as neat circles around each emitter. If water is pooling and running off, your emitter flow rate is too high for your soil type - swap 4 LPH emitters for 2 LPH ones and run the system longer instead.

Only once everything looks right should you stake the emitter tubing neatly, or cover the main line with a thin layer of mulch. Mulch over a drip system is actually ideal - it keeps evaporation low and the tubing protected from UV degradation.

Setting the Timer

Timing depends on your soil, climate, and what you're growing. As a rough starting point: sandy soils need shorter, more frequent watering cycles; clay soils absorb water slowly and do better with a longer, less frequent soak.

For raised beds with a mix of vegetables in a temperate UK summer, I run my system for 20 minutes every morning. In a heatwave, I add a second 15-minute run in late afternoon. In cooler, wetter periods - the kind of weather you'd be planning around if you're working through your spring garden checklist - I dial it back to every other day or skip entirely and let rainfall do the work.

Check soil moisture with your finger rather than trusting the schedule blindly. Push a finger 5 cm into the soil near an emitter; if it's still damp from yesterday's watering, you don't need to run the system today. A timer is a tool, not a substitute for observation.

End-of-Season Care

Drip systems are low-maintenance, but they do need a proper shutdown before frost. Polyethylene tubing handles cold reasonably well, but water left in the lines can crack fittings and freeze emitters.

Before the first frost - the same window when you'd be thinking about how to prepare your garden for winter - disconnect the timer and head fittings, open all the end caps, and let the lines drain. Store the timer and regulator indoors. The main tubing can usually stay in place if it's staked flat; it's the threaded metal fittings that are most vulnerable to frost damage.

Run a quick visual inspection in spring: look for cracks in fittings, any emitters that got knocked askew during winter digging, and whether any section of tubing has been punctured by a spade. Replace what needs replacing; it's cheap. Then flush the lines with the end caps off before reconnecting emitters.

What About Fruit Trees?

A drip system works well for establishing young fruit trees in their first two or three seasons. Run a loop of main line around the root zone rather than a single emitter - young trees need water spread across a wider area as their root system expands. Once a tree is established, it generally doesn't need supplemental irrigation in a normal UK climate, though that depends heavily on the rootstock it's grown on. If you're curious about how rootstocks affect a tree's water needs and eventual size, understanding tree rootstocks is worth a read before you plant.

How long should I run my drip system each day?

It depends on your soil, climate, and what you're growing. A reasonable starting point for a vegetable bed in warm weather is 20-30 minutes daily with 2 LPH emitters. Check soil moisture 5 cm down and adjust - the goal is consistently damp, not waterlogged. Sandy soils dry faster and may need a second short cycle; clay soils hold moisture longer and need less frequent watering.

Can I connect a drip system to a water butt instead of the mains?

Yes, with caveats. A raised water butt (at least 1 metre above ground level) can provide enough gravity pressure for low-flow drip systems. You won't need a pressure regulator, and flow rates will be lower - which actually suits drip irrigation well. Skip the backflow preventer since you're not connecting to the drinking supply, but do use a filter, as collected rainwater often carries debris that clogs emitters.

My emitters keep clogging. What's going wrong?

The most common culprits are grit in the water, algae build-up, and mineral deposits from hard water. Make sure your inline filter is in place and check it monthly - a blocked filter starves the whole system of pressure. For hard water areas, flush the lines at the end of each growing season and soak blocked emitters in white vinegar for an hour before rinsing and reinstalling. If clogging is persistent, upgrade to a finer mesh filter.

A drip system won't turn you into a perfect gardener, but it will remove one of the most time-consuming daily chores - and your plants will almost certainly be healthier for the consistent moisture. Set it up once, tweak it for a season, and it'll quietly do its job for years. That's a decent return on a free Saturday morning.
