You walk past your windowsill, coffee in hand, and something stops you. That pothos you've been quietly proud of - the one trailing down three shelves - has gone patchy. Two leaves, maybe three, the colour of old mustard. Your first instinct is to worry, and fair enough. But here's the thing: yellowing leaves are one of the most readable signals a plant can send. Once you know what to look for, the solution is usually staring you in the face.

I've diagnosed more yellow-leaf crises than I can count, from beginner spider plants to fussy fiddle-leaf figs that cost someone an embarrassing amount of money. The cause is almost always one of a handful of culprits: water, light, or nutrients. Get those three right and your plant will reward you with growth that's genuinely worth showing off.

This is a mistakes-and-fixes tour through the most common reasons leaves go yellow. Each section names the mistake, explains what's happening inside the plant, and gives you a concrete fix you can act on today.

Mistake 1: Overwatering - the most common culprit by far

Overwatering is responsible for more dead houseplants than anything else. The irony is that it usually comes from love - people want their plants to thrive, so they water on a schedule rather than on a signal.

When roots sit in waterlogged soil, they can't take up oxygen. They begin to rot. And once root rot sets in, the plant can't move water or nutrients upward - so the leaves turn yellow and eventually mushy. Lower leaves usually go first, which is your early warning.

The fix: stick your finger into the compost up to the second knuckle. If it's damp, wait. If it's dry, water thoroughly, then let it drain. Most tropical houseplants - monsteras, pothos, peace lilies - prefer the top inch or two of soil to dry out between waterings. If you're caring for something like a monstera and you haven't already settled into a rhythm, the monstera care guide walks through watering cadence in detail.

Root rot check

If you suspect overwatering has gone on a while, tip the plant gently out of its pot. Healthy roots are white or pale tan. Rotten roots are brown, black, and often smell musty. Trim away the rotten portions with clean scissors, let the roots air for an hour, then repot into fresh, well-draining compost.

Mistake 2: Underwatering - the other extreme

Less common than overwatering, but it happens. Underwatered plants go yellow in a slightly different way: the leaves often look dry and crispy at the edges before going fully yellow, and the soil will be bone dry and possibly pulling away from the sides of the pot.

The fix is simple but requires patience. Don't just pour a cup of water on - soak the pot thoroughly in a bucket or sink until water drains freely from the bottom, then let it drain fully. Repeat every few days until the plant starts to perk up. Some wilted leaves won't recover, but healthy new growth should follow within a few weeks.

Mistake 3: Wrong light - yellowing that moves around the plant

Light deficiency is sneaky because it doesn't hit the whole plant at once. If one side is yellowing while the other looks fine, your first question should be: where is the light coming from, and is it enough?

Most tropical houseplants evolved under a canopy - they like bright, indirect light. Too little, and they can't photosynthesize efficiently, so they shed leaves they can't afford to power. Too much direct sun and you'll see yellowing or bleaching in the leaves most exposed, often paired with brown scorched patches.

The fix: move the plant closer to a north- or east-facing window for soft light, or back from a south-facing window if it's getting direct afternoon sun. Rotate the pot a quarter turn every week or two so all sides get equal exposure. If your flat just doesn't have great natural light, some beginner-friendly houseplants are genuinely tolerant of low-light conditions and will save you a lot of frustration.

Mistake 4: Nutrient deficiency - usually nitrogen

If your watering and light are both dialled in but leaves are still going yellow - especially older leaves lower on the plant - the problem is often a lack of nitrogen. Nitrogen is what plants use to build chlorophyll, the green stuff. No nitrogen, no green.

This tends to happen most in plants that haven't been repotted for a year or more, because the potting compost simply runs out of available nutrients over time.

Feeding tip

During the growing season (roughly spring through early autumn), give your houseplants a balanced liquid fertiliser once a month. Always follow the label - more is not better, and over-fertilising can burn roots and cause its own yellowing. In winter, most plants rest and don't need feeding at all.

What about yellowing from the top down?

If new growth at the top is going yellow while lower leaves stay green, that points to an iron deficiency rather than nitrogen. Iron deficiency (chlorosis) shows as yellowing between the veins, with the veins themselves staying green. The fix is an iron-rich chelated fertiliser or, longer-term, checking the soil pH - iron becomes unavailable to plants in overly alkaline conditions.

Mistake 5: Cold drafts and temperature stress

Most houseplants come from warm climates and dislike sudden cold. A plant sitting near a single-glazed window in January, or directly in the path of an air-conditioning unit in summer, may start dropping yellow leaves within days of a cold snap or temperature shock.

Check the plant's location. Is it near an exterior door that opens regularly? Directly under a vent? Move it somewhere with more consistent temperatures - most tropical houseplants are happiest between around 15°C and 27°C (60-80°F), away from radiators and cold draughts alike.

Mistake 6: Pests - the cause people forget to check

Tiny pests like spider mites, fungus gnats (whose larvae attack roots), and scale insects can cause yellowing that looks exactly like a watering or nutrient problem. If you've corrected the obvious issues and still see decline, flip a leaf over and look at the underside. Spider mites leave fine webbing; scale looks like tiny brown bumps; mealybugs look like white fluff.

The fix depends on the pest. Neem oil solution (always follow label directions and test on a leaf first) handles many soft-bodied insects. Isolate any affected plant immediately to protect your other plants. For fungus gnats, let the soil dry more thoroughly between waterings - their larvae need moist soil to survive.

A note on toxicity

Some common houseplants - pothos, peace lily, dieffenbachia - are toxic if ingested by people or pets. If a curious child or pet chews on a plant and you're worried, contact your local poison control line or vet immediately rather than waiting.

Mistake 7: Root-bound plants that have outgrown their pot

A plant that's been in the same pot for several years may have roots circling the drainage holes or emerging from the top of the soil. When roots have no more room to grow, the plant struggles to take up water and nutrients evenly - and yes, yellow leaves follow.

Check by tipping the root ball out. If it's a dense mass of roots with little compost visible, it's time to go up a pot size. Choose a pot around 5-7cm larger in diameter, use fresh compost, and water in well. Spring is the ideal time for repotting - which also happens to be when bulbs and other spring plantings are stirring outdoors; the guide to spring-flowering bulbs is a good companion read if you're doing seasonal gardening jobs at the same time.

Should I remove yellow leaves from my houseplant?

Yes - once a leaf has gone fully yellow, it won't recover, and leaving it on the plant can invite fungal problems. Remove it cleanly at the stem with clean scissors or your fingers. Concentrate your energy on fixing the underlying cause so you don't lose more.

Why is only one leaf turning yellow while the rest of the plant looks healthy?

A single yellowing leaf, especially a lower or older one, is often just natural ageing - plants shed old leaves as they put energy into new growth. If it's just one leaf and the rest of the plant is perky and growing, don't panic. Watch for a week; if more follow, then investigate watering, light, or nutrients.

How quickly will my plant recover after I fix the problem?

It depends on what caused the yellowing and how long it went on. Minor issues like inconsistent watering can turn around within a few weeks once you establish a better rhythm. Root rot recovery takes longer - sometimes months - and not all plants make it. Be patient, keep conditions stable, and look for fresh new growth as your sign that the plant is bouncing back.

Yellow leaves are a conversation, not a catastrophe. Your plant is telling you something specific, and with a bit of observation you can usually work out what. Get the watering right first - it's the answer more often than not - and then work through light, feeding, and location. Most plants are more resilient than they look, and there's a real satisfaction in watching new green growth push through after you've figured out what was wrong.
