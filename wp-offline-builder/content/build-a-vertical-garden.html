My back garden is exactly four metres wide. I know this because I measured it in a mild panic the weekend I decided I wanted to grow tomatoes, herbs, climbing beans, and a couple of strawberry plants. Spoiler: the floor space wasn't happening. What saved me was the six-foot timber fence along the north-facing boundary - which I promptly ignored - and the south-facing brick wall on the other side, which became the most productive square footage in the whole plot.

Vertical gardening sounds like a design magazine concept, but the mechanics are genuinely simple. You are just redirecting where the plants grow - up, rather than out. A weekend afternoon is enough to have a structure in place and your first plants in soil. I've done it twice now, on two different walls, and the second attempt was considerably less embarrassing than the first.

This is the method I'd use if I were starting from scratch today. I'll flag the bits where I wasted money or time, so you can skip straight to the good part.

Step 1: Assess the Wall Before You Buy Anything

The single biggest mistake people make is falling in love with a planter kit at the garden centre before checking whether their wall can actually support it. A fully watered vertical planter can weigh more than you expect - soil is heavy, and fabric pockets hold moisture.

Check that your wall or fence is structurally sound. Timber fences need to be stable in the ground; a wobbling post means the structure will rock in the wind and stress plant roots. For masonry walls, look for crumbling mortar or damp patches that suggest drainage issues behind the wall.

Then note the sun exposure. A south-facing wall is prime real estate for fruiting plants and most herbs. East-facing gets morning light, which suits leafy greens and ferns nicely. North-facing is a real challenge - stick to ferns, mind-your-own-business, and shade-tolerant perennials if that's all you've got. Getting this step right saves you from replacing dead plants in three weeks.

Step 2: Choose a Structure That Matches Your Ambition

There are broadly three options, and each suits a different kind of gardener.

Freestanding Trellis or A-Frame

Good for renters or anyone who doesn't want to put holes in a wall. You can move it, rotate crops, and take it with you when you leave. The downside is that it eats a little floor space at the base, which is exactly what we're trying to protect.

Wall-Mounted Pockets or Modular Planters

These attach directly to the surface with screws and rawlplugs (for masonry) or timber screws (for fencing). Each pocket holds one plant and drains into the one below or onto the ground. They look smart, they're cheap, and they're genuinely good for herbs and trailing nasturtiums. Drainage matters here - make sure pockets have holes and aren't sitting on a flat base where water pools.

Wire or Cable Systems for Climbers

Tensioned horizontal wires fixed to the wall let climbing plants do the heavy lifting - quite literally. Beans, cucumbers, small climbing roses, and clematis all train easily along wires. This is my preferred approach because the plants become the structure; there's very little to buy. You'll need vine eyes, tensioning wire (galvanised or stainless to resist rust), and a decent drill. For structural fixings into masonry, if you're uncertain, it's worth checking with a builder or your local extension service - a fixing that fails under load is a frustrating afternoon.

Tip: Check toxicity before you plant

Some popular climbing plants - certain clematis species, for example - are toxic if ingested. If you have young children or pets with access to the garden, cross-check your plant choices with a reputable toxic-plant database before you commit. Your vet or local poison control line is the right call if you suspect an animal has eaten something questionable.

Step 3: Get the Growing Medium Right

Containers dry out faster than beds. This is not a maybe - it's physics. Vertical systems, especially fabric pockets, can dry out in a single warm afternoon in summer. You need a growing medium that holds moisture while still draining freely.

A mix of good-quality multipurpose compost and perlite (roughly 80/20) works well for most plants. Avoid garden soil in containers - it compacts, drains poorly, and usually brings weed seeds along for the ride. For edibles, use a peat-free compost where you can find it; your local conditions and climate zone will affect how quickly the mix breaks down and needs refreshing.

If you're planning a serious installation - eight or more pockets, or a large modular system - it's worth factoring in how you'll water regularly. Drip irrigation is not overkill for a vertical garden. Even a simple timer connected to a drip line makes the difference between thriving plants and a guilt-inducing brown wall.

Step 4: Pick Plants That Actually Work Vertically

Not everything does well in a vertical setup. Shallow-rooted, drought-tolerant, or naturally vining plants are your friends here.

  • Herbs: Basil, thyme, oregano, parsley, and chives all suit small pockets and don't need much root depth. I keep a six-pocket herb panel next to the kitchen door and it earns its wall space every time I cook.
  • Salad leaves: Lettuce, rocket, and spinach are perfect for this setup - shallow roots, quick harvest, and you can replant the pockets as you cut.
  • Climbing beans and peas: French beans and mange-tout are natural climbers. Sow them after your last frost date (check your local frost date calendar if you're unsure - getting this wrong is a rite of passage but also fixable) and they'll cover a trellis surprisingly quickly.
  • Trailing strawberries: Everbearing varieties like 'Albion' cascade nicely from pockets and produce fruit through summer. Slot them into the upper tiers so the berries hang where you can see them.
  • Nasturtiums: Edible flowers, pest-deterring properties, and near-indestructible. They fill gaps and look good doing it.

Avoid deep-rooted vegetables - parsnips, carrots, large tomato varieties - unless you have genuinely deep containers (30cm or more). They'll struggle, and struggling plants attract pests. I learned this with a row of cherry tomatoes in shallow pockets; they survived but they were not happy about it.

Step 5: Build It - The Actual Weekend Part

Set aside Saturday morning for fixings and structure, Saturday afternoon for filling and planting. Sunday is honestly for sitting in front of your new wall and feeling smug.

For wire systems: mark your wire positions with a pencil, drill pilot holes, insert vine eyes (use wall plugs in masonry), and run the wire tautly between them. If you're working on a ladder, keep it stable and have someone spot you - basic safety, but worth saying. For pocket systems: arrange them on the ground first to get spacing right before you start drilling.

Fill pockets or containers, leaving about 2cm at the top for watering. Plant, water thoroughly, and then check the drainage - water should run freely out of the base. If it pools, you need more drainage material or better-draining compost.

This is a great project for a weekend with variable energy levels, which is why it often appears on lists of satisfying one-weekend garden builds - you can stop and start without losing momentum, and the results are visible immediately.

Step 6: Maintain Without Making It a Job

A vertical garden lives and dies on watering consistency. Check soil moisture daily in warm weather - poke a finger into the top couple of centimetres. If it's dry, water it. If it's wet, leave it alone. Overwatering in pockets is a common error because the system looks like it needs constant attention when it doesn't.

Feed container plants every two weeks through the growing season with a balanced liquid fertiliser. Follow the product label - more fertiliser is not better, and overfeeding causes soft, leggy growth that's more vulnerable to pests. For most edibles, a general-purpose balanced feed works fine; fruiting plants (strawberries, beans) benefit from a higher-potassium formula once they start flowering.

At the end of the season, pull out annuals, top up the compost in pockets, and cut back any perennial climbers to tidy them. The same principles that apply to pruning fruit trees - cut clean, cut at the right time, and don't over-remove - translate reasonably well to woody climbers like climbing roses: leave enough healthy growth to bounce back in spring.

End-of-season checklist

Remove spent annuals. Top-dress pockets with fresh compost. Check fixings for rust or looseness. Cut back woody climbers to healthy buds. Store fabric pockets indoors if temperatures will drop hard - freezing can degrade the fabric faster than UV does.

How much weight can a fence support for a vertical garden?

This varies widely by fence type, age, and post depth - there's no single number that applies to every installation. As a rough guide, a stable six-foot timber fence with well-set posts can handle a modest pocket system with six to twelve small pockets, but heavier modular systems should ideally be attached to masonry or a purpose-built frame. When in doubt, ask a builder or fencing contractor to assess the structure before you fix anything heavy to it.

Can I build a vertical garden on a north-facing wall?

Yes, but your plant choices change significantly. North-facing walls in the northern hemisphere receive little to no direct sun, so fruiting plants and most herbs won't perform well. Ferns, ivy, hostas (in pots or very deep pockets), shade-tolerant lettuces, and ornamental foliage plants can work. Manage your expectations and treat it as a foliage display rather than a productive kitchen garden.

How often do I need to replace the soil in pocket planters?

Most pocket systems benefit from a top-up of fresh compost each spring, and a full refresh every two to three years. Compost breaks down over time, losing structure and drainage, and nutrients deplete quickly in small volumes of soil. Replacing roughly a third of the mix each season keeps the system productive without the effort of a full overhaul every year.

A vertical garden is one of those projects where the planning phase feels slightly more daunting than the reality. Once the first fixing is in the wall, the rest follows quickly. Give yourself a weekend, keep the plant list simple for the first season, and see what works in your specific spot before scaling up. You'll figure out your wall's quirks fast - mine taught me that south-facing brick stores heat like a storage radiator, which the basil loves and the lettuce absolutely does not.
