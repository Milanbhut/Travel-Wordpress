Last autumn I helped a neighbour set up her first compost bin. She had a tiny concrete-edged patch behind the kitchen, a pile of vegetable peelings she didn't know what to do with, and a vague worry that the whole thing would smell terrible. Three months later she was topping her raised beds with dark, crumbly compost - and wondering why she'd waited so long.

Starting a compost bin really is that straightforward. The science is simple: you are giving microbes, fungi, and small creatures the right conditions to break organic matter down into something your soil loves. Get the balance roughly right and the process runs itself.

The two things that trip people up are picking the wrong container and misjudging the ratio of wet, nitrogen-rich materials to dry, carbon-rich ones. Both are easy to fix once you understand the why behind each step.

Choosing the Right Bin for a Small Space

You don't need a sprawling three-bay system. A single enclosed plastic bin - the kind many councils sell cheaply - works well for a small backyard. It holds heat, keeps rodents out, and sits neatly in a corner.

Wooden slatted bays look attractive and allow better airflow, which speeds decomposition. A 60 cm × 60 cm footprint is enough. If aesthetics matter (and I understand if they do), cedar or untreated hardwood ages gracefully and won't leach anything worrying into your finished compost.

Avoid solid-sided metal containers with no ventilation - they tend to turn wet and anaerobic, which is where the bad smells come from. Whatever you choose, place it directly on soil, not on paving. Worms and beneficial organisms need a route in from the ground.

Siting tip

Choose a spot with partial shade. Full sun dries the pile out quickly in summer; full shade keeps it too cold in winter. A few hours of morning light is ideal.

What Goes In: Greens and Browns Explained

Every composting guide mentions greens and browns, and almost none of them explain the distinction clearly enough. Here is the short version: greens are wet, nitrogen-rich materials; browns are dry, carbon-rich ones. Microbes need both.

Greens include vegetable and fruit peelings, coffee grounds, tea bags (remove the staple), fresh grass clippings, and green garden trimmings. Browns include cardboard torn into pieces, dried leaves, paper bags, straw, and woody prunings shredded small.

The rough target is two or three parts browns to one part greens by volume. I think of it as a lasagne: a layer of kitchen scraps, then a roughly equal or deeper layer of cardboard and dry leaves. You don't need scales or formulas - just make sure the pile never looks like a wet heap of food alone.

  • Greens to add freely: vegetable peelings, fruit scraps, coffee grounds, fresh weeds (before they set seed), grass clippings
  • Browns to add freely: cardboard (remove tape), egg boxes, dried leaves, paper towels, wood chip from untreated timber
  • Add sparingly: cooked food, citrus peel (slows decomposition), wood ash (raises pH - a handful is fine)
  • Keep out: meat, fish, dairy, diseased plant material, dog or cat waste, anything coated in persistent pesticides

How to Build the Pile from Scratch

Start with a 10-15 cm layer of coarse browns at the base - woody prunings or scrunched cardboard. This creates airflow from the bottom and stops the first layers from sitting in any moisture that collects.

Add your first batch of kitchen scraps on top, then cover with two to three times as much torn cardboard or dried leaves. Water lightly if the material feels very dry - the pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge, damp but not dripping.

Each time you add kitchen scraps, cover them with browns. That one habit prevents almost every smell complaint I've ever heard. Exposed food is what attracts flies and produces odour; buried food breaks down quietly and quickly.

Keeping It Active: Turning and Moisture

Decomposition happens fastest when the pile has air and consistent moisture. Turning the pile every two to four weeks introduces oxygen, which energises aerobic microbes. I use a long-handled garden fork and work from the outside in, trying to get the cooler outer material into the warmer centre.

You'll know the pile is working when the centre feels warm to the touch - noticeably warmer than the air around it. In a well-balanced heap this can happen within a week of starting. If it's cold and inert, it usually means not enough nitrogen (add more greens), not enough moisture (add a little water), or both.

In a dry summer I check the pile weekly and add a watering-can's worth if it feels dusty. Good compost doesn't need as much attention as people fear; it mostly just needs you to notice and nudge when something's off.

No-turn composting

Short on time? You can leave the pile undisturbed for six to twelve months and still get compost - it just takes longer. This cold composting method suits people who add material in small amounts and aren't in a rush. The result is just as good.

Troubleshooting Common Problems

It smells like ammonia

This means too many greens, not enough browns. The pile is nitrogen-heavy. Add a generous layer of torn cardboard or dried leaves, turn it once, and the smell should ease within a day or two.

It smells like rotting eggs

A sulphur smell means the pile has gone anaerobic - probably too wet and compacted, not enough air. Turn it thoroughly and work in dry material. If the base is waterlogged, fork it up to let air reach the bottom.

Nothing seems to be happening

The pile is probably too dry, too cold, or too heavily weighted towards browns. Add green material, water lightly, and give it a turn. In winter, decomposition slows dramatically in cold climates - this is normal. The pile isn't dead, just resting until temperatures rise.

I can see flies

Fruit flies around the bin usually mean exposed food scraps. Always bury food under a layer of browns. A fine-mesh lid on your kitchen caddy and a layer of soil or finished compost over new additions will both help.

Healthy compost does attract some small invertebrates - woodlice, millipedes, the occasional slow worm if you're lucky. These are beneficial, not problems. They help break material down and indicate a well-functioning system.

Knowing When It's Ready

Finished compost is dark brown, crumbly, and smells like damp woodland - earthy and pleasant. You shouldn't be able to identify what went in. If you can still see recognisable peelings or cardboard, it needs more time.

Most home compost bins produce usable material in three to six months in warm weather, longer in winter. I usually run two sections - one I'm actively filling, one that's resting - so there's always a batch maturing without me having to hold back new additions.

Once it's ready, spread it on your beds in spring or autumn. Dig it in lightly or leave it as a mulch and let the worms do the rest. A 5 cm layer makes a noticeable difference to soil structure and water retention within a single season. If you're growing vegetables - I find it especially rewarding when growing potatoes in bags, where good compost mixed into the medium pays back richly at harvest time - you'll want to keep the supply going year-round.

Good compost also helps create a resilient garden ecosystem. Healthy soil supports diverse plant populations, which in turn supports populations of predatory insects that keep pest numbers in check. When you do spot something concerning on your leaves, it's worth learning to identify common garden pests accurately before reaching for any treatment. And when you do need to act, natural pest control methods fit neatly alongside an organic composting practice - because a garden fed on home compost tends to need less intervention overall.

Can I compost in a flat or apartment with no garden?

Yes - a small worm bin (vermicomposting) works well indoors or on a balcony. Worms process kitchen scraps quickly and produce a concentrated worm casting that's excellent for pot plants and window boxes. Keep the bin out of direct sun and frost, and avoid adding citrus, onion, or strong-smelling foods in large amounts as worms find them unpleasant.

How do I stop my compost bin attracting rats or mice?

The best deterrent is a bin with a secure base and lid, sited away from fences or walls that provide cover. Never add meat, fish, dairy, or cooked food with sauces. Burying fresh scraps under several centimetres of browns and turning the pile regularly both reduce the scent signal that attracts rodents. A hardware cloth base fixed beneath the bin adds a further barrier.

Can I add weeds to the compost bin?

Annual weeds pulled before they set seed are fine and make good greens. Perennial weeds with persistent roots - bindweed, ground elder, couch grass - are trickier. A domestic heap rarely gets hot enough to reliably kill them, and adding them risks spreading the problem. Either dry them in the sun until completely desiccated, or set them aside for your council green waste collection. Weeds that have already set seed should also go to the council bin, not yours.

Starting a compost bin is one of those changes that feels small at first and quietly transforms the way you garden. You stop seeing kitchen scraps as waste and start seeing them as raw material. The soil improves, the plants notice, and the whole system starts to feel connected in a way that's genuinely satisfying. Give it a few months - the results will speak for themselves.
