By mid-March I had seventeen browser tabs open, a spreadsheet with colour-coded planting zones, and a raised bed that still existed only as a sketch on graph paper. That was year one, month three. I had been "planning" since January.

I'm Arjun. I have a 6-by-9-metre plot behind a terraced house, heavy clay soil, and a tendency to overcomplicate things before doing anything. This is the honest record of how that first year actually went - not the version where everything works out beautifully in April, but the one with the July panic, the irrigation rethink, and the shrub I nearly killed twice.

If you're standing at the edge of a neglected patch wondering where to start, this might help. Or at least make you feel better about your own spreadsheet.

January and February: The Planning Trap

I spent the first two months researching instead of doing. Not entirely wasted - I learned my hardiness zone, identified the clay problem, and figured out that the north-facing corner gets roughly three hours of sun in winter and nearly seven in summer. Useful information.

What I got wrong was scope. I planned for twelve raised beds. I built two. That ratio is, in my experience, about right for most first-year gardeners. The gap between what looks manageable on paper and what you can actually maintain while holding down a job is significant.

The one genuinely good thing I did in February was print the spring garden checklist and work backwards from it. Knowing what tasks were coming in April meant I could prep soil in late February when the ground wasn't frozen solid.

March: Soil First, Everything Else Second

Clay soil is not a death sentence. It holds nutrients well and stays moist longer than sandy ground. The problem is drainage and compaction - both of which I had in abundance.

I spent three weekends in March digging in grit and homemade compost. Not the most glamorous work, but it's the kind of investment that pays off quietly all year. By the time I put seedlings in, the soil passed the basic squeeze test: a handful formed a ball, but broke apart easily rather than staying in a solid lump.

I also marked out permanent paths with wooden boards. Compacting the soil you actually want to grow in is one of the most common first-year mistakes - if you step on your beds regularly, you undo the drainage work you did in March pretty fast.

Soil prep tip

Work organic matter into clay soil when it's moist but not waterlogged. If it sticks to your boots in clumps, wait a few days. Working wet clay damages its structure and leaves you with dense, airless clods that are harder to fix than what you started with.

April and May: First Plantings - and First Lessons

I put in tomatoes too early. Everyone does this at least once. A warm spell in late April convinced me that the last frost was behind us. It wasn't. I lost four out of six seedlings to a cold snap in early May and had to start again from shop-bought plants.

The courgettes, on the other hand, were unstoppable. Planted in mid-May directly into the ground (not into modules first, which is what I'd planned), they barely paused. By June they were producing faster than I could give them away to neighbours.

Spring is also when I realised how quickly hand-watering becomes a burden. Twice a day for six weeks through a dry May nearly put me off the whole project. That frustration is what pushed me to finally look at irrigation - and following up on that in summer was one of the better decisions I made all year.

June: The Irrigation Rethink

I installed a basic drip system in June, during a dry stretch, which is exactly the right time to feel the urgency. I'd read about it for months and kept putting it off because it sounded complicated. It wasn't.

The hardest part was mapping the layout before buying anything. Once I had that sorted, the actual install took a Saturday afternoon. The full drip irrigation setup process covers the specifics - emitter spacing, flow rates, connecting to a timer - better than I can summarise here. What I'll say is: it cut my watering time from roughly 40 minutes a day to about five, and the plants did better with consistent moisture than they ever did with my erratic hand-watering.

The other June revelation was mulch. A thick layer of straw around the tomatoes and peppers kept the soil cool, reduced watering frequency further, and mostly suppressed the weeds that had been colonising the beds since April.

July and August: Peak Season, Peak Chaos

This is the part no one writes about in the gardening books: summer is overwhelming. Everything ripens at once, pests appear, and you realise the beds need weeding again despite the mulch.

I had aphids on the broad beans (severe), blackfly on the runner beans (moderate), and something - still unidentified - that chewed holes in the chard leaves in patterns that looked almost deliberate. I used a diluted soap spray on the aphids, checking the product label and applying in the evening to avoid harming bees. It helped, but didn't eliminate the problem. The beans recovered anyway.

What surprised me most was that the messy corner of the garden - the one I hadn't got round to clearing - turned into a habitat for ground beetles and hoverflies, both of which are genuinely useful. I've since stopped feeling guilty about that corner.

What actually thrived

  • Courgettes (relentlessly)
  • Dwarf French beans (low maintenance, heavy yield)
  • Cherry tomatoes - better than large varieties in my climate
  • Chard (despite the mystery pest)

What I'd do differently

  • Start fewer varieties, grow more of each
  • Thin brassica seedlings sooner - I left them too crowded
  • Get the drip irrigation in by May, not June

September and October: Winding Down and Looking Ahead

Autumn felt slower, which I appreciated. I cleared beds as crops finished, added another round of compost, and planted garlic in late October - a ridiculously low-effort crop that makes you feel like you're getting away with something.

I also used October to make the permanent planting decisions I'd been putting off since spring. The back boundary needed something structural that wouldn't demand constant attention. After looking at what other gardeners in similar conditions had used, I ended up choosing two of the varieties from the low-maintenance shrub recommendations that suit exposed spots and clay soils. They went in November, one step ahead of the worst frosts.

The Takeaways: What One Year Actually Taught Me

Year-one lessons

Start smaller than you think you need to. Fix the soil before anything else. Get irrigation sorted in spring, not summer. Leave some mess - it earns its keep ecologically. And accept that roughly a third of what you try will not work in year one, and that's normal.

The garden is not the same patch it was in January. The soil is genuinely better. There are two raised beds that work reliably. The back boundary has structure. The drip system means I can go away for a week without everything dying.

That feels like enough for year one. Not the twelve-bed masterplan. Enough.

How much time does a first-year vegetable garden actually take each week?

Realistically, budget around four to six hours a week during peak season (late spring through summer), dropping to one or two hours in autumn and near-zero in winter. That includes watering, weeding, harvesting, and occasional pest checks. A drip system with a timer can save two to three hours a week on its own during dry spells.

Is clay soil really a problem, or can you just work with it?

You can absolutely work with clay - it's actually nutrient-rich and holds moisture well. The main issues are drainage and compaction. Working in grit and organic matter over one or two seasons improves both significantly. Avoid walking on beds and never dig it when it's waterlogged, and clay becomes an asset rather than a liability.

What's the single most important thing to get right in year one?

Soil preparation, without question. Seeds and seedlings can be replaced cheaply; poor soil affects everything you grow for years. A season spent improving drainage and organic content pays off more than any particular variety choice or clever technique. Get the foundation right and most other problems become manageable.

A first-year garden is less about perfection and more about learning the specific conditions of your own patch. Every plot has its quirks - the shady corner, the spot that stays wet, the microclimate near the fence that's always a week ahead of everywhere else. Year one is how you find them. Year two is when you start using them.
