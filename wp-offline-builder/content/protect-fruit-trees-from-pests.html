Last summer, a neighbour wandered over just as I was inspecting my young apple trees. She'd lost nearly every plum to codling moth the season before - not a few plums, almost all of them. She wasn't doing anything obviously wrong. She just hadn't put a system in place early enough.

Protecting fruit trees is less about reacting to damage and more about layering defences before the threats arrive. The pests and birds don't wait. Codling moth lays eggs in early summer. Aphid colonies build through spring. Bullfinches strip flower buds from stone fruits all through winter. Getting ahead of the calendar is half the work.

This is a long game. One good season of protection builds habits and knowledge that make the next season easier. Here's how to build that system, step by step.

Know Your Orchard's Timing

No two orchards face exactly the same pressures. A garden in a mild coastal region may deal with different aphid species or fruit fly variants than one further inland. Climate, hardiness zone, and what's growing in neighbouring gardens all shift the picture.

The best starting point is a simple seasonal log. Note when you first see aphid colonies forming, when blossom sets, when you spot the first codling moth adults. After two or three seasons, patterns become clear. Your month-by-month garden task log is the natural place to record these observations - pen them in each year and you'll build an accurate forecast for your own plot.

Knowing your rootstock also shapes how you approach protection. Dwarf and semi-dwarf trees are easier to net and treat precisely because you can reach them without a ladder. If you're not sure what you're working with, understanding tree rootstocks will help you read the tree's eventual size and manage it accordingly - including choosing the right barrier systems for the height.

Start with Physical Barriers

Physical barriers are the most ecologically sound tool in the orchard. No residues, no harm to beneficial insects, no label to read. Done correctly, they're also highly effective.

Fruit tree grease bands are placed around the trunk and any supporting stakes in mid-autumn and left through early spring. They stop wingless female winter moths climbing the bark to lay eggs in the canopy. Wrap them tightly and check monthly that gaps haven't opened up where bark is rough.

Netting is the most versatile barrier. Fine insect mesh (roughly 1mm aperture) excludes coding moth, plum fruit moth, and sawfly if fitted when blossom opens. Larger bird netting (19mm or so) keeps starlings and pigeons off the ripening fruit. The key detail: always anchor the base of the net so birds can't work their way underneath. I've seen well-intentioned netting trap more birds than it protected against because the edges were loose.

Safety note

Check nets daily once birds are active near the tree. Entangled birds panic quickly and can injure themselves. Use wildlife-friendly fine-mesh rather than wide-weave nets where possible, and remove netting as soon as the harvest is complete.

Copper tape on raised beds or containers can deter slugs that travel up the pot to reach low-hanging fruit on dwarf trees. It's a small edge, but in a wet season it's worth having.

Use Traps to Monitor and Reduce Populations

Pheromone traps are not a silver bullet. I want to be honest about that. What they do reliably is tell you when a pest is flying so you can time other interventions accurately. A codling moth trap hung in the tree canopy from late spring onwards lets you track adult moth activity - and your intervention window opens roughly 10 days after the first catch.

Sticky yellow traps catch winged aphids, plum fruit moth, and other small insects. They're monitoring tools rather than population control at orchard scale, but in a small garden they do reduce numbers meaningfully.

Glue traps on stakes near the base of the tree can catch vine weevil adults. Vine weevil damage on fruit trees is less dramatic than it is on strawberries, but container-grown trees are vulnerable because the larvae attack roots confined in a pot.

Encourage Natural Predators

A healthy orchard is one where pest populations are kept in check by a web of predators and parasitoids - not just by your interventions. This takes time to establish, and it's the long-game thinking that rewards patience.

Blue tits and great tits eat extraordinary numbers of caterpillars during the breeding season. Provide nest boxes in or near the orchard, and avoid anything that might discourage them from foraging in the canopy. Lacewings and ladybirds consume aphids - both are attracted to an undergrowth of umbellifers like fennel or cow parsley around the base of the trees. Even low-maintenance flowering shrubs planted near the orchard can support hoverfly populations whose larvae are voracious aphid predators.

Avoid broad-spectrum insecticide sprays during flowering. They kill pollinators and natural enemies indiscriminately. If you must spray, choose evening, when bees are not active, and select the least-toxic formulation available. Always follow the product label and local regulations.

Time Any Sprays for Maximum Effect, Minimum Harm

There are times when a well-chosen spray is the most practical option. Winter tar oil washes (where still available and approved in your region) smother overwintering aphid eggs and scale insects on the bark. Apply them when the tree is fully dormant and after leaf fall - check your regional regulations first, as some products are restricted.

Kaolin clay sprays form a physical particle barrier on fruit and foliage that irritates and deters many insects without being toxic. They need reapplying after rain, which is the main inconvenience.

Biological controls deserve more attention than they typically get. Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) is a naturally occurring bacterium that, when ingested by caterpillars, prevents feeding. It has a short residual life, is harmless to birds and mammals, and works well against winter moth caterpillars and codling moth larvae if timed correctly - within the first 10 days after egg hatch.

Timing tip

If you're using any spray, biological or otherwise, keep a note of the date and what pest pressure you saw that prompted it. After a few seasons you'll see whether your spray timing is ahead of the problem or behind it - and you can adjust.

Manage the Ground Beneath the Trees

This step gets overlooked because it feels too indirect. But a tidy strip of clear ground directly beneath the canopy does real work. Woolly aphids overwinter on bark and roots; vine weevil pupae hide in soil; codling moth caterpillars pupate under bark and in nearby ground debris. Removing loose bark and keeping the base of the trunk clear, then applying a light mulch away from direct bark contact, disrupts several of these cycles at once.

Mulch is still worthwhile - it suppresses weeds competing for nutrients and moderates soil moisture during dry spells. Just keep it a few centimetres away from the trunk to reduce the risk of rot and the cosy hiding spots that some pests exploit.

Protect Young Trees from Deer, Rabbits, and Squirrels

Mammal damage is different in character from insect or bird damage - it tends to be sudden, severe, and demoralising. A rabbit can ring-bark a young tree below the snow line overnight and kill it outright.

Spiral tree guards protect against rabbits and voles on single-stemmed young trees. They're inexpensive and clip on and off easily for inspection. For deer pressure, a proper post-and-wire guard around each tree, at least 1.2 metres tall, is the only reliably effective option. Squirrel pressure on nuts and stone fruit is harder to manage; fine netting at ripening time and harvest timing (picking slightly early and ripening indoors) are your most practical tools.

When is the best time to put up fruit tree netting?

For insect exclusion, fit fine mesh netting as the blossom begins to open in spring - before adult moths are flying and laying eggs. For bird protection, put up larger-mesh netting as the fruit starts to colour and swell. Time it too early and you may impede pollinating insects during flowering.

Is it safe to use pesticide sprays near pets or children?

Follow the product label precisely - it will specify any re-entry intervals and precautions. Many products are safe once dry, but some require longer exclusion periods. Store all products locked away out of reach. If you suspect a pet or child has been exposed to a pesticide, contact your local poison control line or vet immediately rather than waiting for symptoms.

My fruit always has grubs inside - what's causing it and what can I do?

Internal grubs in apples and pears are most often codling moth larvae; in plums and cherries, plum fruit moth or cherry fruit fly. The damage is done early - by the time you see it at harvest, the larvae have already entered and left. The fix is preventive: pheromone traps to time your intervention, followed by fine-mesh netting or a well-timed biological spray the following season, before egg hatch.

A protected orchard is built over years, not in a single frantic afternoon before the birds arrive. Start with the basics - grease bands in autumn, netting at blossom time, a pheromone trap or two to help you read the season - and add more layers as you learn what your own plot faces. The trees, given a fair chance, will do the rest.
