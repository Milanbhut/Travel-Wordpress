Last spring I watched a neighbour pull up her tomato seedlings three weeks after planting them, convinced the variety was wrong. The leaves were pale, the stems were spindly, and nothing she did made any difference. She bought new plants, the same variety, put them in the same spot - and got the same result. The soil was the problem, not the tomatoes.

Depleted soil does not announce itself with a sign. It sends quieter messages: yellowing leaves, waterlogged beds that never seem to drain, vegetables that fruit poorly no matter how much you water. Once you learn to read those signals, you can stop chasing symptoms and actually fix what's wrong.

This is a mistakes-and-fixes walkthrough. For each warning sign, I'll explain why it happens and what you can do about it - starting with the simplest, cheapest remedies first.

1. Water Pools on the Surface and Stays There

After a decent rain, your bed should absorb moisture within a few minutes. If puddles linger for an hour or more, you're looking at compacted soil or a layer of hardpan beneath the surface. Compaction squeezes out the air pockets that roots and soil organisms depend on.

The fix is rarely as dramatic as you'd expect. Start by loosening the top six to eight inches with a broadfork or garden fork - avoid rotary tillers, which can actually worsen compaction over time by pulverising soil structure. Then work in a generous layer of mature compost. Compost opens the soil, feeds the microbes, and improves drainage simultaneously.

If the pooling persists after two seasons of amendment, the problem may be structural. That's when it's worth considering building a raised garden bed to give your plants a fresh start above the problem layer entirely.

A note on timing

Never work compacted soil when it's waterlogged - you'll make compaction worse. Wait until it's moist but not sticky, then squeeze a handful: it should crumble apart when you open your palm.

2. Pale or Yellow Leaves That Aren't a Disease

Yellowing leaves have a long list of possible causes - pests, overwatering, root rot - but when multiple plants in the same bed show the same symptom and you've ruled out the obvious culprits, the soil itself is often deficient. Nitrogen deficiency turns leaves a uniform pale green to yellow, starting from the older leaves at the bottom. Iron or magnesium deficiency shows as yellow between green veins.

Before you reach for any fertiliser, get a soil test. Many county extension offices offer inexpensive testing, and home kits cover the basics: pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. pH matters enormously here - soil that's too acidic or too alkaline locks up nutrients that are technically present, making them unavailable to plants no matter what you add on top.

Once you know what's missing, you can choose the right response. Understanding the difference between adding a quick nutrient fix and genuinely feeding the soil is worth taking seriously - if you haven't already, the comparison of compost versus fertiliser is a useful place to start. For most deficiency cases, building organic matter through compost is the slower but more durable answer.

3. Almost Nothing Is Living in There

Dig down about six inches in healthy garden soil and you should find earthworms - at least a handful per square foot in a well-managed bed. You should also see the tiny white threads of fungal mycelium and the general sense that the soil is alive and active. If you come up with nothing but dry, pale, or grey-brown soil and not a single worm, that's a red flag.

Soil biology drives almost everything good that happens underground: decomposition, nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and the formation of stable soil structure. When the biology collapses, the whole system slows down.

The most common killers of soil biology are repeated synthetic fertiliser use without organic matter, pesticide overuse, and letting soil sit bare between seasons. Cover crops - even a simple sowing of winter rye or clover - feed the biology and protect the surface. Compost additions, meanwhile, re-inoculate the bed with beneficial organisms. Give it a full season and you'll start seeing worms again.

4. Your Vegetables Fruit Poorly Despite Good Weather

You did everything right: consistent watering, decent sun, proper spacing. The plants look reasonable. But the tomatoes are sparse, the courgettes are small, and the peppers gave you six fruits all season. Low productivity like this, when weather and care aren't the issue, often points to poor soil structure or nutrient imbalance - particularly a phosphorus shortfall, which directly affects flowering and fruiting.

It can also point to soil that's nutritionally narrow. Beds that receive the same synthetic fertiliser blend every year can end up out of balance: the main three nutrients (N-P-K) are present, but trace elements like calcium, boron, or zinc are gone. Plants need those in small amounts, and their absence shows in the harvest, not in the leaves.

Diversifying your inputs is the most practical fix. Compost brings trace elements that synthetic blends miss. Adding well-aged manure or a balanced organic fertiliser once a year fills in other gaps. Always follow the product label and your soil test results when applying any amendments - more is not always better, and some elements become toxic at high concentrations.

5. The Surface Crusts Over Between Waterings

If your soil forms a hard crust that cracks as it dries, you're dealing with low organic matter combined with fine-textured soil, often made worse by rain impact. That crust is not just cosmetic: it repels water, blocks seedling emergence, and signals that the topsoil has lost its structure.

The most effective long-term fix is mulch. A two-to-four inch layer of organic mulch - straw, shredded leaves, wood chips - keeps the surface from drying out, protects it from rain impact, and feeds the soil as it breaks down. The full case for mulch goes beyond just crusting: consistent mulching rebuilds organic matter faster than almost any other simple practice, as explored in more depth at the guide to mulching benefits.

Quick fix for surface crusting

Before mulching, scratch the surface with a hand cultivator to break the existing crust. Water gently, then apply mulch while the soil is still moist. Keep mulch two to three inches away from plant stems to avoid rot.

6. A Strange or Sour Smell When You Dig

Healthy soil smells earthy - that distinctive fresh scent comes largely from a compound called geosmin, produced by beneficial soil bacteria. If your soil smells sour, swampy, or like rotten eggs, you have an anaerobic problem: pockets where oxygen can't reach, allowing different bacteria to take over and produce sulphur compounds.

This is most common in chronically waterlogged beds, in containers that sit in saucers full of water, or in areas where someone has buried fresh grass clippings or uncomposted organic matter. The fix involves addressing drainage first (see the section on pooling water above), then aerating and adding well-made compost to restore the microbial community.

If you've been adding fresh kitchen scraps or lawn clippings directly to a bed, stop. Raw organic matter in the wrong stage of decomposition creates exactly these anaerobic pockets. Compost it properly first, or use it as surface mulch only.

7. Weeds You've Never Seen Before Are Suddenly Thriving

Experienced growers pay attention to which weeds show up. Dock and horsetail often indicate poorly drained, acidic, compacted ground. Shepherd's purse and chickweed can signal high nitrogen. Plantain tends toward compacted, underworked soil. Weeds are opportunists, but they're also indicators - they tell you what conditions your soil is currently offering.

Rather than simply pulling them and moving on, take a minute to notice which ones dominate. That information, combined with a soil test, gives you a clearer picture of what's actually depleted or imbalanced. The fix follows from the diagnosis: amend for pH if the test shows it's off, add organic matter if it's compaction and low fertility, improve drainage if it's waterlogged species taking hold.

How quickly can I improve unhealthy soil?

It depends on how depleted the soil is and what you add. Adding compost and mulch produces noticeable improvement within one growing season for most issues - better drainage, more earthworm activity, healthier plant growth. Rebuilding genuine soil structure and biology after years of neglect is more like a two-to-three year project. Starting now still matters: every season of amendment adds up.

Do I need a soil test, or can I just add compost?

Compost is almost always a safe first step - it rarely does harm and usually helps. But a soil test pays for itself if you've had persistent problems. It tells you the pH and which specific nutrients are low or excessive, so you're not guessing. Applying lime to soil that doesn't need it, for example, can lock out nutrients and make things worse.

My soil looks dark and rich - can it still be depleted?

Yes, absolutely. Dark colour usually indicates organic matter, which is a good sign, but it doesn't tell you anything about pH balance, trace element levels, soil biology, or compaction. A bed that's been heavily mulched for years might look gorgeous and still be depleted of specific nutrients or harbouring drainage problems below the surface. Colour is one data point, not the whole story.

Tired soil is recoverable - that's the most reassuring thing I can tell you. The warning signs above aren't reasons to panic; they're information. Start with a soil test, add compost, protect the surface with mulch, and give it a season. Plants are more forgiving than we give them credit for, and so is soil, once you give it what it needs.
