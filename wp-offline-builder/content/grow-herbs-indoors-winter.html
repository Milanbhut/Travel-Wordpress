By late November, the kitchen windowsill at my friend Clara's flat had become something of a shrine. Three terracotta pots, a clip-on grow light angled over them like a reading lamp, fresh sprigs of thyme still going strong. She'd cracked the code that most of us miss in October: moving herbs indoors isn't just about survival - it's a design opportunity and a daily pleasure.

Winter herbs don't have to be leggy, yellowing, half-dead things gasping on the sill. With a little intention around light, temperature, and airflow, you can harvest sprigs of rosemary for roasted potatoes and a fistful of parsley for scrambled eggs well into February. That's the vision I want you to hold onto as we work through this together.

The principles here apply whether you're wintering over existing garden plants or starting fresh from a nursery pot. Let's set the scene, then walk through each step.

Choose Your Herbs Wisely

Not every herb wants to be inside in winter, and that's worth knowing upfront. Mediterranean herbs - rosemary, thyme, oregano, and sage - are adapted to cool, dry conditions and handle the transition well. Basil, on the other hand, is a tropical plant that despises cold windowsills and draughts. It needs warmth above almost everything else.

Mint is enthusiastic to a fault; it'll grow indoors but tends to sprawl and can harbour aphids if ventilation is poor. Chives are reliably tidy and produce slender, onion-flavoured leaves with minimal fuss. Parsley is a biennial that slows in low light but rarely dies on you if you water carefully.

My shortlist for beginners: thyme, chives, and parsley. They're forgiving, genuinely useful in the kitchen, and don't need a south-facing window to survive. Once you're confident with those, rosemary and basil are the natural next step.

Get the Light Right - This Is the Big One

Winter light through a north- or east-facing window is rarely enough for herbs that evolved in full Mediterranean sun. A south- or west-facing sill gets you closest to adequate, but even then, the sun angle in December means far fewer usable hours than you'd imagine.

A dedicated grow light changes everything. You don't need an elaborate rig - a simple LED strip or a small clip-on panel positioned 15-25 cm above the foliage, running for 14-16 hours per day, will do the job beautifully. I use a timer socket so I never have to think about it: on at 6 am, off at 9 pm.

Light tip

Rotate pots a quarter-turn every few days so all sides of the plant get equal exposure. Herbs leaning toward the light are telling you they're not getting enough - listen to them.

If you're already maintaining a broader indoor plant collection, you may already have a grow light shelf you can incorporate herbs into. Herbs sit happily alongside most tropical foliage plants at the same light levels.

Temperature and Air Circulation Matter More Than You Think

Mediterranean herbs want cool nights and moderate days - roughly 10-15 °C overnight and no warmer than 20-22 °C during the day. The average centrally-heated home is often too warm and too dry for rosemary and thyme to thrive long-term. They'll survive, but they tend to go soft and flavourless without a cool rest period.

If you have an unheated but frost-free porch or a cool bedroom, that's prime real estate for rosemary in winter. Alternatively, move the pot away from the radiator and closer to the glass in the evening - just don't let it touch the cold pane, which can scorch leaves overnight.

Air circulation is quietly important. Still, humid air encourages mould, particularly on basil and parsley. A ceiling fan on its lowest setting, or simply cracking a nearby window for ten minutes each morning, keeps things healthy without chilling the plants.

Watering: Less Is Almost Always More

Overwatering kills more indoor herbs in winter than any other single mistake. Plant metabolism slows when light levels drop, so roots aren't drinking nearly as fast as they would in June. The compost should feel barely moist before you water again - not bone dry, not soggy.

The finger-test rule: push your index finger an inch into the compost. If it feels damp at that depth, wait two more days and check again. Clay pots dry faster than plastic or ceramic, which is actually an advantage here since they're more forgiving of occasional overwatering.

Drainage is non-negotiable. Herbs sitting in a saucer of water will develop root rot within a couple of weeks. Empty saucers after watering or, better still, water in the sink and return the pot only once it's stopped dripping.

Feed Lightly, Not Often

Most herbs don't need feeding from November through February. Growth is slow, and pushing it with fertiliser in low light tends to produce weak, pale, stretched growth that's more susceptible to pests. Hold off entirely unless the plant looks genuinely hungry - pale leaves on otherwise healthy plants are sometimes a feeding cue.

From late February onward, as light levels begin to creep back up, a half-strength liquid feed every three to four weeks is plenty. Follow the product label on whatever fertiliser you use, and lean toward the lower end of the recommended dose for slow-growing winter herbs. A balanced, general-purpose liquid feed works fine; there's no need for specialist herb products.

How to Harvest Without Wrecking the Plant

The instinct to pull whole stems or strip leaves from the bottom is understandable but counterproductive. Herbs regenerate from the tips, so cutting or pinching the growing ends encourages branching and more leaves over time.

For thyme and rosemary: snip 5-8 cm from the tips of young growth, leaving at least two-thirds of each stem intact. For parsley: harvest outer stems at the base, leaving the centre crown and young inner leaves to keep growing. For basil: always cut just above a leaf node to trigger two new shoots where one was.

The design principle here is the same as for training cut-flower garden plants - every cut is a decision about future shape, not just today's harvest. Think three weeks ahead when you snip.

Common Problems and Quick Fixes

Yellowing Leaves

Usually overwatering or insufficient light. Check drainage and consider adding a grow light before assuming the plant is sick. Yellow lower leaves on basil after transplanting are normal - it's shedding older growth while it settles.

Leggy, Pale, Stretched Growth

This is a light problem, almost without exception. Move the pot closer to the window or add supplemental lighting. Cutting back the leggy growth won't help if the root cause isn't addressed.

White Powdery Patches on Leaves

Powdery mildew, caused by poor air circulation and high humidity. Improve ventilation first. Remove affected leaves promptly and don't compost them indoors. Fungicidal sprays exist, but always follow the product label, and check that any product is safe for edible plants if you're growing for the kitchen.

Tiny Insects on New Growth

Aphids love basil and mint. Try a strong jet of water in the sink first; that removes a surprising proportion. Insecticidal soap (neem-based or similar) is an effective next step - again, follow the label on edible plants and check re-harvest intervals. If you're propagating new plants from stem cuttings, it's worth reading about leaf and stem propagation techniques - many of the principles around healthy cuttings translate directly to herbs.

Safety note

Several common herbs can be harmful to cats, dogs, or other pets in large quantities - particularly rosemary, sage, and oregano. Keep pots out of reach of animals that might chew the foliage, and contact your vet promptly if you suspect ingestion.

Potting Mix and Container Choice

Herbs hate waterlogged roots. A mix of roughly two parts peat-free multipurpose compost to one part perlite or horticultural grit drains well and keeps roots aerated. Avoid heavy garden soil in indoor pots - it compacts and stays wet far too long.

Container size matters too. Herbs in pots that are too large for their root systems sit in damp compost for too long after watering. Go for a snug fit - about 2-3 cm clearance around the rootball is ideal. If you're potting up multiple herbs together as a kitchen display, ensure each variety has enough soil volume for its roots rather than cramming three plants into a pot designed for one.

Can I bring herbs in from the garden in autumn and expect them to survive indoors?

Yes, with some acclimatisation. Dig them up before the first hard frost, pot them into free-draining compost, and keep them in a cool, shaded spot for a week or two before moving them to a warm indoor position. This reduces transplant shock. Woody herbs like rosemary and thyme do best; leafy annuals like basil rarely transition well from the garden - you're better off buying a fresh nursery pot in autumn.

How long can I keep supermarket herb pots going indoors?

Supermarket pots contain many seedlings crammed together and are designed to be used quickly, not grown on. Divide them into individual pots of 10-12 cm diameter, use fresh free-draining compost, and give them good light. Done this way, parsley and chives can last several months. Basil is the exception - it's usually too stressed by the time it reaches the shelf to reliably rebound, though it's always worth trying.

When should I move herbs back outside in spring?

Wait until nighttime temperatures are consistently above 10 °C and the risk of frost has passed for your area - this varies widely by region and year, so watch local forecasts rather than going by calendar date alone. Harden plants off by moving them outside for a few hours each day over one to two weeks before leaving them out permanently. Plants that have spent months under artificial light can sunburn in direct outdoor sun without this gradual transition.

A winter herb garden on the windowsill doesn't need to be complicated or particularly large to earn its place. Two or three well-chosen plants, a grow light, and a watering discipline that errs toward dry - that's genuinely all it takes to have fresh greenery in the kitchen from October through to March. Start small, get confident, and you'll find yourself expanding the operation by year two. I always do.
