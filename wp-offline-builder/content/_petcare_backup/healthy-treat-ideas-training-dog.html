I keep a small jar of treats by the back door, and for years it held the same thing: those crunchy biscuits the size of a fifty-cent coin. Then I actually counted. Twelve biscuits across one ten-minute training session, every one of them a little brick of calories, and my dog was getting rounder while learning almost nothing new.

The fix wasn't training less. It was training smarter, with treats sized and chosen for the job. A reward you hand out forty times in a session has to be tiny, fast to eat, and exciting enough that your dog keeps offering behavior.

Below is the checklist I wish I'd had: eight treat ideas that stay high-value to your dog and low-impact on the scale.

The 10% rule

Treats - training rewards included - should make up no more than about 10% of your dog's daily calories. The rest comes from a complete, balanced diet. When treats creep higher, trim the meal slightly to match.

1. Pea-sized pieces of cooked chicken

Plain cooked chicken breast is the gold standard of training rewards, and most dogs would happily work for it all day. No salt, no oil, no seasoning - just boiled or baked, then torn into pieces no bigger than a green pea.

The trick is the size. One chicken breast can yield fifty or sixty rewards if you tear carefully, and it's lean enough to fit inside that 10% budget. Skip the skin and any garlic or onion in the cooking water, both of which dogs should never have.

2. Single-ingredient freeze-dried meat

When I need something I can carry in a pocket on a hot day, freeze-dried liver or turkey is my default. It's shelf-stable, crumbles into tiny bits, and tends to be irresistible because the flavor is concentrated.

Read the label and look for one ingredient - just the meat. Many "training treats" hide sugar, glycerin, or fillers that bump the calorie count without adding value your dog cares about.

Pocket trick

Break freeze-dried pieces in half or quarters before your session. Smaller rewards mean more repetitions per calorie, and dogs rarely notice the difference in size when the food is this flavorful.

3. Frozen green beans and peas

For the bigger dog who loves to crunch, plain frozen green beans are a near-zero-calorie win. They're mostly water and fiber, so you can be generous without guilt, and the cold makes them feel like a real treat in summer.

Use plain, unsalted beans - not the canned kind swimming in sodium, and never anything cooked with butter or onion. Peas work the same way and are easy to toss one at a time during loose-leash practice.

These also make a useful "filler" reward. I mix a few low-value veggies into a bag of higher-value meat so the jackpot moments still feel special.

4. Tiny cubes of low-fat cheese

Cheese is the reward I save for hard moments - recall in a distracting park, or the first vet-parking-lot rehearsal. It's high-value precisely because it's rich, which means a little goes a long way.

Cut it into cubes smaller than a pea and reach for lower-fat options like mozzarella. Some dogs are sensitive to dairy, so introduce a single small piece first.

Because cheese is calorie-dense, I treat it as special-occasion currency. If you're rewarding forty times a session, this is not your base treat.

5. Blueberries and banana slivers

Fruit surprises a lot of owners, but plenty of dogs adore blueberries, and they're small enough to serve as-is. They're sweet, low in calories, and easy to keep in a container by the door.

Banana works too, sliced thin and torn into small pieces - though it's sugary, so it stays in the occasional column. Avoid grapes and raisins entirely; they're genuinely dangerous to dogs even in small amounts.

Fruit pairs nicely with the idea of a varied, balanced bowl. If you're curious about how rewards fit into the larger picture, my piece on what a balanced diet actually means for pets walks through where extras like these belong.

6. Their own kibble, repurposed

This is the most underrated trick on the list, and it costs nothing. Measure out part of your dog's regular daily kibble in the morning and use it as your training "currency" through the day.

Because it's already part of their balanced ration, you sidestep the calorie math entirely - you're feeding the same food, just one piece at a time and tied to good behavior. For young dogs and easy reps, kibble is plenty motivating.

When kibble isn't enough

Save kibble for low-distraction settings - your kitchen, basic cues your dog already knows. The harder the environment, the higher-value the reward needs to be. Match the paycheck to the difficulty of the job.

7. A smear of plain pureed pumpkin

Not every reward has to be a bite. For dogs who do better with a lick than a crunch, a thin smear of plain canned pumpkin (not pie filling) on a silicone mat or spoon is calming and low-calorie.

Pumpkin is gentle on the stomach and adds fiber. I lean on it for grooming and nail-trim cooperation, where a slow lick keeps a nervous dog settled.

The same lick-to-relax idea is one I rely on for stressful outings - there's more on that in my notes on preparing your pet for a calm vet visit, where a familiar treat can change the whole mood of the appointment.

8. Homemade single-ingredient jerky

If you like a small project, thin slices of sweet potato or lean meat dried low and slow make a sturdy, additive-free chew you can snap into training-sized bits - and you control exactly what goes in.

Keep it simple - no salt, no seasoning, no oil - and store it in the fridge since there are no preservatives.

Whenever you introduce a brand-new treat, go slowly, the same way you would with a new food. My guide to safely transitioning your pet to a new food applies to treats too: one new thing at a time, small amounts first, and watch how your dog responds.

How to keep treats from adding up

The treats matter less than the system around them. A few habits keep training rewarding without the slow weight creep:

  • Pre-portion the day. Measure your treat budget each morning so you can see when you're running out, instead of guessing.
  • Rotate value. Use kibble and veggies for easy reps; save chicken and cheese for the hard stuff.
  • Shrink the size. Most dogs respond to the act of getting a reward, not its volume. Half a piece is still a win.
  • Adjust meals. On a heavy training day, take a little off dinner to balance the books.
Keep off the no-go list

Never use chocolate, grapes, raisins, onion, garlic, anything with xylitol, or salty human snacks as rewards - these can be toxic to dogs. If your dog eats something dangerous or shows trouble breathing, collapse, or seizures, contact your veterinarian or an emergency clinic right away.

A quick word on weight and health

If your dog is already carrying extra weight, or has a condition like pancreatitis or diabetes, the treat list changes - some owners find lean veggies are the only rewards that fit.

I'm a checklist person, not a vet, so treat this as a starting point rather than a prescription. For weight plans, food allergies, or ongoing health concerns, your own veterinarian knows your dog and should have the final say.

How many treats can I give during a single training session?

There's no fixed number - what matters is the total calories. Aim to keep all treats under roughly 10% of daily calories, then make each reward tiny so you can still do plenty of repetitions. Trimming a little off meals on heavy training days helps you stay in budget.

Are store-bought training treats bad for my dog?

Not necessarily, but read the label. Look for short ingredient lists and skip ones loaded with sugar, glycerin, or fillers. Single-ingredient meat treats and your dog's own kibble are usually lower in calories and easier to portion.

My dog ignores low-value treats outdoors. What should I do?

Match the reward to the difficulty. Distracting environments call for your highest-value currency - chicken, cheese, or freeze-dried meat - while kibble is fine for quiet rooms. If motivation stays low everywhere, check with your vet to rule out anything physical.

Pick two or three of these, pre-portion them tonight, and you'll have a training jar that works for your dog instead of against the scale. Start with the easy options and save the rich rewards for when you really need them. And as always, loop in your veterinarian for anything tied to weight or health - they're your best partner in keeping training both fun and safe.
