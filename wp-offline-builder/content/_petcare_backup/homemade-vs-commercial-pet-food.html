A client of mine once arrived with a binder. Inside were recipes, a spreadsheet of ingredient costs, and a photo of her senior Labrador, Biscuit, looking deeply unimpressed by his kibble. She wanted to cook for him, and she wanted to know if she'd be doing him a favor or quietly shortchanging him.

That question comes up in my exam room almost weekly, and it rarely has a tidy answer. Homemade feeding can be wonderful. It can also go sideways fast if the recipe isn't balanced. Commercial food, for all the eye-rolling it gets, exists because feeding a complete diet by hand is genuinely hard.

So let's weigh both honestly. No tribalism, no scare tactics, just the trade-offs I walk owners through before they commit either way.

What "homemade" and "commercial" actually mean

Homemade food is anything you cook or assemble yourself, from a vet-formulated cooked diet to a raw plan to the "I just add chicken and rice" approach. The defining feature is that you control every ingredient.

Commercial food covers everything you buy ready to serve: dry kibble, canned, fresh-frozen subscription meals, and freeze-dried. The good ones are formulated to meet established nutrient profiles, so a single bag is meant to be a full diet, not a topping.

That distinction matters more than the marketing on the label. A diet is "complete and balanced" when it supplies every nutrient your pet needs in the right ratios, and that's the bar both approaches have to clear.

The case for cooking at home

The biggest draw is control. You know exactly what's in the bowl, which is reassuring for pets with confirmed food allergies or sensitive stomachs that react to one specific protein.

Fresh, gently cooked food is also often more palatable. Picky eaters, dogs recovering from illness, and seniors who've lost interest in dry food frequently perk up for something warm and aromatic. Biscuit certainly did.

And for some owners, cooking is simply an act of care they enjoy. That's valid. The catch is that enthusiasm doesn't equal nutritional completeness, which is where I see the most heartbreak.

The balance problem

Most homemade recipes found online are not nutritionally complete. Studies of internet recipes have repeatedly found gaps in calcium, certain vitamins, and essential fatty acids. A diet that's missing nutrients for months can cause real harm, especially in growing puppies and kittens. Have any homemade plan reviewed by a board-certified veterinary nutritionist.

The case for commercial food

Convenience is the obvious win, but it's not the only one. A reputable commercial diet has been formulated to a defined nutrient standard, and the better brands run feeding trials and quality testing you simply can't replicate at home.

Consistency helps too. Every bag delivers roughly the same nutrition, so you're not riding the daily variability of whatever produce was in your fridge. For multi-pet homes or busy schedules, that reliability is worth a lot.

Cost is usually lower as well, particularly for large dogs. Cooking a balanced diet with the right supplements often runs more than people expect once you tally the muscle meat, organ, calcium source, and oils.

Reading a label like a vet

Look for a statement that the food is "complete and balanced" for your pet's life stage, ideally backed by feeding trials. Match the food to the animal in front of you: puppy, adult, senior, or a specific condition. If you're unsure which life-stage formula fits, that's a great question for your next vet visit.

Mistakes I see owners make with both

The classic homemade error is "balanced over time" thinking, the idea that variety alone averages out. It doesn't. Calcium and phosphorus, in particular, need to be right in most meals, not eventually.

On the commercial side, the most common slip is portioning by eyeball. Free-feeding a bowl that never empties is one of the fastest routes to weight gain. If your pet inhales meals or guards the bowl, a measured portion plus a tool like a slow feeder that breaks up fast eating can help both digestion and pace.

People also forget that "human food is healthy, so it's safe for pets" is a flawed leap. Plenty of wholesome groceries are toxic to animals, and homemade cooks bump into them constantly. Before you start improvising, read up on the foods you should never feed a cat, because onions, garlic, and a few common pantry staples cause serious harm.

Lifestyle shapes calories

How much and what your pet should eat depends partly on activity level. The energy needs of a couch-loving senior and a fence-leaping adventurer differ a lot, and the same logic that drives the indoor versus outdoor cat health conversation applies here: match the calories to the life your pet actually lives.

Which wins, and for whom

For most households, a well-chosen commercial diet is the safer, simpler default, and there's no shame in that. It frees you from the math and lowers the risk of accidental deficiencies.

Homemade earns its place when there's a specific reason: a documented allergy, a finicky senior, a medical condition your vet wants to manage through diet, or an owner who's committed to doing it properly with professional formulation. Done right, with the recipe checked and the supplements correct, it can be excellent.

Many of my clients land in the middle, feeding a quality commercial base and adding small amounts of safe fresh food as toppers. Biscuit ended up there, and he tolerated his kibble far more graciously once it came with a spoon of vet-approved cooked pumpkin.

Quick takeaways

Choose commercial for reliability, lower cost, and built-in balance. Choose vet-formulated homemade for control and palatability when there's a real reason for it. Whatever you pick, portion it carefully, introduce changes gradually, and loop in your veterinarian, especially if your pet has a health condition. Any change in appetite, vomiting, or weight loss deserves a call to your vet rather than a recipe tweak.

Is homemade food healthier than commercial food?

Not automatically. Homemade can be excellent when a veterinary nutritionist formulates it, but unbalanced recipes are common and can cause deficiencies over time. A reputable, tested commercial diet is often the more reliably complete choice.

Can I mix homemade and commercial food in the same bowl?

Often yes, in moderation. Many owners feed a complete commercial base with small amounts of safe fresh toppers. Keep additions under roughly ten percent of daily calories and confirm the foods are pet-safe with your vet.

How do I switch my pet's diet without upsetting their stomach?

Transition gradually over about a week, mixing increasing amounts of the new food into the old. If you see persistent vomiting, diarrhea, or refusal to eat, slow down and contact your veterinarian for guidance.

There's no single right bowl for every pet, and that's genuinely freeing once you stop chasing the "perfect" diet. Pick the approach that fits your pet's needs and your real life, do it carefully, and check in with your own vet for anything health-related. If something ever looks like an emergency, such as collapse, seizures, trouble breathing, or suspected poisoning, skip the recipe debate and head straight to an emergency clinic.
