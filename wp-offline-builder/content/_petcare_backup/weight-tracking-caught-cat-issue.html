The first thing I noticed wasn't the cat. It was the number. A client of mine - I'll call her Renata, with her permission - emailed me a photo of a spiral notebook. Down one column were dates, and beside each, a weight: 5.1 kg, 5.0 kg, 4.9 kg, 4.8 kg. Four months. Three hundred grams gone. Her cat, Biscuit, a nine-year-old orange tabby, was still eating, still purring, still flopping onto the warm laundry. Nothing looked wrong.

That slow downward drift is exactly the kind of thing a yearly checkup can miss. A cat can lose ten percent of its body weight and a casual glance won't catch it, because the change happens a gram at a time. Renata caught it because she'd started doing something deceptively boring: weighing her cat once a week.

I want to walk you through what happened next, because it's one of the clearest examples I've seen of how a humble home habit can buy a pet months of lead time.

The habit that started by accident

Renata didn't set out to be a careful monitor of feline health. She'd bought a kitchen scale for sourdough during a stretch of working from home, and one quiet Sunday she set Biscuit in a cardboard box, put the box on the scale, and subtracted the box's weight out of pure curiosity.

It became a ritual. Sunday coffee, Sunday weigh-in, a number jotted in the notebook. For the first two months the readings barely moved - a few grams up after a big meal, a few grams down the next week. That's normal. Healthy adult cats wobble around a stable baseline.

Then the line started to tilt. Not a cliff, a gentle slope. And because she had the earlier numbers written down, the slope was undeniable.

Why the baseline matters

A single weight tells you almost nothing. A cat at 4.8 kg could be perfectly healthy or quietly declining - you can't know without yesterday's number. The value of home weighing is the trend, not the snapshot.

Why a few hundred grams is a big deal on a cat

Here's the part owners underestimate. Biscuit weighed about 5 kilograms. Three hundred grams is six percent of his body. Scale that to a 70-kilogram adult and you're talking about losing roughly four kilos without trying - the kind of thing that would send a person to the doctor.

On cats, unexplained weight loss is one of the most useful early flags we have. It rides ahead of a long list of conditions: dental disease, hyperthyroidism, kidney changes, diabetes, and others I won't list out to avoid sounding alarmist. The point isn't to diagnose from a scale. It's that a falling line tells you to go looking before symptoms you can actually see show up.

And the inverse matters too. Creeping gain deserves the same attention, which is why I often point owners toward the common feeding habits that quietly pad a pet's waistline - overfeeding hides as easily as illness does when no one is keeping score.

What the vet visit actually found

Renata called me with her notebook in hand, and that changed the entire appointment. Instead of starting from "he seems a little off," we started from data: a steady, four-month decline with normal appetite. That combination narrows the field fast.

We ran a physical exam and bloodwork. Biscuit's thyroid level came back elevated - early hyperthyroidism, caught at a stage where his coat still looked glossy and he hadn't yet developed the ravenous, restless behavior that usually drags owners into the clinic. Because we found it early, we had a calm range of management options and time to choose well, rather than reacting to a cat in obvious distress.

I'm being deliberately careful here: every cat is different, and elevated values mean one thing in Biscuit and could mean something else entirely in your cat. A trend line is a reason to investigate, never a diagnosis. Always have your own veterinarian interpret weight changes and run the appropriate tests. What the scale did was get Renata into my exam room with a question worth asking.

Bring the numbers, not the worry

If you track at home, bring the actual log to your appointment - a notebook, a notes app, a photo of a spreadsheet. "He's lost 300 grams since February" is far more useful to your vet than "he seems thinner lately."

How to weigh a cat at home without a fight

People assume this is harder than it is. You do not need a special pet scale. Here's the method I give owners, and it works for most cats who tolerate a few seconds of stillness.

  1. Weigh yourself alone on a normal bathroom scale and note the number.
  2. Pick up your cat and step back on. Subtract. The difference is your cat.
  3. For more precision on smaller cats, use a kitchen or luggage scale with a carrier or a box, then subtract the empty container's weight.
  4. Weigh at the same time of day, ideally before a meal, so you're comparing like with like.
  5. Write it down the same day. A number you didn't record is a number you'll misremember.

Once a week is plenty for a healthy adult. If you're watching something specific with your vet, they may ask for more often. Don't weigh daily out of anxiety - normal fluctuation will just stress you out and won't tell you anything a weekly line can't.

The trend lines worth reacting to

Not every change is a red flag, and I don't want anyone spiraling over a 30-gram blip. This is the rough framework I share, with the firm caveat that your vet's judgment beats any chart.

Trouble breathing, collapse, seizures, suspected poisoning, or a cat that refuses food entirely for more than a day are emergencies. A weight log is for catching slow problems - it is not a substitute for urgent care when something is clearly wrong.

Why this matters even more for older cats

Biscuit was nine, squarely into the years when small declines stop being random and start being signal. Older cats are masters at hiding illness, and weight is one of the few things they can't disguise from a scale. A weekly weigh-in slots neatly into the broader rhythm of keeping a senior pet comfortable as they age - it's cheap, it's quick, and it gives you a head start that's hard to get any other way.

Weight also pairs well with watching the rest of the body. A drop alongside a dull or flaky coat, for instance, is worth flagging - sometimes those skin changes overlap with the kinds of itching and coat problems that have their own underlying causes. None of these signs lives in isolation, and a home log helps you connect them instead of noticing each one separately and forgetting the last.

What Biscuit's story teaches

A weekly weigh-in cost Renata about ten seconds and a notebook. It surfaced a six-percent decline her eyes couldn't see, turned a vague worry into a concrete data point, and got her cat into care at an early, manageable stage. The scale didn't diagnose anything - it just made sure the right question got asked in time.

Starting your own log this week

You don't need anything fancy. A bathroom scale, a notebook or your phone, and a recurring reminder on the same day each week. Three numbers in, you'll have a baseline; three months in, you'll have a line that can genuinely tell you something.

Biscuit, by the way, is doing well. Renata still weighs him on Sundays, still writes it in the same spiral notebook, and the line has leveled off. She likes to say the sourdough scale earned its keep. I'd agree.

How often should I weigh a healthy adult cat?

Once a week is enough to build a useful trend without obsessing over normal fluctuation. Weigh at the same time of day, ideally before a meal, and write the number down each time. Your vet may ask for more frequent checks if you're monitoring a specific condition.

How much weight loss should make me call the vet?

As a rough guide, a steady, unexplained loss of around five percent of body weight or more is worth a conversation with your veterinarian - especially in a senior cat. Don't try to diagnose the cause yourself; bring your log so your vet can interpret the trend and decide on testing.

Do I need a special pet scale to do this?

No. For most cats you can weigh yourself, then weigh yourself holding the cat, and subtract. A kitchen or luggage scale with a box or carrier works well for smaller cats and gives more precise readings. Consistency matters far more than the equipment.

If you take one thing from Biscuit, let it be this: you don't need to be a vet to catch a problem early - you just need a number, written down, week after week. It's one of the gentlest, lowest-effort forms of love you can give an animal who can't tell you when something feels off. And if a trend ever worries you, your own veterinarian is the right person to make sense of it.
