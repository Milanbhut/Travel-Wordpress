Picture your resident cat on a quiet Tuesday. She has mapped every sunbeam, claimed the back of the couch, and decided which windowsill belongs to the morning and which to the afternoon. To her, the home is not a place. It is a set of agreements she negotiated over months, and she expects them honored.

Then a carrier arrives smelling of a stranger, and every one of those agreements feels suddenly up for renegotiation. From her point of view, this is not a happy surprise. It is an occupation.

The good news is that cats are remarkably willing to share a home, provided we let them do it on cat time. Rushing the meeting is the single most common way the whole thing goes sideways. Here is the slow, scent-first plan I walk owners through, one step at a time.

Step 1: Set up a separate base camp before anyone meets

Before the new cat ever sees your resident cat, give the newcomer a closed room of their own. A spare bedroom or a quiet bathroom works. This room holds a litter box, food, water, a scratching surface, and a couple of hiding spots.

This base camp does two jobs at once. It gives the new arrival a small, manageable world to feel safe in, and it keeps your resident cat from feeling that everything she owns has been handed away overnight.

Tip

Keep both cats' resources separate from day one. If you are still gathering bowls, beds, and litter boxes, our list of essential supplies for a new kitten covers the basics you will want doubled up in a two-cat home.

Step 2: Let them smell each other before they see each other

Scent is a cat's first language, long before sight. So we introduce the cats by smell while a closed door keeps everyone safe.

Take a soft cloth and gently rub one cat's cheeks, then leave that cloth near the other cat's food. Do the same in reverse. You are pairing the stranger's smell with something pleasant, like dinner, so the unfamiliar scent stops meaning threat and starts meaning routine.

Watch the reactions. A cat who sniffs, blinks slowly, and walks off is doing beautifully. A cat who hisses at the cloth is simply telling you to slow down, not that the introduction has failed.

Step 3: Trade rooms so each cat explores the other's space

After a few calm days of scent swapping, start rotating territory. Put your resident cat behind a closed door, then let the newcomer explore the main living area for fifteen or twenty minutes. Later, reverse it.

Nobody meets face to face yet. What is happening is quieter and more important: each cat investigates where the other sleeps, eats, and rubs, building a full scent map of a housemate they have not laid eyes on.

I love this stage because you can almost watch the curiosity build. By the time these two finally see each other, the stranger already smells like home.

Step 4: Feed them on opposite sides of a closed door

Now we connect the new cat directly to the best part of any cat's day, which is food. Set a bowl down for each cat on either side of the closed base-camp door, close enough that they can sense one another but far enough that neither feels cornered.

If both eat calmly, inch the bowls a little closer at the next meal. If anyone stops eating, stiffens, or backs away, slide the bowls apart again and hold there. You are letting them decide the pace, and they will, if you listen.

Why this works

A relaxed cat does not eat near a threat. By keeping both cats comfortable enough to finish a meal in each other's company, you are stacking up small, positive associations long before they share a room.

Step 5: Open the door a crack for short, supervised peeks

When meals at the door are boring and easy, it is time for a first look. Prop the door open just an inch, or use a baby gate or cracked screen door so they can see but not lunge. Keep these first visual sessions short, only a few minutes, and end on a calm note.

Have treats and a wand toy ready to redirect attention. A little staring is normal. A slow tail flick is normal. What you are watching for is escalation, and you want to interrupt it gently before it builds, not after.

When to pause

Flattened ears, a puffed tail, growling, swatting, or a cat who refuses to eat or use the litter box means back up to the previous step for several more days. Pushing through hostility teaches both cats that the other one's presence equals stress, and that lesson is hard to undo.

Step 6: Allow full, supervised time in the same room

Once they can see each other through the gap without drama, open the door fully while you supervise. Keep sessions short at first and gradually stretch them as the cats relax. Scatter a few toys and treats so the shared space feels like a good place to be, not a standoff.

Give each cat an easy exit and a high perch to retreat to. Cats settle disputes far more peacefully when nobody feels trapped, so vertical space and clear escape routes do a lot of quiet work here.

Resist the urge to leave them alone together until you have seen many calm, uneventful visits in a row. Most homes reach that point in a few weeks. Some take a couple of months, and that is still a complete success.

Reading the signals along the way

Throughout this process, your cats are narrating their comfort level constantly. Slow blinks, loose bodies, grooming, and shared napping all say things are going well. Hissing during a tense moment is ordinary feline punctuation and not a verdict.

Hiding, though, deserves a closer look. A newcomer who tucks away for a day or two is simply being a sensible cat. If a resident cat who used to greet you starts vanishing for long stretches, read more on why a cat may suddenly hide more than usual, and watch for changes in appetite or litter-box habits that could signal stress or illness.

Vet note

Behavior changes can have medical roots. If either cat stops eating, hides for more than a day or two, or shows signs of illness, contact your veterinarian. For an emergency such as trouble breathing, collapse, seizures, or suspected poisoning, seek emergency veterinary care right away rather than waiting.

Little things that smooth the whole introduction

A few details make the steps above easier on everyone. Keep play, feeding, and cuddle time generous for your resident cat so the new arrival never coincides with her losing your attention. Add an extra litter box, since the rule of thumb is one per cat plus a spare.

Trim sharp claws ahead of time to keep any scuffle gentler, the same kind of routine grooming care that keeps any pet comfortable. Owners often ask me whether grooming matters as much for cats as it does for dogs, and while a healthy cat handles most of her own coat, the telltale signs a dog's coat needs professional grooming are a useful reminder that mats and overgrown nails deserve attention in every species.

Above all, move at the pace of the more nervous cat. The introduction is finished when both of them act like the other has simply always been there.

How long does it take to introduce two cats?

Most slow introductions take two to six weeks, though some confident pairs gel faster and some sensitive cats need a couple of months. Let the more anxious cat set the pace rather than forcing a deadline.

What if my cats hiss or swat at each other?

A hiss during a tense moment is normal communication. Persistent growling, puffed tails, or swatting means you have moved too fast, so calmly return to the previous step for several more days before trying again.

Should I let them fight it out to establish a hierarchy?

No. Letting cats fight teaches each one that the other equals danger, which is hard to reverse. Interrupt rising tension gently with a toy or treat and step back to an easier stage instead.

Bringing a second cat home is less an event and more a slow conversation in scent, sound, and stolen glances. Give your cats the gift of going at their own speed, and most pairs arrive somewhere comfortable, sharing windowsills they once would have fought over. When in doubt, slow down a notch, and let curiosity do the rest.
