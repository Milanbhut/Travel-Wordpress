A cat who pees on the bath mat is not being spiteful. She is leaving you a note, written in the only language she has, and the note usually says something very practical: this box is too dirty, too crowded, too scary, or in the wrong spot.

I have spent years watching cats negotiate their bathrooms, and the pattern is almost always the same. The accident looks like a behavior problem. It is really a logistics problem.

Here are seven common litter box mistakes, why each one pushes a cat to go elsewhere, and what to change.

1. Hiding the box in the basement or a back corner

We tuck the litter box where we won't see or smell it, which often means a dark basement, a cramped closet, or behind the rumbling water heater. To us it is tidy. To a cat, it is a long, exposed walk to a spot where loud noises happen and exits are limited.

A cat in mid-squat is at her most vulnerable, and instinct tells her to keep escape routes open. A box wedged in a corner with one way out can feel like a trap.

Try this

Place the box somewhere quiet but not isolated, on the floor she already lives on. She should be able to see the room and reach the box without passing a noisy appliance or a closed door.

2. Scooping too rarely (the box is dirtier than you think)

This is the big one. A cat's sense of smell dwarfs ours, so a box that seems "fine" to you on Thursday can read as deeply unpleasant to her by Tuesday. Many cats will hold it, then give up and choose a clean rug instead.

Scooping every single day is the most effective change most owners can make. Dump and wash the whole box with unscented soap every week or two, and skip the heavily perfumed cleaners that mask odor for you but offend her.

If your cat suddenly starts avoiding a box she used happily for years, do not assume it is only the cleaning. A sudden change in bathroom habits can signal a urinary issue or pain, so call your veterinarian to rule out a medical cause before you chase behavior fixes.

3. Owning too few boxes for too many cats

The rule of thumb among feline folks is one box per cat, plus one extra. Two cats means three boxes. It sounds like a lot until you realize cats do not love to share, and a confident cat may quietly guard the only box like a tollbooth.

The shy cat then has nowhere comfortable to go. She waits, gets desperate, and finds the laundry pile. Adding boxes removes the bottleneck and the standoff.

If your cats already bicker over resources, more bathrooms can lower the tension the same way more food bowls and napping spots do. I dug into that whole picture in my notes on getting two cats to finally live in peace, and litter logistics show up there again and again.

4. Spreading boxes too close together

Adding boxes only helps if they are genuinely separate. Two boxes side by side read as one big bathroom to a cat, not two options. A bully can block both with a single seat at the table.

Spread them across different rooms or at least opposite ends of a large space, and across floors in a multi-level home. The goal is that no cat ever has to walk past another cat to reach a toilet.

Why placement beats quantity

Three boxes in a row solve almost nothing. Three boxes in three locations give every cat a private, unguardable route. Think coverage, not just count.

5. Picking the wrong litter (and switching it overnight)

Most cats prefer a soft, fine, unscented clumping litter that feels good under sensitive paws. Strong floral scents, big crystals, or coarse pellets can feel and smell wrong to her, even when the box is spotless.

The second half of this mistake is switching brands suddenly. A cat who walks up to her box, sniffs an unfamiliar texture, and walks away is not being difficult. She is telling you she did not agree to the change.

If you need to switch, mix a little of the new litter into the old over a week or two so the change is gradual. Depth matters too: aim for a couple of inches of litter so she can dig and cover the way instinct demands.

6. Choosing a box that is too small or too covered

Kittens grow, and the starter box rarely keeps up. A cat should be able to turn around fully and dig without her tail or rump hanging over the edge. As a loose guide, the box should be at least one and a half times her body length.

Covered boxes are trickier. We love the lids because they hide the contents, but a hood traps odor inside, right where she has to breathe it, and removes the open sightlines many cats want. For a large cat, a senior with stiff joints, or a nervous one, a tall lip or a narrow door can be a genuine deterrent.

Easy upgrade

Try a large, open storage tote with a low entry cut into one side. It is bigger and easier to climb into than most retail boxes, and it costs around the same as a fancy hooded model.

7. Treating accidents as attitude instead of information

When a cat goes outside the box, the instinct is to assume she is mad, jealous, or acting out of spite. Cats do not do spite. They do discomfort, anxiety, and unmet needs, and an accident is feedback, not a grudge.

Punishing her after the fact does not connect to the behavior in her mind. It only teaches her that you are sometimes scary near the box, which makes her more likely to avoid it. Clean accidents with an enzyme cleaner, never an ammonia-based one, since ammonia smells like urine and invites a repeat.

This is the same gentle, curiosity-first lens I bring to other quirks, like the question of why a cat carries toys around at night. The behavior is rarely random once you ask what she is trying to accomplish.

When to call the vet, not the trainer

Straining to urinate, crying in the box, blood in the urine, or frequent tiny trips with little output can signal a urinary blockage, which is an emergency, especially in male cats. If you see these signs, seek veterinary care right away rather than adjusting the box.

A quick recovery plan when accidents start

If accidents have already begun, reset rather than guess. Scoop daily and deep-clean every box, add one box if you are short, and spread them out across rooms. Switch back to a fine, unscented litter if you changed it recently.

Then watch. Most cats respond within a week or two once the box meets their standards again. If she still refuses a clean, well-placed box, that persistence is itself a reason to involve your veterinarian, since pain and stress can both look like stubbornness.

The same observe-then-adjust habit rescues plenty of pet care headaches, the way our dog-side writers untangle grooming gripes in their roundup of dog bathing mistakes that dry out the coat. Different animal, same lesson: read the signals before you blame the personality.

How many litter boxes do I really need for two cats?

Three. The widely used guideline is one box per cat plus one spare, placed in separate locations so no cat can guard them all. Two boxes side by side count as one option to a cat, not two.

My cat used the box for years and suddenly stopped. What changed?

A sudden change deserves a veterinary check first, because urinary issues and pain often show up as box avoidance. Once health is ruled out, look at recent changes: a new litter, a moved box, a less frequent scooping schedule, or a new pet or person in the home.

Are covered litter boxes bad for cats?

Not always, but they trap odor inside and limit a cat's sightlines, which some cats dislike. If yours avoids a hooded box, try removing the lid or offering a large open box and see which she chooses on her own.

Your cat is not trying to ruin your floors. She is using the only tools she has to tell you the bathroom does not meet her quiet, reasonable standards. Adjust the placement, the cleaning, and the count, and most accidents fade on their own. When they do not, lean on your veterinarian, who can sort the medical from the behavioral and get you both back to peace.
