My own cat, a tabby named Biscuit, hit her senior years before I really noticed. The clue wasn't dramatic. She simply stopped landing the jump onto the bed and started using the ottoman as a halfway step. No yowl, no limp, just a quiet workaround. That is how aging usually shows up in pets: not with a crash, but with small accommodations they make on their own.

Dogs and cats are remarkably good at hiding discomfort. By the time you notice the stiffness or the missed meal, the change has often been brewing for weeks. The reassuring part is that most of what makes a senior pet comfortable is within your control at home, and it does not require a major overhaul.

Here are nine adjustments I recommend most often in practice, with the reasoning behind each one so you can adapt them to your own dog or cat.

1. Trade slick floors for traction

Hardwood and tile feel fine to us, but to an arthritic dog they are an ice rink. When a senior pet's legs splay out and they have to scramble for grip, they brace, tense, and start avoiding those areas entirely. That avoidance quietly shrinks their world.

Lay down runners or washable rugs along the paths your pet uses most: the route to the water bowl, the spot by the door, the landing at the bottom of the stairs. Toe-grip nail covers or simply keeping the fur between the paw pads trimmed can help too.

Try this first

Watch your pet walk across your hardest floor for thirty seconds. If you see paws slipping or a wide, careful stance, traction is the cheapest comfort upgrade you can make today.

2. Rethink the bed (then add a second one)

A thin pad on a cold floor asks a lot of stiff joints. Orthopedic foam that does not fully compress under your pet's weight makes a real difference, especially for larger dogs and for cats who have lost the padding they used to carry.

Place beds away from drafts, since cold air settles low and aging bodies hold heat poorly. I usually suggest two beds in different rooms so your pet never has to choose between resting and being near you.

3. Adjust the food, not just the bowl

Senior pets often need fewer calories but the same or better nutrition, because muscle matters more than ever for supporting tired joints. Many do well on a diet built for their life stage, and some benefit from added moisture if they have started drinking less.

This is also where food myths trip owners up. If you have been weighing a grain-free option, it is worth reading an honest breakdown of how grain-free and grain-inclusive recipes actually compare before you switch a senior pet's diet based on packaging alone.

Ask your vet

Weight, kidney function, and dental health all shape what an older pet should eat. A quick conversation with your veterinarian beats guessing from a label, and any diet change should be made gradually over a week or so.

4. Raise the bowls and bring the water closer

Bending to a floor-level bowl strains a stiff neck and shoulders. Raised feeders bring food up to a kinder height, and for many dogs that single change makes mealtime visibly easier.

Hydration matters even more as pets age. Put extra water stations on each floor of your home so a thirsty senior never has to trek across the house, and notice whether they are drinking more or less than usual.

5. Keep moving, gently

It feels kind to let an old dog rest all day, but stillness stiffens joints and burns away the muscle they rely on. Gentle, regular movement is one of the best things you can offer.

Swap one long walk for two or three short, flat strolls. For cats, a few minutes of slow wand play keeps them limber. Let your pet set the pace, and watch for the line between healthy effort and overdoing it.

Stop and call your vet

Sudden limping, refusing to stand, trouble breathing, collapse, or a seizure are not normal aging. Treat these as emergencies and seek veterinary care right away rather than waiting to see if it passes.

6. Make litter boxes and doors easy again

A senior cat who suddenly misses the box is rarely being spiteful. More often, the high sides hurt to climb over. Switch to a box with a low entry, and add an extra box so an arthritic cat never has far to go.

For dogs, a ramp over a few steps or up to the car saves their joints from repeated impact. These small ramps and step-downs protect dignity as much as bodies, and your pet keeps doing the things they love.

7. Watch the bathroom habits closely

Changes in elimination are some of the earliest signals that something is shifting internally. Straining, accidents, a change in frequency, or anything unusual in the stool deserves attention rather than a shrug.

If you are not sure what counts as normal, this guide to what healthy pet poop looks like and the warning signs to flag is a useful baseline. Bring a fresh sample or a clear photo to your vet when something seems off.

8. Schedule more frequent, calmer checkups

Healthy senior pets generally benefit from veterinary visits twice a year rather than once. Conditions like arthritis, dental disease, and kidney changes are far easier to manage when caught early, and bloodwork can reveal trends long before symptoms do.

Older pets can find the clinic stressful, which makes accurate exams harder. A little preparation goes a long way, and these tips for helping your pet stay calm before a vet visit can turn a dreaded trip into a manageable one.

9. Protect their mind and their routine

Comfort is not only physical. Older pets can become confused, anxious, or disoriented, sometimes pacing at night or seeming briefly lost in a familiar room. A steady routine and a predictable home are deeply reassuring to a foggy brain.

Keep furniture and food bowls in the same places. Offer gentle enrichment like a snuffle mat or an easy food puzzle. Nightlights help pets with fading vision find their way, and your calm presence does more than you might think.

The short version

Traction underfoot, a supportive warm bed, the right food at a kind height, gentle daily movement, accessible bathrooms, twice-yearly checkups, and a steady routine. Small changes, stacked together, add up to a comfortable old age.

How to tell a good day from a bad one

Owners often ask me how to know if their pet is genuinely happy or just coping. I tell them to track a few simple things week to week: appetite, willingness to greet you, interest in a favorite toy or sunny spot, and how easily they settle to sleep.

One stiff morning is not a verdict. A steady drift downward across several of those markers is your cue to check in with your veterinarian. You know your animal better than anyone, and that daily familiarity is genuinely diagnostic.

At what age is my dog or cat considered a senior?

It varies by size and species. Many cats and small dogs are seniors around ten or eleven, while large-breed dogs may qualify closer to seven. Body and bloodwork matter more than the number, so ask your vet where your individual pet stands.

My senior pet sleeps far more than before. Should I worry?

More rest is normal with age, but a sudden, marked increase in sleeping, or sleep paired with reduced appetite, weakness, or disinterest, is worth a veterinary visit. Trust a clear change from your pet's own baseline rather than comparing to other animals.

Are joint supplements worth giving an older pet?

Some pets do benefit, but quality and suitability vary, and supplements are not a substitute for veterinary care. Talk to your vet before starting anything so it fits your pet's overall health and any existing treatment, and avoid dosing on your own.

Aging is not a problem to fix; it is a chapter to make comfortable. Most senior pets have a lot of contented years left when their environment meets them where they are. Watch closely, adjust gently, and lean on your veterinarian as your partner. Biscuit still rules the house from her sunny ottoman, and she is in no hurry to give up the title.
