The door opens, and your dog launches. Front paws on a coat, a little spin, that hopeful tail going like a metronome. From where your dog sits, this is the warmest greeting they know how to give. From where your guest stands, holding a casserole dish, it feels like an ambush.

I find jumping fascinating because it is almost never about dominance or rudeness. It is enthusiasm with nowhere polite to go. Your dog has learned that leaping upward gets a face closer to a face, and that faces are where all the good attention lives. Our job is not to crush that excitement. It is to give it a better address to deliver itself to.

The method below rewards one simple thing: four paws on the floor. Done patiently, it replaces the jump with a greeting your friends will actually thank you for. Most dogs start to shift within a week or two, though older habits take longer to soften.

Step 1: Understand what the jump is really asking for

Before you change a behavior, it helps to read it. A jump is usually a request for contact and attention, delivered with the only tool a dog has reliably found effective. If jumping has ever earned a pat, a laugh, or even a flustered "no, down" while you made eye contact, the dog logged that as a win.

Watch your dog for a day and notice the exact moments the paws leave the ground. Is it the doorbell, the sound of keys, the moment you crouch to take off your shoes? Those triggers are your starting line. You cannot train a polite greeting if you do not know precisely when the impolite one fires.

Reframe it

Your dog is not breaking a rule. They never learned the rule. Jumping is a question, and right now the answer they keep getting is "yes."

Step 2: Make four paws on the floor the thing that pays

Here is the whole secret in one sentence: attention only flows downward. The instant all four paws are on the ground, your dog gets your eyes, your voice, your hands. The moment they lift off, all of that vanishes.

Keep small treats in a jar by the door. When you come home, ignore the jumping completely, no scolding, no eye contact, just a calm turn of your shoulder. The second the paws land, mark it and reward at floor level. Drop the treat between their front feet so the reward itself pulls their nose toward the ground.

That marking moment matters more than people expect. A precise marker, whether a click or a crisp word, tells the dog exactly which split second earned the reward. If you are unsure which to use, this breakdown of how clicker training compares to a verbal marker for speed is worth a read before you start, because consistency in your marker is what makes the floor pay off.

Step 3: Teach a default greeting to replace the leap

Removing a behavior leaves a hole, and dogs hate holes. So give the excitement a job. For most dogs, a sit is the cleanest replacement, because a sitting dog physically cannot jump at the same time.

Practice this away from the doorway first, when nobody is excited. Ask for a sit, reward generously, repeat until it is nearly automatic. Then start asking for that sit a half-second before the usual trigger, as your hand reaches the doorknob, for example. You are pre-loading the calm behavior into the exact moment the jump used to happen.

Trainer's tip

Reward the sit at the dog's eye level, then a second treat tossed to the floor. The first treat marks the choice; the second resets their position so they are grounded and ready to greet the next person.

Step 4: Set the room up so success is the easy choice

You can stack the deck in your favor by managing the environment, especially early on. A dog dragging a light leash during greetings can be gently guided to the floor without anyone grabbing a collar in a tense moment. A baby gate or a comfortable confinement spot gives an over-aroused dog a chance to settle before meeting guests.

This is where good early habits pay dividends. If you are still shaping a young dog's downtime spaces, the differences laid out in this look at choosing a crate or a playpen for a new puppy can help you build a settling zone the dog actually likes, rather than a place they feel banished to.

Management is not cheating. It simply prevents your dog from rehearsing the jump while the new habit is still fragile. Every leap they do not practice is one less repetition you have to undo.

Step 5: Coach your guests before the door opens

Your dog can be perfect, and one delighted visitor squealing "oh I don't mind, I love dogs!" can erase a week of work. People who reward the jump, even affectionately, are teaching your dog that the floor rule is optional.

Brief arrivals quickly and kindly. Ask them to ignore the dog until all four paws are down, then to greet calmly and crouch sideways rather than leaning over the top, which is a body posture that invites a jump. A few seconds of coaching at the threshold protects all your training inside.

Keep greetings safe

Jumping is more than a manners problem with kids, frail relatives, or anyone unsteady on their feet. A medium dog's enthusiastic leap can knock a person down. If your dog's jumping is paired with growling, stiffness, or snapping rather than happy wiggles, that is a different issue, and a certified behavior professional or your veterinarian should help you assess it.

Step 6: Stay consistent and let the habit set

The hardest part is that consistency cannot have an off day. If jumping earns attention even one greeting in ten, your dog learns that the slot machine occasionally pays, and intermittent rewards are famously sticky. Everyone in the household has to play by the same floor rule.

Expect a brief "extinction burst," a stretch where the jumping gets worse before it fades, as your dog tries harder at the thing that used to work. Hold steady. That spike is usually the sign the old habit is on its way out, not a sign the plan is failing.

Most of the stumbles I see come from small inconsistencies rather than bad technique. If progress stalls, it is worth scanning this rundown of common training mistakes that quietly confuse dogs and how to fix them, because the culprit is often a mixed signal nobody noticed they were sending.

The four-paws method in brief

Read the trigger, reward only grounded paws, install a sit as the default greeting, manage the environment so jumping cannot rehearse, coach your guests, and keep every household member perfectly consistent. The leap fades when the floor becomes the thing that pays.

What to do when nothing seems to work

If you have been steady for several weeks and the jumping is unchanged or escalating, do not just push harder. Sometimes a dog is so over-aroused that they genuinely cannot think, and the answer is lowering the excitement before greetings, more exercise earlier in the day, or shorter, calmer arrivals.

And occasionally a sudden change in behavior has a physical root. A dog who becomes frantic, clingy, or unusually reactive when they were previously settled deserves a veterinary check to rule out pain or discomfort. Behavior and health are tangled together more often than people realize, so it is never wasted effort to ask your own vet when something shifts without explanation.

Should I knee my dog or use a spray to stop the jumping?

I would steer away from both. Kneeing can hurt a dog and frighten them, and aversive sprays add stress without teaching what to do instead. Rewarding four paws on the floor and a calm sit gives the dog a clear, kind alternative that actually lasts.

How long until my dog stops jumping on people?

Many dogs show real improvement within one to two weeks of consistent practice, but a long-rehearsed habit can take a couple of months to fully settle. The single biggest factor is consistency across everyone the dog greets.

My puppy jumps because they are tiny and cute. Is it really a problem?

It feels harmless now, but the same dog at full size, leaping at a child or an elderly guest, is a different story. Teaching the floor rule while they are small means you never have to retrain a big, enthusiastic adult later.

Be patient with the dog in front of you, because that joyful leap is affection that simply lost its way. Give the excitement a polite address, reward the floor every single time, and keep your whole household telling the same gentle story. Before long, the door opens, four paws stay down, and your guest gets the warm welcome your dog wanted to give all along.
