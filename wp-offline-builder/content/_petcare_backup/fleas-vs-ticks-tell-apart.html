A client once brought in her terrier convinced he had a single, stubborn flea that "wouldn't jump off." It was a tick, fully attached behind his ear, and the distinction mattered a great deal for what we did next. Owners mix these two up all the time, and I understand why: both are small, dark, and unwelcome.

But fleas and ticks are not interchangeable nuisances. They look different up close, they carry different risks, and the safe way to remove one is the wrong way to handle the other. Getting the identification right in the first thirty seconds shapes everything that follows.

Let me walk you through how I tell them apart in the exam room, and how you can do the same at home before you reach for tweezers or a comb.

Meet the two parasites

A flea is a tiny, wingless insect, usually around 1 to 3 millimeters, with a body that looks flattened side to side. It does not fly, but it jumps remarkably well, which is why you so often see one vanish before you can pin it down. Fleas live their adult lives moving through the coat, feeding and laying eggs as they go.

A tick is not an insect at all. It is an arachnid, a cousin of spiders and mites, with eight legs once it reaches maturity. Ticks do not jump or run across the coat. They climb on, find a spot of skin, bite down, and stay attached for days while they feed, swelling as they fill.

That single behavioral difference, restless movement versus stubborn stillness, is often the fastest clue you have.

Spotting the difference at a glance

When I part the fur and look, I am checking a few things at once: is it moving, how many legs can I count, and is it stuck in place or roaming free. Fleas scatter when the coat opens. A tick sits there, embedded, sometimes with its rear end visible like a small grey or brown seed swelling against the skin.

Color helps too. Fleas are reddish-brown and shiny. Unfed ticks are flat and can be brown, black, or tan; once engorged with blood they balloon into a pale grey or greenish bead that owners frequently mistake for a skin tag or wart.

The risks behind each bite

Fleas are mostly a numbers problem. One or two are an annoyance, but they reproduce fast, and a heavy infestation can trigger flea allergy dermatitis, where a single bite sets off intense, raw itching. Fleas can also pass tapeworm if your pet swallows one while grooming, and in very young or small animals a large flea burden can cause anemia.

Ticks are more of a quality problem than a quantity one. A single tick attached long enough can transmit diseases like Lyme, anaplasmosis, or ehrlichiosis, depending on where you live. Most ticks need to stay attached for many hours before disease transmission becomes likely, which is exactly why prompt, correct removal matters so much.

Why time matters with ticks

The longer a tick stays attached, the higher the chance of disease transmission. Finding and removing one early is one of the most protective things you can do, so a quick daily check after walks in tall grass or wooded areas is worth the minute it takes.

How to remove a flea safely

You rarely remove fleas one at a time, and you should not try to. Catching a single flea between your fingers does almost nothing about the eggs and larvae already in the environment. Instead, the goal is to treat the animal and the home together.

Start with a fine-toothed flea comb, working through the coat and dipping the comb in a bowl of soapy water to drown what you catch. This confirms the diagnosis and gives some immediate relief. Then talk to your veterinarian about a proper preventive product suited to your pet's species, age, and weight.

Never share products between species

Some dog flea products are dangerous, even fatal, to cats. Do not assume a product is interchangeable, and do not estimate doses yourself. Your vet will match the right product to the right animal.

Wash bedding in hot water and vacuum thoroughly, because most of the flea life cycle is living in your carpet and soft furnishings, not on your pet. While you have your pet still and cooperative, it is a good moment to glance at the gums and teeth too; problems hide there, and I cover several common ones in our piece on the dental care mistakes that quietly harm teeth.

How to remove a tick safely

Tick removal is a different skill entirely, and the folk methods, burning it, smothering it in petroleum jelly, twisting it off, tend to make things worse. Irritating an attached tick can cause it to regurgitate into the bite, which is the opposite of what you want.

Use fine-tipped tweezers or a dedicated tick-removal tool. Grasp the tick as close to the skin as you can, then pull straight upward with steady, even pressure. Do not jerk or twist. Once it is out, clean the area, wash your hands, and watch the spot over the coming days.

Save the tick

Drop the removed tick into a small sealed bag or container. If your pet develops symptoms later, identifying the tick species can help your veterinarian a great deal.

Call your vet if the head stays embedded, if you cannot remove the whole tick, or if you are simply not confident. There is no shame in having it done properly, and it takes us moments.

Which approach wins for your situation

If your pet is scratching constantly, you are seeing tiny dark specks in the fur, and nothing seems to stay put when you look, you are almost certainly dealing with fleas, and your answer is a comprehensive plan covering the pet and the home, not a single bug hunt. Quantity is the enemy here.

If you have found one firm, stationary lump after time outdoors, especially around the head, ears, or paws, treat it as a tick and remove it correctly and promptly. Here the clock, and your technique, are what protect your pet.

Both situations reward the same underlying habit: knowing your pet's normal body well enough to notice when something changes. Owners who run their hands over their animal regularly catch things early, the same way the family in our story about how routine weight tracking surfaced their cat's hidden problem caught an issue long before it became serious.

Keeping both at bay year-round

Prevention beats removal every time, for fleas and ticks alike. A year-round preventive recommended by your vet does the heavy lifting, and it sidesteps the guesswork of choosing products off a shelf. Consistency is what makes it work; gaps in coverage are where infestations restart.

Support that with good general health. A well-nourished pet with an intact, healthy coat is simply better equipped, and good nutrition is part of that foundation, including knowing which treats are reasonable, like the options in our guide to human foods that are safe for dogs in sensible amounts.

Check your pet after every outdoor adventure in warm months, keep grass trimmed where you can, and never wait out a suspected infestation hoping it resolves on its own. It rarely does.

Can my pet have both fleas and ticks at the same time?

Yes, and it happens more than people expect, especially in warm months. If you find one parasite, keep checking, because finding a tick does not rule out fleas hiding elsewhere in the coat, and vice versa.

Do fleas and ticks bite humans too?

They can. Fleas will bite people, usually around the ankles, and ticks attach to humans readily. This is another reason to treat the home and check yourself after the same outdoor outings, not just your pet.

When should I call the vet instead of handling it myself?

Call if you cannot fully remove a tick, if the bite area becomes swollen, red, or oozing, or if your pet seems lethargic, feverish, or off their food afterward. Any sign of collapse, seizures, or trouble breathing is an emergency, so seek immediate veterinary care.

Telling fleas from ticks is a small skill with an outsized payoff. Once you can spot which one you're looking at, the right response becomes obvious, and your pet spends far less time uncomfortable. When you're unsure about identification, a product, or a reaction, your own veterinarian knows your pet's history and is always the best person to ask.
