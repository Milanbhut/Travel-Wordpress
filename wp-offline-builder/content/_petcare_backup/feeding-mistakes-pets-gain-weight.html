A few weeks ago, a client set her tabby on my exam table and said, "He barely eats anything." Then she described his day: a scoop in the morning, a topped-off bowl by the window, three "tiny" treats while she made coffee, and the licked-clean plate after dinner. That cat was eating plenty. He just wasn't eating on purpose.

This is how most pets gain weight. Not from one big overindulgence, but from small, well-meaning habits that add up faster than the scale shows. The reassuring part is that almost every one of these mistakes has a clean, kind fix, and your pet won't resent you for it.

Here are the seven feeding errors I see most, why each one matters more than it looks, and exactly how to correct it.

1. Leaving the bowl full all day

Free-feeding feels generous. The bowl is always there, your pet "self-regulates," and nobody goes hungry. In practice, very few dogs and cats are good at portioning themselves, especially the relaxed, food-motivated ones who would happily graze out of boredom.

Why it matters: When food is always available, you lose your single best monitoring tool. You can't tell if your pet skipped a meal (an early sign something's wrong) or quietly ate double. Calories blur, and the slow climb begins.

The fix: Switch to measured meals at set times, usually twice a day for adults. Put the food down, give 15 to 20 minutes, then lift the bowl. Most pets adjust within a few days once they learn food comes on a schedule.

2. Eyeballing the portion instead of measuring it

The "scoop" is the silent saboteur. A coffee mug, a yogurt container, the same cup that came with a different bag years ago - these vary enormously, and a generous hand can add 20 to 30 percent without anyone noticing.

Try this

Use a kitchen gram scale, not a measuring cup. Weighing food is the single most accurate change most owners can make, and it takes about ten seconds once it's a habit.

The fix: Start from the feeding guide on the bag, then treat it as a ceiling rather than a target - those charts often run high. Weigh the daily amount, split it across meals, and adjust based on your pet's body condition over the following weeks.

3. Letting treats creep up on you

Treats are where good intentions quietly betray us. A few training rewards, a dental chew, a crust of toast, a lick of peanut butter to hide a pill - individually trivial, collectively a second dinner.

Why it matters: Treats should stay under roughly 10 percent of daily calories. Past that, you're diluting a balanced diet with extras and adding fuel your pet didn't burn.

The fix: Pick a small daily treat allowance and stick to it. Break treats into smaller pieces; pets count the event, not the gram. For training, peel kibble from the daily ration so rewards aren't extra calories at all.

4. Trusting the label more than your pet

Feeding charts are starting points based on averages, not your specific neutered, indoor, lower-energy companion. Two dogs of identical weight can have very different calorie needs depending on activity, age, and metabolism.

Why it matters: Following the bag to the letter while ignoring the actual animal in front of you is how a "correct" portion still produces a chunky pet. The food doesn't know your dog naps all afternoon.

The fix: Learn to body-condition score at home. Run your hands over the ribs - you should feel them under a thin layer, like the back of your hand, without pressing. Look for a tucked waist from above and from the side. Let what you feel guide portions, and check in with your vet to confirm a healthy target.

5. Picking food by the front of the bag

Marketing on the front panel sells feelings, not nutrition. "Grain-free," "natural," and a photo of a wolf tell you very little about calorie density or whether the diet suits your pet. Some richer formulas pack noticeably more calories per cup, so the same scoop quietly delivers more.

If you're weighing options, it's worth understanding the real considerations in the grain-free versus grain-inclusive food debate rather than choosing on vibes. The right pick is about your individual pet, not a buzzword.

A note on diet changes

Any food switch should be gradual, over about a week, and ideally discussed with your veterinarian - especially if your pet has a health condition. Sudden changes can upset digestion and muddy whether the food is actually helping.

6. Skipping the scale until clothes-shopping season

Weight gain hides in plain sight because you see your pet every day. Half a pound on a ten-pound cat is the same as roughly seven pounds on a person - significant, and easy to miss until it's well underway.

Why it matters: Trends beat snapshots. A single number means little; a number creeping up month over month is a story you want to catch early. Tracking also flags loss, which can be the first hint of illness.

The fix: Weigh monthly and write it down. For small pets, weigh yourself, then yourself holding them, and subtract. This habit pays off in ways that go beyond waistlines - one reader's routine is a good example of how early weight tracking caught a cat's health issue before it became serious.

7. Crash-dieting once you notice

The panic correction is its own mistake. You realize your dog is heavy, slash the food in half, and expect quick results. This backfires, and in cats it can be genuinely dangerous.

Important for cats

Cats forced into rapid weight loss can develop hepatic lipidosis, a serious liver condition. Never crash-diet a cat. Any feline weight-loss plan should be slow and overseen by your veterinarian.

The fix: Aim for gradual loss - generally a small percentage of body weight per week - using measured portions and a vet-guided plan. Building a feeding routine that sticks

You don't have to fix all seven at once. Start with two: measure the food and set meal times. Those alone solve most of the quiet calorie creep I see in the clinic.

From there, fold in a treat budget, a monthly weigh-in, and an honest hands-on check of your pet's ribs and waist. Small, boring consistency beats dramatic overhauls every time, and it's far gentler on your pet.

If your pet has stalled despite your best efforts, or is gaining without an obvious cause, that's a conversation for your veterinarian - sometimes a thyroid issue or other condition is driving the change, not the dinner bowl.

How fast should my overweight pet lose weight?

Slow is safe. Most pets do best losing a small fraction of body weight each week, not crash dieting. Cats in particular need a gradual, vet-supervised plan because rapid loss can trigger serious liver problems.

Are treats really a big deal if they're small?

They add up faster than you'd think. Keep treats under roughly 10 percent of daily calories, break them into smaller pieces, and consider using part of the regular kibble ration for training rewards so they aren't extra calories.

My pet acts starving on a measured portion. Am I underfeeding?

Begging often reflects habit and food motivation, not true hunger. Spread meals out, add a low-calorie food puzzle, and judge by body condition rather than the pleading eyes. If you're unsure or your pet seems genuinely unwell, check with your veterinarian.

Weight gain rarely arrives in one dramatic moment, so the cure isn't dramatic either. Measure, schedule, budget the treats, and keep an eye on the scale, and you'll catch the creep long before it becomes a problem. And when something feels off beyond feeding, lean on your own vet - that's exactly what they're there for.
