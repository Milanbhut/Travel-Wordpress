Most evenings, my own dog stations himself beside my chair with a look that suggests he hasn't eaten in weeks. That hopeful stare is exactly why so many owners ask me, almost guiltily, "Is it okay if I give him a bite of what I'm eating?"

The honest answer is: sometimes yes, sometimes absolutely not. Plenty of everyday human foods are perfectly fine for dogs in small amounts. The trick is knowing which ones, and keeping portions sane.

Here are ten foods I'm comfortable recommending, with the "why" behind each and a sense of how much is reasonable. Treats should stay under roughly 10% of your dog's daily calories, so think bites, not bowls.

1. Plain Cooked Chicken

Lean, gentle on the stomach, and adored by nearly every dog I've met. Plain cooked chicken breast, with no skin, bones, butter, or seasoning, is one of the safest proteins you can hand over.

I often suggest it for dogs recovering from a mild upset tummy. Skip the garlic and onion powder that coats most roast chicken, though; those are genuinely harmful to dogs.

2. Carrots, Raw or Cooked

Carrots are my favorite "guilt-free" treat. They're low in calories, crunchy enough to satisfy a chewer, and the cold of a refrigerated carrot stick can soothe a teething puppy's gums.

Portion tip

For a medium dog, a few baby carrots or one chopped large carrot across the day is plenty. Cut them into coins for small dogs and flat-faced breeds to lower the choking risk.

3. Plain Pumpkin (Not Pie Filling)

Plain canned pumpkin is the food I reach for when an owner calls about mild constipation or loose stools. The fiber helps firm things up, and most dogs treat it like dessert.

Read the label carefully. You want 100% pumpkin, not the spiced pie filling, which is loaded with sugar and sometimes xylitol. A spoonful stirred into dinner is usually enough; if the digestive trouble lingers more than a day or two, that's a conversation for your vet.

4. Blueberries

Tiny, soft, and packed with antioxidants, blueberries make a brilliant training reward. I like them because they're easy to dole out one at a time, which keeps the calorie math honest.

Fresh or frozen both work, and frozen ones double as a summer cool-down treat. A small handful suits most dogs.

5. Green Beans

Plain green beans, steamed or raw, are a vet's secret weapon for the dog who's always begging. They add bulk and crunch with very few calories, which is why I often recommend them to owners working through a weight problem.

If your dog has been creeping up the scale, swapping half their usual biscuits for green beans is a small, sustainable change. I dig into more of these habits in this rundown of common feeding mistakes that quietly pack on the pounds, because portion creep is sneakier than most people expect.

6. Apple Slices (No Core, No Seeds)

Apples bring a satisfying crunch and a dose of fiber and vitamin C. Most dogs love the slightly sweet flavor, and the texture is great for a dog who likes to "work" at a treat.

Skip the core

Apple seeds contain a compound that releases cyanide, and the core is a choking and obstruction hazard. Slice the flesh, toss the core, and you're set.

7. Plain Yogurt, in Small Amounts

A spoonful of plain, unsweetened yogurt can be a nice treat for dogs who tolerate dairy, and the live cultures may be gentle on digestion. Choose one with no added sugar and, crucially, no xylitol.

Some dogs are lactose intolerant and will tell you so with gas or loose stools, so start with a tiny amount. If your dog seems oddly ravenous all the time, that's worth investigating rather than simply feeding more; appetite changes can be a clue, something I touch on when explaining why a pet can act perpetually hungry even when well fed.

8. Cooked Sweet Potato

Sweet potato is a wholesome, fiber-rich carbohydrate that's easy to digest. Bake or boil it plain, with no butter, salt, or marshmallow topping, and offer it mashed or in small cubes.

It's filling, so a couple of cubes goes a long way. I've known fussy senior dogs who'll accept their joint supplement only when it's tucked inside a bit of warm sweet potato.

9. Plain Peanut Butter (Xylitol-Free)

Peanut butter is the classic stuffing for a hollow chew toy, and for good reason: dogs adore it and it keeps them busy. The single most important rule is to check the label for xylitol, a sweetener that's deadly to dogs.

Read every label

Xylitol (sometimes listed as "birch sugar") shows up in some peanut butters, sugar-free gum, and baked goods. Even small amounts can cause a dangerous drop in blood sugar. If you ever suspect your dog ate a xylitol product, treat it as an emergency and call your vet or an animal poison line right away.

Stick to plain, unsalted, no-sugar-added varieties, and keep it to a thin smear rather than the spoonful your dog is lobbying for.

10. Watermelon (Seedless, No Rind)

On a hot afternoon, few things beat a cube of cold watermelon. It's mostly water, so it's hydrating, and the sweetness feels like a real treat without much sugar by volume.

Remove the seeds and never let your dog gnaw the rind, which can cause a blockage. A few small, bite-sized pieces are perfect.

How to Introduce Any New Food Sensibly

Whenever you add something new, offer a small amount first and watch for the next day or so. Mild gas or a single soft stool isn't unusual; repeated vomiting, diarrhea, or facial swelling means stop and call your vet.

Dogs with conditions like pancreatitis, diabetes, kidney disease, or food allergies play by different rules, so clear new treats with your own veterinarian first. And remember that a treat is a moment of connection, not a meal.

One more note from the exam room: many owners assume anything healthy for humans is automatically healthy for dogs. It isn't. Grapes, raisins, onions, garlic, chocolate, and macadamia nuts are all toxic. That well-meaning instinct trips people up elsewhere too, like confidently telling apart two pests that need different responses, which I cover in my guide on spotting fleas versus ticks and reacting correctly.

Can I give my dog these foods every day?

In small amounts, yes, as long as all treats together stay under about 10% of your dog's daily calories. Rotate them rather than piling on, and keep meals as the main source of balanced nutrition.

My dog grabbed something off this list and now seems unwell. What should I do?

Mild, brief tummy upset from a new food often settles on its own. But if you see repeated vomiting, diarrhea, weakness, facial swelling, trouble breathing, or any sign of distress, contact your vet or an emergency clinic straight away.

Are there human foods I should never share?

Yes. Grapes, raisins, onions, garlic, chocolate, macadamia nuts, and anything containing xylitol are toxic to dogs and should be kept well out of reach. When in doubt about a specific food, ask your veterinarian before offering it.

Sharing a snack with your dog can be one of the small joys of the day, and you can usually do it safely with a little label-reading and a steady hand on portions. Keep toxic foods firmly off the menu, introduce anything new slowly, and lean on your own vet whenever your dog has a health condition. Your begging companion will be thrilled either way.
